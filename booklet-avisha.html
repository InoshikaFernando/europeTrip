🇨🇳 China · Forbidden City · history

Forbidden City

Beijing · 600 years at the heart of an empire 🏯

More than 600 years ago, China was ruled by the Yongle Emperor, one of the most powerful emperors in Chinese history. However, he was not originally supposed to become emperor. His father, the Hongwu Emperor, had chosen his grandson to inherit the throne. When the young emperor took power, Yongle disagreed with some of his decisions and began a civil war known as the Jingnan Campaign. After several years of fighting, Yongle's army captured the capital, and he became emperor in 1402.

To show his power and create a grand center for his empire, Yongle decided to move the capital from Nanjing to Beijing. He ordered the construction of an enormous palace unlike anything the world had seen before. Work began in 1406, and it is believed that more than a million workers — builders, craftsmen, artists, and laborers — helped create it. Huge stones were dragged from distant mountains, and valuable wood was transported from forests hundreds of kilometers away. After 14 years of hard work, the palace was completed in 1420. This magnificent complex became known as the Forbidden City.

"The Son of Heaven shall dwell at the center of the world."
— the Forbidden City was built as the symbolic heart of the universe

The Forbidden City was designed to impress everyone who saw it. Surrounded by a moat and high red walls, it contained hundreds of buildings and thousands of rooms. The emperor lived there with his family, servants, guards, and advisors. Ordinary people were not allowed to enter without permission — which is why it became known as the "Forbidden City." The emperor was considered the "Son of Heaven," and many believed he had a special connection to the gods. Important ceremonies, celebrations, and government meetings all took place inside.

For nearly 500 years, the Forbidden City remained the center of Chinese power. Twenty-four emperors from the Ming and Qing dynasties ruled from there. During this time, China experienced periods of great wealth, scientific discoveries, trade, and cultural achievements — but also wars, rebellions, and challenges from foreign powers. Over time, the Qing Dynasty became weaker, and many people wanted change.

In 1912, the last emperor of China, Puyi, was forced to give up the throne when China became a republic. Puyi was only six years old at the time. Although he was allowed to continue living in part of the Forbidden City for several years, he eventually had to leave. The age of emperors had come to an end.

Today, the Forbidden City is known as the Palace Museum and is one of the most famous historical sites in the world. Millions of visitors walk through its gates every year, exploring the halls where emperors once ruled. As they wander through its courtyards and gardens, they are stepping into a place that witnessed centuries of Chinese history — from powerful emperors and royal ceremonies to the end of an empire and the beginning of modern China.

🇦🇹 Austria · Salzburg · history

Salzburg

The "Salt Castle" — Mozart's birthplace and Sound of Music country 🎶

A long time ago, in the beautiful mountain region of Austria, a city called Salzburg began to grow along the Salzach River. The city's name means "Salt Castle," because salt was very important in this area. Salt was like "white gold" in the past because it helped preserve food before fridges existed.

Because of the salt trade, Salzburg became rich and powerful. It was ruled by prince-archbishops — religious leaders who were also like kings. They built strong fortresses to protect the city, especially the famous Hohensalzburg Fortress, which sits high on a hill and still overlooks the city today.

"The hills are alive — with salt money, Mozart sonatas, and 1,000 years of prince-archbishops."
— Salzburg's wealth came from "white gold" (salt), its fame from Mozart, and its modern reach from a 1965 Hollywood musical

Salzburg became very wealthy, and the rulers built beautiful churches, palaces, and gardens like Mirabell Palace. The city also became famous for its music and culture.

One of the most famous people ever born in Salzburg was Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, a musical genius who started composing music when he was just a child. His music later became famous all over the world and is still played today.

Over time, Salzburg became part of larger European empires, including the Habsburg Monarchy, and later became part of modern Austria. Even though it lost its political power, it remained a cultural treasure.

During the 1900s, Salzburg was affected by World War II, but many of its historic buildings survived. After the war, the city was carefully restored.

Today, Salzburg is famous for its stunning old town, mountain views, and music festivals. It is also known as the filming location for The Sound of Music, which shows its beautiful hills and landscapes.

🇦🇹 Austria · Hallstatt · history

Hallstatt

7,000 years of salt, mountains & history 🏔️

Long before Austria had emperors, palaces, and famous cities like Vienna, there was a small lakeside village called Hallstatt, deep in the mountains. What makes Hallstatt special is that people have lived there for more than 7,000 years, even in the Stone Age. But its real importance began around 800 BC, during a time called the Hallstatt Culture — one of the earliest known Iron Age cultures in Europe.

The people of Hallstatt became famous because of something hidden under their mountains: salt. Salt was extremely valuable in ancient times — used to preserve food before refrigerators existed. The salt mines in Hallstatt were so rich that the village became one of the most important trading centers in early Europe. People traded salt for tools, animals, and luxury items from faraway places.

"White gold of the Alps."
— salt mined from Hallstatt's mountains, more precious than coins in the ancient world

As the miners dug deeper into the mountains, they discovered ancient tools, weapons, and tombs. These finds helped archaeologists understand what life was like in Europe over 2,000 years ago. Because of this, historians named an entire early European civilization the "Hallstatt Culture" — after this very village.

Over time, other powerful cities like Vienna became the political center of Austria, and Hallstatt became quieter. But it never lost its beauty or importance. The village sits between a lake and steep mountains, almost like a postcard. In fact, it is now considered one of the most beautiful villages in the world.

Today, Hallstatt is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, protected because of its cultural and historical importance. Tourists visit not only for its views, but also because it represents one of the oldest chapters of European history.

🇨🇿 Czech Republic · Prague · history

Prague

A castle, a bridge, and a velvet revolution 🏰

A very long time ago, on the banks of the Vltava River, there were small settlements where early Slavic tribes lived. Over time, one of these settlements grew on a hill above the river, where a strong fortress was built. This fortress later became Prague Castle — one of the oldest and largest castle complexes in the world. It started around the 9th century, when early rulers wanted to protect their land and control the river crossing.

As centuries passed, Prague became more important. It was ruled by powerful kings, especially during the time of the Holy Roman Empire. One of the most famous rulers was Charles IV, who made Prague the capital of the empire in the 1300s. He built bridges, universities, and beautiful buildings. The famous Charles Bridge was named after him and still connects the two sides of the city today.

"The city of a hundred spires."
— a nickname Prague earned in the 19th century for its Gothic skyline

During this golden age, Prague became a center of learning, art, and trade. But like many European cities, it also faced wars and struggles. Religious conflicts, invasions, and political changes often affected the city. Even so, Prague survived and kept rebuilding itself each time.

In the 1900s, Prague became part of Czechoslovakia after World War I. It went through difficult times again during World War II, when it was occupied by Nazi Germany. After the war, it came under communist rule during the Cold War period. Life was controlled and restricted, but people continued to preserve their culture and identity.

A major change came in 1989 during the peaceful Velvet Revolution, when people protested and ended communist rule. Soon after, democracy returned. In 1993, Czechoslovakia peacefully split, and Prague became the capital of the new country — the Czech Republic.

Naples & Pompeii

The bay, the volcano, and the city frozen in time 🌋

A long time ago in southern Italy, a beautiful coastal city called Naples began its life as a Greek settlement named Neapolis, meaning "New City." Because it sat right by the sea, it quickly became an important trading port. Ships came from all over the ancient world, bringing goods, stories, and cultures. Later, the Romans took control, and Naples became a peaceful place where wealthy Romans would come to enjoy the warm weather and the sea.

Not far away from Naples, about 25 km inland, was another Roman city called Pompeii. Pompeii was very different from Naples — it was not a port city, but a busy inland town full of markets, homes, bakeries, and grand villas. Life there was comfortable, and people enjoyed Roman baths, theatres, and busy streets. Both Naples and Pompeii were part of the same Roman world.

"A city frozen in time, exactly as it was on one summer afternoon in 79 AD."
— Pompeii was buried so quickly that bread is still in the ovens

Watching over both cities was a powerful volcano called Mount Vesuvius. At the time, people did not realise how dangerous it could be. They saw it as just another mountain. But in 79 AD, everything changed suddenly. Mount Vesuvius erupted violently, sending ash, rocks, and poisonous gas into the sky. Pompeii was completely buried under thick layers of ash, freezing the city in time. People, houses, food, and even paintings were preserved exactly as they were that day.

Naples, however, was not destroyed. Because it was closer to the coast and further from the main eruption path, it survived. Over time, it continued to grow, was ruled by many different kingdoms, and eventually became one of Italy's most important cities. Pompeii, on the other hand, disappeared from memory for many centuries until it was rediscovered by archaeologists nearly 1,700 years later.

Today, Naples is a living, modern city full of energy, music, and famous food like pizza 🍕, while Pompeii is an open-air museum where you can walk through ancient Roman streets. Mount Vesuvius still stands between them — quiet but not extinct — reminding everyone of the powerful event that shaped their shared history.

🇨🇳 China · The Great Wall · history

The Great Wall

2,200 years of stone, soldiers, and signal fires across the mountains 🧱

A long time ago in China, there were many small kingdoms fighting with each other. Life was often unsafe, especially in the northern border areas where horse-riding invaders could come quickly from the grasslands.

Around 220 BC, a powerful ruler named Qin Shi Huang united all these kingdoms into one big country for the first time. He wanted to protect his new empire, so he ordered walls to be connected and extended across the north. This was the beginning of the Great Wall.

"21,196 kilometres long — if you walked it nonstop, it would take you over a year."
— the Great Wall is not one wall but many · built, rebuilt, and added to by 9 different dynasties across 2,200 years

But the wall we see today was not built all at once. Over many centuries, different dynasties repaired and rebuilt it. The most famous version was built during the Ming Dynasty (around 1368–1644). The Ming rulers made it stronger using bricks and stone, and they added watchtowers where soldiers could watch for enemies and send signals.

The Great Wall is not just one straight wall — it is a huge network of walls, hills, and fortresses that stretches across mountains, deserts, and valleys. Soldiers lived there, guarding the border and lighting fires or sending smoke signals to warn others if danger came.

Over time, the wall stopped being used for defense because China became more stable and modern armies changed how wars were fought. But the wall remained as a symbol of protection, strength, and unity.

Today, the Great Wall is one of the most famous landmarks in the world and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Millions of people visit it every year to walk on its ancient stones and see how far it stretches across the mountains.
