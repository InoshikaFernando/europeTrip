Who we are,
why we travel.

We're the Munasinghe-Fernandos — two parents and three children who love to see the world together. My husband and I share one dream: to travel to as many countries as we possibly can, as a family.

It's never easy — booking flights, trains and rooms for five is its own small adventure, and we know we can't reach everywhere. But from every country we do get to, we try our very best to see as much as we can. This magazine is where we keep all of it.

Austria · Who We Are03

You can drive the width of Austria in about three hours. In those three hours you pass from a lake the whole world queues to photograph, through the birthplace of Mozart, to a capital that ran an empire for six centuries. Few countries pack so much into so little.

We came, honestly, for a film. Salzburg was where The Sound of Music was shot, and for one of us that was reason enough. What we found around it was a country of salt and snowmelt, of prince-archbishops and Habsburg emperors, of cream cakes on porcelain and buskers playing Bach on street corners — and, closed on the very day we arrived, one very famous salt mine we are still determined to see.

Salzburg · The song made real
The garden she had
only ever pretended into.
For Mum, this was never just another garden.

As a child she had acted out The Sound of Music with her sister and a row of dolls — she was always Maria — and watched the film more than a hundred times. Every time, in her head, she was already here. Thirty years later she was standing at the real Mirabell gate — and she hadn't come alone. She had watched the film with her own three, over and over, until they wanted to see it for themselves; now they lined up on the very steps where the von Trapps sing the last verse of "Do-Re-Mi."

◆ Through their eyes
They had grown up on the film too — watching it with their mother, again and again — and arrived already hunting for the real places: the gate, the steps, the "Do-Re-Mi" fountain. Three children and a thirty-year-old promise, all looking for exactly the same spots. Which is rather the point of travelling as a family.
Maria sets out from the abbey half-afraid — "facing adventure, then why am I so scared?" — and sings herself brave enough for seven children and a whole new life."I Have Confidence" · The Sound of Music

That song — sung as Maria leaves Nonnberg Abbey to become governess to the von Trapps — was the one that came back to Mum standing here. Facing her own small adventure, a month on the road with three children instead of seven, she borrowed the line and told herself: I have confidence — for all of it.

We had reached Salzburg two hours behind schedule, after Beijing, a red-eye and a morning in Munich. So we did the city gently: one unhurried hour in the garden, and let the rest wait.

✎ From Inoshi
I must have watched this film a hundred times as a girl — always Maria, always with a row of dolls. To stand at the real gate thirty years on, my own three beside me chiming in on the bits they knew, was a promise I'd made long before any of them were born. It kept.— Inoshi

Salzburg · The Old Town
Salt built it. The Baroque
gave it its face.

Why the lanes are so narrow. Squeezed between the river Salzach and the sheer cliff of the Mönchsberg, medieval Salzburg had nowhere to grow — so it grew up and back, in tall, thin houses running deep into the block. Not for the heat; simply for want of room.

Why the squares are so grand. Then came the salt. The "white gold" hauled from the mountains made the prince-archbishops some of the richest princes in the Holy Roman Empire, and from about 1600 they cleared whole quarters to build a Baroque stage — "the Rome of the North" — with Italian architects and a vast domed cathedral.

We took it at an ice-cream pace: the guild-sign lanes, Mozart's yellow birthplace at No. 9 (passed in the crush, saved for another day), and the horse-pond fountain, before hurrying on to reach the evening Mass.

◆ Then & now
1077 — the Hohensalzburg fortress begun above the town.
1587 — Wolf Dietrich starts the Baroque rebuild.
1628 — Solari's domed cathedral consecrated.
1944 — Allied bombs pierce the dome; half the city is damaged.
1996 — the restored Old Town becomes a UNESCO site.
◆ Good to know
  • On a Saturday, the shops shut at 6pm — we learned this with three hungry children.
  • The Altstadt is small and walkable; a couple of hours covers the squares.
  • Ice-cream is the local survival strategy in the July heat.

Hallstatt · The Lake07
Hallstatt · Our day
The salt mine was closed —
so we took to the water.

We woke at five, fresh at last after the long haul from China, filled up on a hilltop-castle breakfast and drove out to Hallstatt before the heat. Two things were against us: the famous salt mine — the oldest in the world — and the Skywalk viewpoint above it were both closed. So we did the simplest, best thing instead, and took a boat out onto the lake.

The story under the pretty. Salt has been cut from this mountain for around 7,000 years — the oldest known salt mine anywhere. In the 19th century a vast prehistoric cemetery was found here, so important that an entire age of European prehistory, the "Hallstatt Culture," is named after this one village. The "white gold" made it rich long before it was pretty.

◆ The story of this place
  • 7,000 years of salt mining — the world's oldest.
  • c. 800 BC — the "Hallstatt Culture" of the early Iron Age is named for the great cemetery found here.
  • 1,200 skulls in the Bone House, many hand-painted, from a graveyard with no room to spare.
  • 1997 — village and lake join the UNESCO list.
◆ Good to know
The salt mine & Skywalk can close (they were, for us) — check before you climb. Book the funicular ahead in summer.
◆ With kids
The boat beats the viewpoint — ours loved it. We skipped the Bone House crypt for the four-year-old; older children would be fascinated.
◆ Our one tip
Come early. By mid-morning the lanes run shoulder to shoulder; at 8am the village is almost yours.

Vienna · Schönbrunn
A summer palace, and six
hundred years of Habsburgs.

Vienna kept the Habsburgs for six centuries, and they built as if they meant to stay. Schönbrunn was the family's summer home — some 1,441 rooms in "Schönbrunn yellow" — and it is really the story of one extraordinary woman. Maria Theresa inherited an empire in 1740 that half of Europe promptly tried to carve up; she fought them off, reformed the army and the schools, bore sixteen children, and turned a hunting lodge into this.

In one of these rooms a six-year-old Mozart played for her in 1762 and, the story goes, jumped straight into her lap. A century later the palace kept the age's great love story — the emperor Franz Joseph and his restless "Sisi." For a child it is a great deal of gold; for a parent, a strange thing to explain: that the most admired woman in Europe, in a palace of a thousand rooms, was for much of her life quietly unhappy.

◆ Through their eyes
By Vienna, Avin had a running question for every grand building we passed: "Is THIS a palace too?" Outside anything taller than a tram stop, the answer was usually yes.

1717
Born in Vienna, a daughter of Emperor Charles VI.
1740
Inherits the throne; the War of the Austrian Succession begins.
1762
A six-year-old Mozart plays for her at Schönbrunn.
1770
Her youngest daughter weds the French dauphin — Marie Antoinette.
Six hundred years of one family, laid across a map of everything they once ruled.Schönbrunn · "Echoes of a Monarchy"

The palace tells its own end plainly enough: from Rudolf I in 1282 to Franz Joseph in 1900, seven faces on a single wall — and then, in November 1918, in these very rooms, six hundred years of Habsburg rule quietly came to a close. The children watched a whole empire rise and fall across a projected wall of light, and, for once, forgot to fidget.

Vienna · A Little History11

A church was first consecrated on this spot in 1147; the great Gothic cathedral rose over the centuries that followed. Its roof is its glory — nearly a quarter of a million glazed tiles laid in dazzling zig-zags, the double-headed imperial eagle picked out among them, a coat of arms you can really only read from the sky.

We reached it as the crowds thinned and the stone turned honey-gold. It had survived siege, plague and the last days of the war, when fire gutted its roof in 1945; every province of Austria helped pay to rebuild it. We simply stood in the square and looked up — and then stepped inside, and the whole mood of the evening changed.

◆ Then & now
1147 — first church consecrated.
1359 — Duke Rudolf IV lays the Gothic cornerstone.
1433 — the 136m "Steffl" tower completed.
1683 — watchmen scan from the tower as the Ottomans besiege Vienna.
1945 → 1952 — fire guts the roof; every province of Austria helps rebuild it.
✎ From Inoshi
Of all the palaces and gold in Vienna, this was the evening I keep. We were tired, the light was going — and then the roof caught fire with the last of the sun and even the children went quiet. Some places you don't photograph so much as stand under.— Inoshi

◆ What we ate
Most of what we ate wasn't in restaurants at all: a castle breakfast buffet so generous it carried us through the day, ice-cream in the Salzburg heat, and — on the very first night, with every shop shut at six — a plate of pizza we slid in for minutes before closing. The schnitzel we crossed half the world for arrived last, on the road home.

Reflections · Austria
A closed salt mine, a garden
gate, an emperor's gold.

Maria sings herself brave setting out from the abbey — scared of the adventure ahead, and going anyway. Setting out with our own three and a month of road still to come, we borrowed her line and told ourselves we had the confidence for all of it. So far, through Beijing, Bavaria and all of this, it has held. Every last day of it.

◆ Favourite moment
The Mirabell gate at last — thirty years after a row of dolls — pointing our own three up to the fortress on the hill.
◆ Hardest moment
The first night: shops shut at six, dinner nearly didn't happen, and heavy bags up to the third floor of a castle with no lift.
