No. 03 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
the City of a
Hundred Spires.

Prague survived. That's the line we kept coming back to. It survived the Hussite wars, the Thirty Years' War, Nazi occupation, communist rule, and most miraculously, the wave of Allied bombing that flattened so much of Central Europe in 1945. Walking its cobblestones is walking through the only major European capital that still looks the way it did in the 1700s.

For two summer days we lived inside that surviving city: in a Gold Art Apartment overlooking the Old Town, near the river that splits the city in two. Our days began with cobblestone walks before breakfast, when the Charles Bridge was empty and the morning mist made the statues look like ghosts.

A word on where we are: this is Bohemia. Prague has been the capital of the Kingdom of Bohemia for over a thousand years — the throne of the Bohemian kings, who ruled from the castle on the hill. Its golden age came under Charles IV in the 1300s, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor, who made Prague his imperial capital and built the very things we came to see: the Charles Bridge, St. Vitus Cathedral, and the university that still bears his name. Bohemia is the historic land; Prague is, and always has been, its crown.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

PRAGUE CASTLE · INSIDE ST. VITUS CATHEDRAL
The ceiling simply
leaves you.
Step inside St. Vitus and you lose the top of it — pillars soaring up into a ribbed stone vault, and away at the far end the rose window glowing above the silver organ.
Mon 13 Jul 2026Katedrála sv. Víta · interiorBegun 1344 · finished 1929
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The full length of the nave — clustered pillars rising into a ribbed vault that runs away into shadow, and at the west end, small with distance, the great rose window above the bright pipes of the organ. This, we decided, was the most beautiful room in all of Prague.

We had marvelled at the castle and loved St. George's, but it was inside St. Vitus that the children went quiet. The cathedral is vast — pillars rising higher than seems possible into a ribbed stone ceiling lost in shadow — and the eye is pulled all the way down the nave to a rose window more than ten metres wide, throwing pools of red and blue and violet light onto the gleaming ranks of the organ below.

The pillars simply rise into the dark, and the whole west wall turns to coloured light.

It took nearly six hundred years to build — begun in 1344, and not finished until 1929 — and standing in the middle of it you can feel every one of those centuries. Grander than the castle that surrounds it, as we said before; and inside, grander than we had any way to imagine from the square.

PRAGUE CASTLE · ST. VITUS · THE MUCHA WINDOW
Painted light,
by Alfons Mucha.
Among the cathedral's stone-old windows hangs one that glows differently — the only stained glass the great master of Art Nouveau ever made.
What you are looking at
Not glass, but a painting in glass.

Where the rest of St. Vitus is filled with medieval leaded glass, Alfons Mucha — Bohemia's most famous painter, the master whose posters defined Art Nouveau in Paris — painted his design straight onto the glass in 1931, in the same glowing, sinuous line.

Around the two central saints swirl scenes from the lives of Cyril and Methodius, the brothers who brought Christianity — and the first written alphabet — to the Slavs. It remains the only stained-glass window Mucha ever made.

Who paid for it
Funded by the Slavia insurance bank — whose emblem, the goddess Slavia, hides in the glass near the figure of Christ.

It is an easy thing to walk straight past on the way to the cathedral. We are glad we stopped. A quiet corner of a vast castle, a fountain three and a half centuries old, still doing the one simple thing it was built to do.

PRAGUE CASTLE · ST. GEORGE'S BASILICA
The oldest church
on the hill.
Tucked behind the great cathedral is something far older and quieter — St. George's Basilica, a Romanesque church begun in the year 920, and the surprise of the whole castle.

Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Inside St. George's — bare golden stone, a timber roof, and a pair of curving baroque staircases sweeping up to the raised choir. Above the apse, faded frescoes of the Heavenly Jerusalem, painted more than seven hundred years ago.

Everyone climbs the hill for St. Vitus, but a few steps behind it stands the real ancient heart of the castle: St. George's Basilica, founded around the year 920 and the oldest surviving church in the whole complex. Its pretty red front is baroque, but step inside and you fall a thousand years — into a bare, golden-stone Romanesque hall, arch upon rounded arch, a plain wooden ceiling overhead and the light coming soft through small windows.

A thousand years older than it looks from the square — the quiet, ancient heart of the castle.

Twin baroque staircases curve up to a raised stone choir, and above it the curved apse still carries its medieval frescoes, ghostly now but unmistakable. After the soaring gold of St. Vitus, this old, quiet, honest church was the one that lingered.

ST. GEORGE'S BASILICA · A ROYAL TOMB

The basilica keeps a remarkable resident. In a quiet chapel lies St. Ludmila, carved in pale stone and laid under glass — the first female saint of the Czech lands. She was the grandmother of Wenceslas, the "Good King Wenceslas" of the Christmas carol, and it was she who raised him and brought him up in the faith. For that she made enemies, and in the year 921 she was murdered — strangled, the story goes, with her own veil.

The children pressed close to the glass to look. It is a strange and moving thing, to stand in the oldest church on the hill beside a woman who lived eleven hundred years ago, and whose grandson the whole world still sings about every December.

PRAGUE CASTLE · THE OLD ROYAL PALACE
The books that
ran a kingdom.
In the Old Royal Palace we found the strangest treasure of all — the painted cupboards and heraldic ceilings of the Land Rolls, the offices where a whole kingdom kept its records.
Mon 13 Jul 2026Starý královský palác · Desky zemskéThe Bohemian Land Rolls
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The cabinets of the Land Rolls — the Desky zemské — their doors thrown open on rows of leather books, each spine hand-painted with a number and a little flower or emblem so the clerks could find it again.

Beyond the churches, the castle keeps its old government rooms — the Old Royal Palace, seat of the Bohemian kings and their courts for centuries. Here we found the Land Rolls, the Desky zemské: the great books in which the whole kingdom's property, laws and lawsuits were written down, locked away in beautiful carved cupboards, each volume's spine painted with a number and a small bright emblem.

A thousand years of a kingdom's memory — kept in painted books behind carved wooden doors.

And the clerks who kept them left their mark: the walls and vaulted ceilings of these rooms are covered, edge to edge, with the painted coats of arms of the officials who served here, a riot of red-and-gold shields watching over the paperwork of a kingdom. Nearby, a little model showed the castle growing across a thousand years, tower by tower, into the city on the hill we had climbed that morning.

THE OLD ROYAL PALACE · THE CROWN JEWELS

seven locks.
Beside the throne, behind glass, blazed the coronation regalia of Bohemia — the Crown of St. Wenceslas, the golden sceptre and orb, gold and rubies and old blue sapphires on velvet.
Mon 13 Jul 2026Korunovační klenotyCrown of St. Wenceslas · 1347
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Bohemian crown jewels behind glass — the golden Crown of St. Wenceslas thick with rubies, sapphires and pearls, the sceptre and orb beside it. The real set is shown so rarely that most of us only ever meet a faithful copy.

The Crown of St. Wenceslas was made in 1347 for Charles IV, and set with some of the largest sapphires and rubies in Europe. But you cannot simply walk in and see the true jewels: they are locked in a chamber inside St. Vitus behind a door with seven locks, whose seven keys are held by seven different people — the President, the Prime Minister, the Archbishop of Prague among them — and shown to the public only once in a great while.

Seven locks, seven keys, seven keepers — and the crown behind them shown perhaps once in a decade.

So the children pressed their noses to the glass and drank in every ruby, imagining the coronations this hilltop has seen. Whether we were looking at the crown itself or its careful twin, it was the closest any of us will likely come to a real king's crown.

CHARLES BRIDGE · DAY 4
The bridge before
the crowds wake.
We left early and walked out onto the Charles Bridge to find Prague's most famous crossing almost entirely to ourselves.
Tue 14 Jul 2026Charles Bridge · Old Town → Malá StranaOn the bridge by 8:15am
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The five of us at the Old Town Bridge Tower — Charles Bridge almost to ourselves, just after eight in the morning.

The secret to the Charles Bridge is simple: go early. By mid-morning its 515 metres run shoulder to shoulder, but get there before the day fills up and it becomes a different place. We left the apartment at eight and were out on the bridge by a quarter past — and to our delight, at that hour it was almost empty. The most famous crossing in Prague, near-deserted: just us, the thirty baroque saints who have guarded it since the 1700s, and the quiet morning. The bridge has stood since 1357; for a little while, it was almost entirely ours.

The city of a hundred spires — best seen from an almost-empty bridge, early, before the crowds wake.

We walked its full 515 metres across to the Malá Strana side and back, past the thirty baroque saints, the mist lifting off the Vltava, the towers at each end catching the first real light of the day. By the time we turned back toward the Old Town, the first tour groups were only just arriving. We had beaten them by an hour — and it had been worth every minute of the early start.

The city war forgot to destroy
Ask why Prague dazzles where Warsaw, Dresden and Berlin were reduced to rubble, and the answer is a rare stroke of historical luck. While much of Central Europe was flattened by bombing and street fighting, Prague escaped both systematic bombing and a final scorched-earth battle. The result is astonishing: a thousand years of architecture standing shoulder to shoulder — Romanesque cellars beneath Gothic spires, Baroque palaces beside swirling Art Nouveau façades. Walk from the Old Town Square to the Castle and you cross centuries in an afternoon.
The city owes its grandeur to Charles IV, the Bohemian king who became Holy Roman Emperor and made Prague his imperial capital in the 1300s. He founded Charles University, the first in Central Europe, and laid the stone bridge that still bears his name — small wonder poets dubbed it the "City of a Hundred Spires." That continuity held even through 1989, when the peaceful Velvet Revolution toppled communism without shattering a windowpane.

CHARLES BRIDGE · THE SAINTS & THE LEGEND
Touch the brass,
and you'll come back.
Thirty baroque saints line the Charles Bridge. At one of them we stopped, laid our hands on the worn brass, and made the old wish: that one day we would return to Prague.
Tue 14 Jul 2026Statue of St. John of NepomukRub the plaque · make a wish
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The brass cross and relief on the parapet — the spot where St. John of Nepomuk was thrown into the Vltava in 1393. Rub the plaque, they say, and you will one day return to Prague. The grille above has quietly filled with lovers' padlocks.

Thirty statues of saints stand along the Charles Bridge, added over the centuries until the crossing became a kind of open-air gallery in stone. The most famous of them marks a dark story: St. John of Nepomuk, a priest thrown from this very bridge into the river in 1393 on the order of the king. A brass plaque and a small cross set into the parapet mark the place — polished bright by centuries of hands.

Lay your hand on the brass, make your wish, and one day the city will call you home again.

So of course we did. Five hands on the warm brass, one shared wish — that Prague would not be a place we saw only once. Above the plaque the iron grille has slowly filled with padlocks, each one a couple's promise clicked shut and the key thrown into the Vltava. A bridge of saints and secrets, and we left a little of our own hope on it.

OLD TOWN SQUARE · THE ORLOJ
A clock that has kept
time since 1410.
From the bridge we walked back into the Old Town Square for the thing everyone comes to see — the Astronomical Clock, striking on the hour.
Tue 14 Jul 2026Old Town Hall, Staroměstské náměstíThe Prague Orloj
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
All five of us beneath the Orloj on the Old Town Hall tower — the oldest astronomical clock in the world still in operation, ticking since 1410.

The Astronomical Clock — the Orloj — has hung on the wall of the Old Town Hall since 1410, which makes it the oldest clock of its kind still working anywhere in the world. It is really three instruments in one: a clock that tells the hour, a golden calendar dial below that turns once a year through the signs of the zodiac, and an astronomical dial that maps the sun and moon against the heavens. Six hundred years of gears, and it still keeps perfect time.

Six hundred years of clockwork — and every hour, on the hour, the Apostles still walk.

We joined the crowd on the hour and watched the show. As the bells sounded, two small windows opened and the twelve Apostles paraded across in turn, while beside the dial a skeleton — Death himself — tolled his bell. It is a one-minute performance that has drawn crowds into this square for six centuries; and when it ended, everyone looked up at the same moment, and smiled.

OLD TOWN · PRAGUE · IN OUR OWN WORDS
A city that looks
like King's Landing.
Prague's Old Town is majestic in a way a single photograph can't hold — a real-life King's Landing of spires and red roofs, where even the shop windows wear the city's grandeur.
Tue 14 Jul 2026Staroměstské náměstí, Old TownThe most beautiful square we saw
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The view we kept trying to photograph: a sea of red roofs falling away below the castle, the green dome of St. Nicholas rising among them. This, more than anything, is the "King's Landing" of Prague.

There is a word for Prague's Old Town, and the word is majestic. Standing in the great square, ringed by the twin Gothic spires of the Týn Church, the pastel palaces and the old town hall, I kept thinking of King's Landing — the capital city from Game of Thrones — because that is exactly what Prague looks like: a fantasy capital made real, all towers and turrets and red roofs climbing the hills. Except Prague is not a film set. It has stood, and been loved, and survived, for a thousand years.

Prague looks like King's Landing made real — and no single photograph is ever big enough to hold it.

We tried, and failed, to capture it. Every photograph came back smaller than the thing itself; the majesty of Prague simply does not fit inside a phone screen. You have to stand in the middle of it, turn slowly, and let the spires close in around you.

And there is another kind of grandeur here too. The Old Town streets are lined with every luxury brand you could name — the shopfronts as polished and stately as the palaces above them, the shopping every bit as majestic as the architecture. A city that looks like a crown, with shops to match.

The grandeur of Prague does not stop at the square. Its side streets are lined with names you would find on the smartest avenue in any capital — and, tucked among them, the Czech shops we came to love, like Manufaktura with its window full of beer cosmetics and honey soap. Every lane is cobbled, every corner turns up another spire, and at the end of almost every street a pair of green baroque domes seems to be waiting for you.

We walked slowly, in no hurry, the children's hats bobbing along the shopfronts. There are cities you march through and cities you drift through. Prague, we decided, is one to drift.

The Týn Church over the rooftops of the square — two towers eighty metres high, each crowned with a cluster of golden-tipped turrets. Between them, a golden image of the Virgin catches the morning light.

You cannot photograph the Old Town Square without them: the two great towers of the Týn Church, black and bristling with turrets, rising eighty metres over the pastel houses. The doors, we learned, open only at ten in the morning, so we admired it the way most of Prague always has — from below, in the square, heads tilted all the way back. Some buildings you go inside; this one you simply stand beneath.

The Týn towers close up — each great spire ringed by four smaller ones, gold-tipped and bristling, so that the whole thing looks less built than grown, sharp and dark over the pastel square.

PRAGUE · KLÁROV · A MEMORIAL BY THE RIVER
The lion that
flew for Britain.
Near the river stands a bronze lion with an airman's wings — Prague's thank-you to the men who slipped out of an occupied country to fight in the skies over England.
So few, so much owed
They fought for a country that later jailed them.

Some 2,500 Czechoslovak airmen fled the German occupation and made their way to Britain, where they flew with the RAF through the Battle of Britain and the long war that followed.

Many paid twice for their courage — honoured in London, then imprisoned at home after 1948 for having served in the West. This memorial, funded by Prague's British community, was unveiled in 2014; its sculptor, Colin Spofforth, is an Englishman.

"So much owed by so many to so few."
— Winston Churchill, 1940

JOSEFOV · THE OLD JEWISH CEMETERY
Twelve thousand stones,
and no more room.
Folded between the synagogues lies the oldest Jewish cemetery in Europe — where, forbidden to spread outward, the dead were laid in layer upon layer for three hundred years.
Layer upon layer
Buried twelve deep.

In use from about 1439 until 1787 and never allowed to grow, it holds perhaps 12,000 visible stones — but as many as 100,000 people are thought to lie below, buried up to twelve deep as each generation raised the earth and set the old headstones higher.

Among them rests Rabbi Judah Loew, the scholar bound forever to the legend of the Golem of Prague.

A clock that runs
the wrong way.
In the heart of Josefov stands a pink baroque town hall with two clocks — and the lower one, with its Hebrew figures, turns its hands backwards.
Tue 14 Jul 2026Židovská radnice · Jewish Town HallThe anticlockwise Hebrew clock
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Jewish Town Hall in Josefov — rose-pink walls and a little black tower. High up sits an ordinary Roman-numeral clock; below it, the famous Hebrew clock whose golden hands run anticlockwise.

Of everything in the old Jewish Quarter, the thing the children will not forget is the clock. The Jewish Town Hall — a pretty rose-coloured building beside the oldest working synagogue in Europe — carries two of them. The upper clock has Roman numerals and behaves itself. The lower clock is marked with Hebrew letters, and because Hebrew is read from right to left, its hands turn the other way: anticlockwise, backwards, the wrong way round.

We stood and watched it for a while, quietly delighted. A whole quarter that has survived everything history could throw at it, keeping its own time, in its own direction, exactly as it always has.

★ TASTES OF BOHEMIA · STREET FOOD
Eaten on our feet,
between the spires.
We never sat down to a Czech feast — we grazed from carts on the walk down from the castle. Two snacks, both pretending to be ancient, both devoured in minutes.
What we were really eating
The trdelník is hawked on every Prague corner as an "old Bohemian" treat — but it truly belongs to the Hungarian and Transylvanian kürtőskalács: dough wound around a wooden spit (a trdlo), grilled over coals, then rolled in cinnamon sugar. The ice-cream cone is a modern tourist twist, and none the worse for it.
The spiral potato is younger still, and travelled further — Korean street food that found its way to Prague's lanes. Gone in minutes between five sets of fingers.

REFLECTIONS · CZECH REPUBLIC
A city carved
in a thousand
tiny details.

What we will remember most about Prague is the detail. We loved the cathedral above everything — but it was not alone: every building around it was carved and moulded and ornamented with a thousand small details, as though no surface in the whole city was ever allowed to be plain.

And Prague taught us a lesson we would carry for the rest of the trip: in a July heatwave, you start early. Reach the sights by first light and by one o'clock you have covered almost everything — and beaten both the sun and the crowds. When we crossed the Charles Bridge at a quarter past eight it was nearly empty; the night before, that same bridge had been shoulder to shoulder. Early is everything.

Favourite moment
The cathedral — and the endless carving on every building around it. Nothing in Prague was ever plain.
Hardest moment
The July heat. The trick we learned: start at dawn, and by 1pm you have seen almost everything.
If we come back…
A cooler month, the river cruise we let go this time — and maybe a medieval dinner.
