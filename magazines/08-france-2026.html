No. 08 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
two Frances.

France was a country of contrasts. The first was Lourdes — a small Pyrenean town that becomes, every summer, one of the world's largest Catholic pilgrimage sites. The second was Paris — the loudest, most romantic city on earth.

But Lourdes was no accident. Before Aviann was born, we asked Our Lady of Lourdes for a daughter — and promised that if she came, we would name her Lourdes. She came. Before it was ever a holiday, this leg was the keeping of that promise.

Two cities. Two completely different experiences of France. Both unforgettable.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · LOURDES · DAY 20
A candle, a song,
a procession.
In 1858 a fourteen-year-old peasant girl, Bernadette Soubirous, reported eighteen apparitions of the Virgin Mary in a grotto by the Gave de Pau. The Church declared the visions authentic; today six million pilgrims come each year — for the Grotto, the spring, and the nightly Torch Procession.
We drove in from the north, the Pyrenees rising ahead — come to keep a promise, and to walk that first night in a river of candlelight around the Esplanade.

LOURDES · THE GROTTO · DAY 20
At the Grotto of Massabielle.
Where Bernadette saw the lady in white in 1858. In the evening light we queued along the worn rock, touched the smooth stone, filled our bottles at the spring, and left flowers beneath the statue — then walked home through the streets of Lourdes.

LOURDES · THE TORCH PROCESSION · DAY 20
A candle, a song, a crowd.
We arrived at 20:45 and bought candles for the children. The procession set off as dusk settled; within ten minutes thousands were moving together around the Esplanade. The Ave Maria rose in a dozen languages — and Avin, our four-year-old, sang the refrain with everyone: Ave, ave, ave Maria.

PARIS · DAY 21
Toulouse to Paris in 4h 35m.
We dropped the hire car at Toulouse airport, taxied to Gare Matabiau, and rolled in an hour early — just time for a McDonald's before boarding. Departed 10:20, Coach 12; Paris-Montparnasse by 14:55.
The city that was rebuilt to be seen

The Paris of postcards — honey-stone façades and radiating boulevards — is barely older than the camera. Between 1853 and 1870 Napoleon III unleashed Baron Haussmann on the medieval city. Its most beloved landmark was meant to be temporary: Eiffel's iron tower rose for the 1889 World's Fair, saved only by its use as a radio antenna.

SEEN AT THE LOUVRE · DAY 21
Why is the cross forked?
Our first Paris afternoon was the Louvre — a royal palace turned "universal museum," 35,000 works across eight departments. We queued for the Mona Lisa, then let the children lead us through the Denon wing.
One Christ Carrying the Cross stopped us: the cross is not the neat carpentered beam of most pictures but a rough, Y-shaped tree-trunk. It's deliberate — a crux furcata, popular in 14th–16th-century German and Netherlandish art. The fork carries the meaning: the cross as a living, branching Tree of Life, the "new tree" that undoes the Fall in Eden.

SEEN AT THE LOUVRE · DAY 21
Why is Egypt in the Louvre?
The second question, from the Egyptian halls: why is a Paris palace full of sphinxes and mummies?
It starts with Napoleon. In 1798 he invaded Egypt and brought 150 scholars, who measured and drew everything and set off a wave of "Egyptomania." Then Champollion deciphered hieroglyphics in 1822 using the Rosetta Stone, all but inventing Egyptology — and in 1826 became the first curator of a new Egyptian department. The deeper reason is the Louvre's very idea: a universal museum built to hold the art of every civilisation.

THE EGYPTIAN HALLS · DAY 21
Kings, coffins & cats.
The Louvre's Egypt runs wing after wing — colossal pharaohs, painted coffins, hieroglyphs by the acre. The children came away with one more question, about cats.
Why did Egypt love cats? 🐱

Egypt's cats began as pest-control — killing the rats and cobras that threatened the grain — and ended up as gods. The gentle house-cat became Bastet; her fierce sister the lioness Sekhmet. Cats grew so sacred that harming one could cost a life, and hundreds of thousands were mummified as offerings.

COVER STORY · EIFFEL TOWER · DAY 21
The tower at midnight.
You should not climb the Eiffel Tower at noon. You should climb it at 22:30. We watched the 22:00 sparkle from Trocadéro — 20,000 lights for five minutes — then rode the lift up into a cool, thin-crowded night.
Avin was four years old, awake at 23:00, watching Paris from the second floor. The lift went up smoothly — though Avin, wide-eyed and gripping the rail, decided it was a roller-coaster and held on tight the whole way up. The kids leaned over the rail and pointed: "That one's the Louvre!" "That one's Sacré-Cœur!"

VERSAILLES · DAY 22
The Sun King's palace.
We nearly missed our slot and queued under grey skies — but the Château de Versailles still stops you dead at the gates. Louis XIV built it as the seat of absolute power: 2,300 rooms, gardens to the horizon, gold on almost everything.
— Issue 08 · FRANCE · 15 —
VERSAILLES · INSIDE · DAY 22
Inside the palace.
Past the gilded gate, the palace opens into room after room of the State Apartments — marble and gold, ceilings painted with gods and kings. At its heart runs the Hall of Mirrors, where in 1919 the Treaty of Versailles was signed.
— Issue 08 · FRANCE · 16 —
SAINTE-CHAPELLE · DAY 22
A box of stained-glass light.
Saint Louis built this royal chapel in just seven years — finished 1248 — to house Christ's Crown of Thorns. Its fifteen windows rise 15 metres, holding 1,113 scenes of glass. On a sunny afternoon the whole upper chapel turns to jewelled light.
— Issue 08 · FRANCE · 17 —
PARIS · ALONG THE SEINE · ÎLE DE LA CITÉ
Along the Seine.
Sainte-Chapelle sits on the Île de la Cité — the island where Paris began two thousand years ago. Next to it stands the Conciergerie, where Marie-Antoinette spent her last weeks before the guillotine.
We crossed to the island in the afternoon sun. A bateau-mouche slid under the arches of the Pont Neuf — the "New Bridge," in fact the oldest in Paris — while the towers of the Conciergerie kept watch over the water.

REFLECTIONS · FRANCE
A candle in Lourdes,
a lift up the Eiffel,
two ends of France.

France is two countries layered on the same map. The southern pilgrim France of Lourdes — quiet, devout, intimate. The northern capital France of Paris — loud, electric, monumental. We did the long route between them on a TGV, eating a baguette in Coach 12.

Favourite moment
Avin's face on the Eiffel's second floor at 22:50. He could see the whole city below him.
Hardest moment
The long Versailles queue under grey skies — soaked in the drizzle before we reached the gates.
If we come back…
A week in Paris. The Loire châteaux. Brittany. Provence in lavender season.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
