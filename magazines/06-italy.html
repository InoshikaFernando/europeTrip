No. 06 · Welcome
Welcome to
everything at once.

Italy was the engine of the whole trip. We crossed the Alps from the north and dropped down into Venice on a hot Friday afternoon; eight days later we drove out of Milan toward the Swiss border — and in between we had walked through more Roman ruins, more Renaissance frescoes, more gilded churches and more plates of pasta than in all the rest of Europe combined.

We went history-first and faith-first, in more or less equal measure: a city built on water, a saint's tomb at Padua, a grandmother's shrine in the fields near Brescia, the cradle of the Renaissance at Florence, the Eternal City, a whole Roman town frozen at 79 AD, and the smallest country on earth with the largest church at its heart.

This is the longest issue of the magazine, because Italy refused to be smaller. We could not fit all of it in. We tried.

VENICE · A CITY ON THE WATER · SAT 18 JUL
A city built
on water.
Founded fifteen centuries ago by refugees who fled onto the mud islands of a lagoon because no army could reach them. They stayed, drove millions of timber piles into the mud, and built one of the richest cities in history on top of the tide.
Sat 18 Jul 2026Piazza San Marco · Doge's PalaceBus 2 from Mestre · then on foot
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Piazza San Marco — the Basilica and the Campanile, the wet stone mirroring a sky just washed by rain. Napoleon called this square "the drawing room of Europe."

We came in from the mainland the simple way — the number 2 bus from Mestre, over the long causeway to Piazzale Roma, and then the city takes over and the cars simply stop. There are no roads in Venice. There are canals, and narrow lanes, and one great square; everything else is water. Vaporetti chug past every few minutes and gondolas slip between them.

No cars. No roads. Just canals, a thousand bridges, and a city that has floated here for 1,500 years.

It was a fierce July day — close and hot even by mid-morning. But we had a plan and a booked ticket: the Doge's Palace at half past ten. What we did not expect was that the palace, the churches and one cold drink would spend the whole day quietly saving us from the heat.

A city built on a forest of trees
Venice should not exist. When Roman refugees fled Attila's Huns and later the Lombards in the fifth and sixth centuries, they sheltered in the one place invaders on horseback could not follow: a marshy lagoon of tidal mudflats. To build on water they drove millions of wooden piles deep into the mud, where the oxygen-starved silt petrified rather than rotted the timber — and laid their stone city atop a submerged forest that still holds firm today.
From this improbable foundation rose La Serenissima, the Most Serene Republic, which endured over a thousand years until 1797. Facing east, Venice made itself Europe's gateway to the riches of Byzantium and the Orient, its merchant galleys returning laden with silk and spice. Marco Polo sailed from here toward China, and the wealth of that trade gilded the domes of St Mark's Basilica — where our own Venice day would begin and end.

VENICE · PIAZZA SAN MARCO
The drawing room
of Europe.
The only true piazza in Venice — ringed by the Basilica, the bell tower, and the blue-and-gold clock where two bronze Moors have hammered out the hours since 1499.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
All five of us in the square — the golden Basilica on one side, and the Torre dell'Orologio, where the two bronze giants still strike the great bell.

You step out of a shaded lane and the square simply opens — vast, bright, and loud with pigeons and footsteps. The Basilica glitters at one end; the Campanile stands almost a hundred metres over everything; the arcades run the length of both long sides, and somewhere among them is the café where Casanova and, later, half of Europe took their coffee.

In July it is also a furnace of pale stone with no shade at all, which is exactly why the Venetians learned, centuries ago, to keep their cool indoors — and why our whole day became a hunt for the shade.

VENICE · ST MARK'S BASILICA · OUR VIGIL MASS
Gold, and our
Sunday Mass.
Built to hold the relics of St Mark, smuggled out of Alexandria in 828 under a cargo of pork so the inspectors would not look. Five domes, and more than 8,000 square metres of gold mosaic inside — and on the Saturday evening we came back, not as tourists but as worshippers.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The domes of gold glass — the Ascension in the central cupola, lit by shafts of evening light. More than eight thousand square metres of mosaic climb the vaults of St Mark's.

By day the Basilica now runs on timed tickets and a queue that snakes across the square, so we admired the front — the mosaics, the winged lion of St Mark, the four bronze horses on the loggia (copies now; the looted originals kept safe inside) — and left the inside for later.

And later was the better idea. As the light softened we came back for the Saturday evening Mass, free, entering as worshippers through the little Piazzetta dei Leoni door on the north side. No ticket, no queue — just the church doing the thing it was built for.

For a Catholic family it was our Sunday Mass, sung beneath eight centuries of gold with the low sun in the domes.

Fulfilling the Sunday obligation on the Saturday evening was also a small piece of planning: it meant the next morning could be given wholly to St Anthony at Padua and to Rosa Mistica, without a Mass to catch on the road.

VENICE · PALAZZO DUCALE · 10:30
The palace that
cooled the day.
For a thousand years the seat of the Doge and the Venetian Republic — the courtroom, the treasury, and the throne of a sea empire. On a blazing July afternoon, it was also the coolest place in the city.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Scala d'Oro — the Golden Staircase — its vault crusted in 24-carat gold stucco. Only those whose names were written in the Republic's Book of Gold were allowed to climb it.

We stepped in from the glare and the thick marble simply swallowed the heat. Cool courtyards, cool stone stairs, cool gilded halls one after another — the Republic built for power, and by happy accident for shade.

Venezia is quite hot — but that palace cooled everything down.

Up the Golden Staircase, through the great council chambers, the ceilings drip with gold and the walls are hung with painted glory. It is impossibly grand and, on that day, blessedly cold.

— Issue 06 · VENICE · The Doge's Palace —
VENICE · INSIDE THE PALACE
Ceilings you lie
down to read.
Room after room painted by Veronese and Tintoretto — the Republic hiring its greatest artists to paint its own power onto every wall and ceiling.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Veronese's The Rape of Europa, in the Anticollegio — the waiting room where foreign ambassadors sat before meeting the Doge, surrounded by some of the finest paint in Europe.

The walls here are a who's-who: Veronese's shimmering myths, Tintoretto's brooding canvases, and vast votive scenes of doges kneeling before the Virgin and the Cross — the state painting itself as blessed, chosen, eternal.

You crane your neck until it aches. Every gilded frame overhead holds another masterpiece, and the children quickly learn that the best view is with your head tipped all the way back.

— Issue 06 · VENICE · Painted Power —
VENICE · THE PRISONS
Across the
Bridge of Sighs.
The enclosed stone bridge that carried prisoners from the courtroom straight to the cells — named for the last sigh a captive gave at Venice through its little latticed windows.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The New Prisons — damp stone cells behind heavy iron grilles, reached across the covered bridge. Casanova was locked in the cells above the palace, and famously escaped through the roof in 1756.

From the gilded halls the route drops suddenly into the dark. You cross the Bridge of Sighs, glimpse the lagoon once through its stone lattice, and then you are in the cells — cold, low, and heavy with iron.

Prisoners' names and marks are still scratched into the old wood. The children pressed their faces to the grilles and went quiet — the one truly spooky corner of a very grand day.

Venice · a day on foot
Between the palaces, the city itself.
We crossed the lagoon and spent a whole day chasing shade through Venice — palace to church, canal to café — before a summer downpour washed the square clean at dusk. This is the walk in between.
The west front of St Mark's — golden mosaics over five portals, the winged lion above, the bronze horses on the loggia.
Inside, the marble forest of the nave, every arch above it sheathed in mosaic — where we came back for the Vigil Mass.
A quiet corner of the Doge's Palace — the Museo dell'Opera, where the palace's original Gothic capitals are kept safe out of the weather.
San Zulian, off the busy Merceria — a Sansovino church and a cool, still nave: the day's second refuge from the heat.
The Campanile from directly below — the near-hundred-metre bell tower that fell in 1902 and was rebuilt "as it was, where it was."
Step off the main lanes and Venice becomes green water and arched bridges — a gondola slipping under a little humpback bridge.
The gondola station at Orseolo, the striped-shirted gondoliers calling the corners to one another as they turn.
We didn't ride one — but we held a two-euro magnet of the Bridge of Sighs up against the real thing, and called it a souvenir.
The day's third refuge: the coldest air-conditioning in the city and a row of iced drinks, before the bells called us to Mass.

PADUA · BASILICA DEL SANTO · 07:30
The road south begins
at a saint's tomb.
We left Venice at first light and stopped, an hour down the road, at the great domed Basilica of Saint Anthony — where the beloved saint of lost things and lost causes has lain since 1231.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: the Tomb of St Anthony, its marble worn smooth by eight centuries of pilgrims' hands and heaped with photographs and written petitions. Right: the great nave, cool and dim, light pouring down from the domes.

You come in from the morning heat and the basilica is vast and hushed, seven domes riding over a forest of stone. We made straight for the Cappella dell'Arca, where the saint's tomb stands against a wall of carved marble — and joined the slow line of pilgrims who lay a hand flat on the green stone and pray.

You lay your hand on the wall, close your eyes, and ask — for the lost thing, the sick, the worry carried from home.

Around the tomb the wall is papered with photographs and folded notes, thank-yous and pleas in a dozen languages. We lit our candles, said our prayers, and left St Anthony a few of our own worries before the road called us on.

ROSA MISTICA · FONTANELLE DI MONTICHIARI
A grandmother's
favourite miracle.
West of Verona, down a country lane near Brescia, a spring in the fields where — in 1947 — Our Lady is said to have appeared to a young nurse named Pierina Gilli, asking to be honoured as Rosa Mystica, the Mystical Rose.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Ingresso — the way in to the Santuario Rosa Mistica at Fontanelle, the Madonna's image on the banner and a summer sky over the trees.

Some of these stories we have known all our lives. In a devout Catholic home a grandmother once told them to the children the way others tell fairy tales — the apparitions, the graces, Our Lady coming to a simple nurse in a small Italian town. Of them all, Rosa Mistica was her favourite.

To stand at last at the spring she used to tell us about — with our own children beside us — was to close a circle she began long ago.

So this was the one detour we made against the map: a good hour and a half the wrong way, west when Florence lay south. Nobody minded. Some places you go for the miles, and some you go for the grandmother who first told you the story.

ROSA MISTICA · MATER ECCLESIAE
The Mystical Rose,
Mother of the Church.
Three roses — white, red and gold — for the spirit of prayer, of sacrifice, and of penance. In 2024 the little country shrine was recognised at last as a diocesan Marian sanctuary of the Diocese of Brescia.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
ROSA MISTICA · MATER ECCLESIAE — the Mystical Rose, Mother of the Church. Pilgrims from around the world fill the simple hall, many kneeling, many in tears.

The shrine itself is plain — a covered hall of quiet folk from every corner of the earth, rosaries moving, a low murmur of prayer in a dozen tongues. There is nothing grand about it, and that is rather the point.

The devotion asks for small, ordinary things: prayer, sacrifice, penance — and a great deal of trust. We sat with the children among the pilgrims and let the stillness do its work, a world away from the Venetian crowds of the day before.

ROSA MISTICA · THE HOLY WATER POOL
Shoes off, and
into the water.
A shallow tiled basin filled with the spring water, watched over by a white statue of Our Lady — here pilgrims take off their footwear and step in barefoot, an act of trust from the days the Madonna asked that the sick be brought to the spring.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The pool — a tiled square filled with the holy water of Fontanelle, a handrail to steady you, and Our Lady in white at the head of it, orchids and lilies at her feet. Pilgrims slip off their shoes and wade in.

You leave your shoes at the edge and step down into the cold, clear spring water, one hand on the rail, and walk its length under the gaze of the little white Madonna. It is quiet, and simple, and somehow enormous.

This is what our grandmother's story had always been about, without our quite knowing it — not a spectacle, but this: bare feet, cold water, Our Lady in white, and a family come at last to stand where the water rises.

ROSA MISTICA · THE SPRING & THE GRACES
At the spring,
a wall of thank-yous.
At Fontanelle — "the little springs" — pilgrims come to the water and to the white statue of Our Lady, and leave behind marble plaques of two words repeated a thousand times: grazie ricevute, graces received.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: Our Lady at the spring, rosary in hand, white orchids at her feet. Right: the family among the ex-voto plaques — walls of grazie from pilgrims whose prayers, they believe, were answered here.

We filled our little bottles at the spring to carry home — the water of Fontanelle, all the way to New Zealand — and stood a while before the white statue with her rosary and her orchids.

A grandmother's story, a country spring, five of us with our bottles full — some prayers take a lifetime to arrive.

Then south at last, back to the motorway and on towards Florence — a little behind schedule, a little quieter than we set out, carrying between us a small bottle of water and a very old story finally kept.

FLORENCE · CRADLE OF THE RENAISSANCE · 15:00
Where the Renaissance
became inevitable.
We rolled in from the north around three — over the Apennines, past the great IKEA at the city's edge — and parked the car for a city built to be walked. This is Florence: the cradle of the Renaissance, packed into a mile of the Arno.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
All five of us under the Duomo — Santa Maria del Fiore — its façade a fever of green, white and pink marble, Giotto's bell tower alongside. Behind rises Brunelleschi's dome, still the largest masonry dome ever raised.

The shrine had run us late, so Florence began at a gallop: park outside the ZTL, drop the bags, and walk straight into the storm of marble that is the cathedral square. The Duomo simply stops you — there is no easing into it.

Half a mile of the Arno holds more art than most whole countries. You do not so much visit Florence as wade through it.

Two great tickets waited: the Uffizi at a quarter past four, and the gold-ceilinged Baptistery. The Bell Tower climb we let go — a shrine is worth more than a staircase — and set off on foot.

How one city reinvented the world
Why did the Renaissance ignite in Florence? Follow the money. In the fifteenth century the Medici, a family of ambitious bankers, held the accounts of popes and princes — and poured their fortune into art. Their patronage, and that of rival Florentine guilds, gave the world Botticelli, a young Leonardo da Vinci, and a temperamental genius named Michelangelo, whose David still stands as the city's marble heartbeat.
Nowhere is Florentine daring clearer than the cathedral dome. For over a century the Duomo gaped open, its planned crown deemed impossible to build. Then a goldsmith, Filippo Brunelleschi, cracked the puzzle: a self-supporting double shell of over four million bricks laid in a herringbone pattern, raised without the wooden centring everyone believed essential. Completed in 1436, it remains the largest masonry dome ever built.

FLORENCE · THE UFFIZI · 16:15
Booked at a quarter
past four.
Once the Medici's offices — uffizi means "offices" — now one of the greatest galleries on earth, a long U of corridors hung with the whole Renaissance.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: the Niobe Room — Roman marble copies of Greek Hellenistic originals beneath a great battle canvas. Right: the Laocoön by Baccio Bandinelli, a marble copy of the ancient Rhodian group (unearthed in Rome, 1506), commissioned by Pope Leo X around 1520.

The timed ticket spared us the worst of the queue, and then it is simply room after room of things you have seen in books all your life — Botticelli, Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael — hung on the walls as if it were nothing.

The children lasted better than we feared, partly for the statues (easier to love than paintings at eight) and partly for the windows: the long corridor looks straight down onto the Arno and across to the Ponte Vecchio.

Florence · the Uffizi & the Baptistery
The whole Renaissance, room by room.
For a Catholic family the Uffizi is a strange, double thing — a museum, and wall after wall of the very faith we had just been praying in Padua and at Fontanelle, here painted by the finest hands the world has produced.
A Holy Family tondo — the round panel Florentine painters favoured for the home: Mary, the Christ Child, the infant St John and an aged Joseph.
A crowded narrative panel by Andrea del Sarto (1486–1530) — the painter Vasari praised as senza errori, the faultless one.
A sacred scene set in a richly carved and gilded frame — the Florentine rooms are full of them.
A multi-panelled altarpiece — a mitred bishop-saint and companions above a predella of small narrative scenes.
And, framed in a window, an antique marble — a Roman copy after a Greek original, of the kind the Medici collected obsessively.
Then, across the square, the Baptistery — the mosaic vault a whole heaven of gold, patriarchs and prophets in tiny cubes of glass.
The eight-sided dome overhead — Genesis, the life of Christ, and a colossal Last Judgement laid in gold eight centuries ago.

FLORENCE · PIAZZA DELLA SIGNORIA
A square that is
an open-air museum.
Beneath the tower of the Palazzo Vecchio, Florence keeps some of its greatest sculpture out in the open air, free to all — colossal marble and green bronze under the Loggia dei Lanzi.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: Bandinelli's Hercules and Cacus guarding the Palazzo Vecchio, where a copy of Michelangelo's David also stands. Right: Cellini's bronze Perseus with the Head of Medusa, cast in a single pour in 1554.

You come out of the Uffizi straight into this square, and it is almost comic how much marble is simply standing about — Perseus with Medusa's head, the Rape of the Sabines, Neptune's fountain, and a David where the real one once stood.

It is the best free show in Florence. The children ran between the statues in the low gold evening light while we caught our breath and worked out where dinner might be.

FLORENCE · THE BAPTISTERY
Look up: a ceiling
of solid gold.
The oldest building on the cathedral square — an eight-sided marble box where Dante was baptised — and, inside, a dome sheathed in shimmering 13th-century gold mosaic.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: the mosaic vault — a whole heaven of gold (half-hidden this summer behind restorers' scaffolding). Right: Donatello & Michelozzo's tomb of Antipope John XXIII, set into the marble wall.

After the Uffizi you step into the Baptistery, tip your head all the way back, and simply stare. The whole ceiling glitters — angels, patriarchs, Genesis and the Last Judgement — in tiny cubes of gold glass laid eight centuries ago.

Every Florentine for six hundred years was baptised under this gold — Dante among them.

The famous bronze doors — Ghiberti's Gates of Paradise — are outside (the originals safe in the museum). But it is the ceiling that stays with you: a quiet, blazing heaven you enter off a busy square.

FLORENCE · STREET FOOD
Lunch, folded
into warm bread.
Between the galleries, the cheapest and happiest meal in Florence: schiacciata — flat Tuscan bread split and piled with prosciutto, pecorino, roast vegetables and sun-dried tomatoes.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
A tray of schiacciata — mortadella and stracciatella, roast courgette and tomato, chicken and pecorino — eaten standing in the street for a handful of euros.

Florence taught us that you do not need a table to eat well. A few euros at a bakery counter buys a slab of warm schiacciata heavy with filling, and you eat it on a step with the family, watching the crowds go by.

It fed five of us for the price of one sit-down plate, and nobody complained. The Renaissance can wait twenty minutes; the warm bread cannot.

Two stops

A long drive is never only a line between two cities. Between the great set-pieces we broke the journey twice — for a tower that will not stand straight, and a hilltop town of pink stone.

ROME · THE ETERNAL CITY · DAYS 12–14
A city the Romans
built, the popes kept.
Three days in Rome were not enough. The Colosseum, the Forum, the Pantheon, the Spanish Steps, the Trevi Fountain — every corner had something two thousand years old, and every third street had a reason to stop for gelato.
Mon 20 — Wed 22 Jul 2026Rome · the Eternal CityRoughly 30 km on foot
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Colosseum — completed around 80 AD, it held some 50,000 spectators for four centuries of games. Even a ruin, it is the largest amphitheatre ever built.

You cannot understand Rome in three days, so we did not try. We picked a handful of things — the Colosseum and the Forum, the Pantheon, the Spanish Steps, the Trevi Fountain — and walked slowly between them in the July heat, eating gelato every few streets to keep three children moving.

All roads lead to Rome. And they really did: some 80,000 km of Roman roads still shape the map of Europe.

Two of our Roman days spilled outward: one south to Pompeii and Naples, and one given wholly to the Vatican — each big enough for its own chapter, which follows. This is the city in between: ancient, sun-baked, impossible, and endlessly worth the walking.

Why Rome, and not Jerusalem?
It is one of history's great ironies. Jesus was born in Bethlehem and died in Jerusalem — in the Middle East, in Asia — yet the faith that grew from him came to be governed from Rome, and Europe became its heartland. The reason begins with an empire. First-century Judea was a Roman province, so Christianity was born inside the Roman world and spread along its roads. The apostle Paul carried it west through Asia Minor and Greece; Peter and Paul were both martyred in Rome, the capital, and the bishops of Rome — the popes — claimed Peter's authority ever after. When the Emperor Constantine legalised the faith in 313, and Theodosius made it the empire's official religion in 380, its centre of gravity was already Roman and Western.
Christianity did reach Asia — earlier and further than most people realise. Missionaries carried it along the Silk Road to Persia, to India (the St Thomas Christians of Kerala trace their church to the apostle himself), and even to Tang-dynasty China by 635; Armenia had become the world's first Christian state back in 301. But from the 7th century the swift rise of Islam absorbed those ancient Christian lands across the Middle East and Central Asia, while in Europe the Church of Rome outlived the empire that bore it, converted the Germanic and Slavic peoples, and became the one institution holding a fractured continent together. And so a Middle-Eastern faith, kept and spread from Rome, became the story of Europe.

ROME · THE ANCIENT CENTRE
Walking through
two thousand years.
You do not visit ancient Rome so much as walk into it — an amphitheatre, a triumphal arch, and a Forum where the Republic once argued, all within a few hot streets of one another.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: all five of us under the Colosseum. Right: the Arch of Constantine (315 AD) beside it — raised to mark the victory that would make Constantine emperor and, in time, turn Rome Christian.

The great ring of the Colosseum still throws its shadow over the same ground where crowds once poured in through numbered arches. Next to it the Arch of Constantine marks the battle of 312 that changed everything — the one after which the emperor threw his lot in with the Christians.

From there it is a short, sun-struck walk to the Forum and the Palatine, the broken columns and worn paving of the city that once ran the Western world. We drank litres of water, wore our hats, and let the children set the pace between patches of shade.

The Trevi coins, the Pantheon's open eye, the Spanish Steps at dusk — Rome hands you a wonder on nearly every corner.

And in the evenings, the lighter Rome: a coin thrown backwards into the Trevi Fountain, the Pantheon's great dome open to the sky after two thousand years, and gelato on the Spanish Steps as the stone went gold. A city that never quite lets you rest, and never quite lets you leave.

The Temple of Apollo — and, low on the horizon behind it, Vesuvius itself. The Pompeians rebuilt this temple to their gods in the very shadow of the mountain that would bury them.

Two thousand years ago Pompeii was a busy town of some fifteen thousand people — merchants, bakers, politicians and slaves — living beneath a green, vine-covered mountain no one knew was a volcano. Then, in 79 AD, Vesuvius exploded, firing ash and rock thirty-three kilometres into the sky. Pumice rained down for hours; by the next morning superheated clouds swept the streets, and the city was gone, sealed under six metres of ash.

The closest thing to time travel humans have ever managed.

It lay forgotten for nearly seventeen centuries, rediscovered by accident in 1748 — not as ruins but as a city frozen mid-morning: loaves still in ovens, election slogans on the walls, cart-ruts worn into the stone. We walked straight in.

The city that death kept alive
In 79 AD Mount Vesuvius erupted with the force of many atomic bombs, burying the Roman town of Pompeii under metres of ash and pumice in a single day. Yet the catastrophe performed a strange mercy. Sealed from air and moisture, the city was not destroyed but paused: frescoes kept their colour, loaves of bread sat carbonised in ovens, graffiti still teased from the walls. Plaster later poured into voids in the ash revealed the very shapes of the dying, caught mid-gesture.
Rediscovered in the eighteenth century after seventeen centuries underground, Pompeii handed us an unmatched window into everyday Roman life. Nearby Naples still lives in the volcano's shadow, Vesuvius looming over the bay — dormant but far from dead. Fittingly, this exuberant corner of Italy also gave the world one of its great pleasures: the pizza, and specifically the tomato-and-mozzarella Margherita.

The Forum was Pompeii's public square — temples, law courts, markets and politics, all under the eye of Vesuvius.

This open rectangle was the centre of everything: elections argued, deals struck, gods worshipped. The fallen column drums and carved capitals still lie where the eruption threw them, and the paving is grooved where carts and market fences once slotted in.

The children ran the length of it while we tried to picture it roofed in marble and noise. Two thousand years on, it is quiet — but the shape of a working Roman town is unmistakable.

POMPEII · THE ANTIQUARIUM
What the ash
kept safe.
The eruption that destroyed a city also preserved it — statues, frescoes and the smallest daily things, held two thousand years beneath the ash.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: a draped marble goddess with a bird at her feet, in the site's Antiquarium — the folds of her robe as crisp as the day the ash swallowed them. Right: back out into the empty streets, walked smooth two thousand years ago.

Because Pompeii was buried so fast and so completely, what came out of the ground was not weathered ruin but a town's belongings: painted walls, mosaics, glassware, even carbonised loaves and eggs. The finest pieces now fill the Antiquarium here and the great National Archaeological Museum in Naples — a short walk, as it happens, from where we would have dinner.

Standing before a statue this sharp, it is hard to believe the centuries between us. The ash was merciless to the living and astonishingly kind to their things.

POMPEII · THE CASTS
The people
of the ash.
The most haunting thing at Pompeii is not a building. It is the shape of a person, curled against the falling ash.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
A plaster cast of one of the victims of 79 AD — not a sculpture, but the exact space a real person left behind in the hardened ash.

When the buried bodies decayed, they left hollow cavities in the rock-hard ash. In the 1860s an archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli realised he could pour liquid plaster into those hollows — and out came the exact shape of a man, woman or child in their final moment.

Not statues. The actual traces of people who lived and died here.

The children went quiet in front of them. It is one thing to read that 79 AD happened; it is another to stand before someone who was there.

We booked the fast train from Rome, and promptly missed it. A quick re-book put us on the next one; by mid-morning we were in Naples, eating the world's most famous pizza at da Michele, then rattling out to the ruins on the little Circumvesuviana.

THE VATICAN · WED 22 JUL
The smallest country,
the largest church.
A walled enclave of forty-four hectares inside Rome — the smallest sovereign state on earth — and at its heart the largest church in Christendom, raised over the grave of a fisherman.
Wed 22 Jul 2026Vatican City · The Holy SeeThe whole day
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
St Peter's Square in the early light — the great Egyptian obelisk, the colonnade curving away, and Michelangelo's dome beyond. We came early, and gave the Vatican a whole day.

Everywhere else on this trip we ran out of time. Not here. Rome we could only sample; the Vatican we finished — from the square at opening to the last hall of the Museums, St Peter's, the Grottoes beneath it, the Sistine Chapel, and the walk back over the river past Castel Sant'Angelo.

One country you can cross on foot in twenty minutes — and could not exhaust in a lifetime.

For a Catholic family this was, quietly, the centre of the whole journey: not just the greatest sight in Rome, but the seat of the faith we had carried with us from St Mark's to St Anthony to a spring in the fields near Brescia. We had saved it for last on purpose.

How a fisherman's grave became a country
Around 64 AD the apostle Peter was martyred in Nero's circus on the Vatican hill and buried in a simple grave nearby. Two and a half centuries later the Emperor Constantine raised a great basilica directly over that tomb. By the 1500s it was crumbling, and the popes rebuilt it from the ground up — a project of 120 years and the genius of Bramante, Raphael, Michelangelo and Bernini — into the vast church that stands today, the altar still set precisely above the apostle's grave.
The Vatican was not always so small. For over a thousand years the popes ruled the broad Papal States across central Italy — until Italian unification swept them away in 1870. For nearly sixty years the popes called themselves "prisoners in the Vatican," until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 created the modern Vatican City State: independent, neutral, with its own post office, radio, railway and Swiss Guard, and a population of only a few hundred. The world's smallest country, and the spiritual home of over a billion people.

THE VATICAN · INSIDE ST PETER'S
A church built
to hold heaven.
You step through the doors and the scale simply refuses to make sense — a nave the length of two football pitches, a dome you could stand the Statue of Liberty beneath, and everything, everywhere, in marble and gold and bronze.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: Bernini's bronze baldacchino (1623–34), nearly 30 metres tall, its twisted columns cast from bronze stripped off the Pantheon. Right: Michelangelo's dome soaring above it — the words of Christ to Peter ringing the base in letters two metres high.

Directly beneath the baldacchino and the dome lies the confessio, sunk before the high altar — and beneath that, the tomb of St Peter himself. Only the pope says Mass at this altar, set over the apostle's grave as it has been for seventeen centuries.

Nothing prepares you for the size of it. The children simply stopped, tipped their heads back, and went quiet.

We walked the length of the nave in a kind of daze — past markers in the floor showing where the world's other great cathedrals would end if you set them down inside this one, none of them reaching even close to the far wall.

THE VATICAN · THE PIETÀ
Marble made
tender.
Just inside the door, behind glass, the one work that stops nearly everyone: Michelangelo's Pietà — Mary holding the body of her son, carved from a single block when the sculptor was just twenty-four.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Michelangelo's Pietà (1498–99) — the young Virgin cradling her dead son, the folds of her robe and the weight of his body drawn from cold stone. It is the only work Michelangelo ever signed.

He carved it at twenty-four, and when visitors credited the work to another sculptor he crept back one night and cut his name across the sash on Mary's breast — the only signature he ever left on anything. It has stood behind glass since 1972, when a man attacked it with a hammer; the marble was painstakingly restored.

You can see the softness of cloth and skin in stone that has not moved for five hundred years.

For all the gold and scale around it, this quiet white group is the thing you carry out of the door — a mother, a son, and grief made unbearably gentle.

The high altar and the sunken confessio before it, ringed with oil lamps that burn day and night over the tomb of St Peter.
The long marble floor of the nave, inlaid with coloured stone and the measured lengths of the world's other cathedrals.
A Baroque papal monument — a pope in bronze above allegories of Charity and Justice in white marble, the Bernini formula copied for two centuries.
A side altar whose "altarpiece" is in fact a mosaic — the Vatican kept its own mosaic studio precisely to make these.
Carved marble around another altar, saints and angels worked in high relief in the cool of a side chapel.
Close up on a tomb — a marble frieze of mourners and virtues, the kind of detail you walk past a hundred times without seeing.
A saint's statue high in its niche above a doorway; each of the great pillars carries one of the founders of a religious order.
A mosaic icon set above an ancient wooden crucifix at a quiet side altar — a place to stop and pray amid the crowds.

Down among the popes.

THE VATICAN · THE MUSEUMS & THE SISTINE CHAPEL
Four hours, and
then we cried.
Seven kilometres of galleries lead, in the end, to a single room — and to a ceiling that has undone grown travellers for five hundred years.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: one of the Museums' great frescoed corridors — the walls and vaults painted end to end, a river of colour that never seems to stop. Right: a quieter gallery among the miles of collections the popes have gathered over centuries.

The Vatican Museums are not one museum but dozens, strung along corridors that run for the better part of seven kilometres — the Gallery of Maps, the Raphael Rooms, Egyptian mummies, Etruscan gold, modern art, and everywhere the crowd shuffling the same way, toward one door.

And then the Sistine Chapel. No photographs are allowed, and the guards hush the room every few minutes, but nothing can prepare you for looking up at Michelangelo's ceiling — the Creation, the outstretched fingers of God and Adam — and, on the far wall, the vast, terrible Last Judgement.

We stood under the ceiling and cried. So did half the room.

Michelangelo painted the ceiling between 1508 and 1512, flat on his back on the scaffolding, and returned decades later for the wall. It is the room where the cardinals still lock themselves away to elect a pope. We simply stood in it, necks craned, until the tears came — and they did.

After a whole morning on our feet, the reward: a cool trattoria in the old Borgo, and an evening walk back over the Tiber past a fortress that was once an emperor's tomb.

Borgo Pio is where the Vatican comes to eat — a narrow lane of checked tablecloths a two-minute walk from the colonnade. We collapsed into chairs, ordered too much pasta, and let the children work through a whole basket of bread while the morning caught up with us.

One country, one church, one very long and unforgettable day — and pasta, at the end of it, tasting like a reward.

Then the walk back over the river toward Piazza Navona, past Castel Sant'Angelo — built as the Emperor Hadrian's mausoleum, later a papal fortress linked to the Vatican by a secret raised passage the popes could flee along in a siege. Gelato at Navona, and the Vatican day was done.

We came into Milan the long way — a whole day's drive north from Rome, a quick lunch somewhere on the autostrada, and then the flat green plain of Lombardy. Milan was the last Italian city on our map, and the only one where we were not tourists in a hotel: old friends live here, and they took us in.

We saved our energy that first evening, because the Duomo — the reason everyone comes to Milan — was booked for the morning. But even at dusk the square pulled us in: pigeons, street sellers, and that impossible marble front glowing pink in the last light.

MILAN · THE DUOMO
A forest of stone,
six centuries tall.
Begun in 1386 and not truly finished until the twentieth century — the largest church in Italy, raised from pink Candoglia marble, its nave a canyon between fifty-two colossal columns.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Inside the Duomo — fifty-two soaring piers march toward the apse, and the morning sun falls in solid shafts through some of the tallest Gothic windows in the world.

Nothing prepares you for the inside. You step out of a bright Italian square into a cool grey twilight, and the columns simply keep rising until they vanish. There are fifty-two of them — one, they say, for every week of the year — and each is thick as a house.

It took nearly six hundred years to build. In Milan, "as long as the making of the Duomo" still means a job that never ends.

The marble came from a single quarry at Candoglia on Lake Maggiore, floated down rivers and canals to the heart of the city for six centuries. Generation after generation of Milanese lived and died with the cathedral still unfinished around them.

Where industry learned to wear couture
Milan wears two faces. Long Italy's engine of industry and finance — home to its stock exchange and its factories — the city channelled that same restless energy into style, rising in the twentieth century to reign as a world capital of fashion and design. The autumn shows draw the globe; yet the ambition is nothing new: it took roughly six centuries, from 1386 into the twentieth, to complete the Duomo, a forest of pink-white marble spires and thousands of statues.
Milan also guards one of art's most fragile treasures. Leonardo da Vinci painted The Last Supper directly onto a refectory wall in the 1490s, using an experimental technique that began flaking almost at once. It has clung to survival ever since — most miraculously in 1943, when Allied bombing flattened the surrounding convent yet the sandbagged wall bearing Leonardo's masterpiece somehow still stood.

MILAN · THE DUOMO · MARBLE & SKY

Along the aisles, Renaissance monuments in carved marble; and up on the roof, a stone forest of spires crowned by the golden Madonnina, watching over the whole city.

The aisles are lined with the dead of Milan — archbishops and soldiers laid out in marble, guarded by saints and framed in coloured stone. You walk past centuries of the city's memory just crossing from one door to the next.

For two hundred years no roof in Milan was allowed to rise higher than the golden Madonnina on her spire.

MILAN · THE GALLERIA
The world's most
beautiful arcade.
Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II — an iron-and-glass cathedral of shopping from the 1860s, cross-shaped beneath a great dome, and known to every Milanese as il salotto: the city's drawing room.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Left: the barrel-vaulted glass roof running the length of the arcade. Right: the great triumphal entrance, carved with the names of the king and of Giuseppe Mengoni, the architect who designed it.

Next door to the cathedral, Milan keeps its other temple — to commerce. Giuseppe Mengoni built it between 1865 and 1877: two glass-roofed streets crossing under an octagonal dome, floored in marble mosaic and lined with cafés that have poured coffee for a hundred and fifty years.

Days before the king came to open it, Mengoni fell from the scaffolding of his own great dome and died.

It is the oldest working shopping gallery in the world; Prada opened its very first shop here in 1913. We just walked it slowly, necks craned at the glass, while the light came down like water.

REFLECTIONS · ITALY
Two thousand years
in eight days.

Italy was the longest, hottest, most exhausting and most rewarding leg of the whole journey. We walked something close to eighteen kilometres a day, ate gelato most afternoons, and lost count of the churches. We stood under a heaven of gold in Venice, laid our hands on a saint's tomb in Padua, filled little bottles at a country spring near Brescia, wept in the Sistine Chapel, and walked a whole Roman city that had stopped in a single morning in 79 AD.

The children will remember the volume — of stone, of frescoes, of statues, of pasta. Italy is a country that does not edit. It piles its history up on the streets and asks you to walk through it. We did, all eight days of it.

Favourite moment
The Sistine Chapel. We stood under Michelangelo's ceiling and cried — and so did half the room.
Hardest moment
Rome and Pompeii at close to 40°C in mid-July. Four water bottles per person, per day, and never quite enough.
If we come back…
A week each for Rome, Florence and the Amalfi Coast. Sicily next — and then Puglia.
We came to Italy for its art and its ruins, and we did see them — but what we will carry home is the faith woven through it all: a Vigil Mass under the gold of St Mark's, a hand on St Anthony's tomb, bare feet in a country spring, and five of us in tears beneath the Sistine ceiling. Italy gave us the greatest museum on earth, and reminded us, gently, that most of it was built as a prayer.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
