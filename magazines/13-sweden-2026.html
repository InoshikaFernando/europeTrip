No. 13 · Who We Are
Who we are,
why we travel.

We are the Munasinghe-Fernandos — two parents, three children, and one home a very long way from here, on the far side of the world in New Zealand. This summer we set out to cross Europe end to end: thirty-odd nights, more countries than the children can now name in order, and more trains, cathedrals and scoops of gelato than we could ever count.

We travel for two reasons. The first is the obvious one — to show our three how wide and various the world really is, to let them stand under the domes and on the bridges and taste it all first-hand.

The second is quieter, and for us it is the real one: to close the distance to the people we love. Our family is scattered across the planet, and a journey like this is the only way to gather a little of it back, even for a few days.

That second reason is what this whole issue is about. Sweden was never on the list for its sights. It was on the list for a sister — and for two days in Gothenburg, the distance simply disappeared.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

No. 13 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
family.

For three weeks this trip had been about places. The Forbidden City. The Colosseum. Cathedrals, castles, museums, timetables. Then the train crossed the water into Sweden, and for two days the itinerary held only one thing on it — the people we had come all this way to see.

We reached Gothenburg on Monday afternoon, in from Copenhagen on the X2000 over the Øresund, and spent the days that followed the way you only can with family and friends: slowly. There was no monument to tick off, no ticket to pre-book. There was a sister, there were cousins for our kids, and long unhurried walks that wandered from the old town to the new city across the river and back.

So this is the warmest issue we have made. It has no historical set-piece to march you through — just a summer heart made of flowers in a square, a colourful tower on the far bank, a little tram in a shopping centre, a fridge magnet held up against the real skyline, and the long Nordic light that refused to go dark until well past ten.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

Over the water,
into family.
The X2000 slipped out of Copenhagen, crossed the Øresund, and turned north up the Swedish coast. By late afternoon we were in Gothenburg — and the family we had crossed a continent to reach was waiting.
Mon 3 Aug 2026Copenhagen → GöteborgX2000 · over the Øresund
GÖTEBORG · THE VALLGRAVEN
Along the Vallgraven — Gothenburg's old moat-canal, its banks planted with summer flowers, grand nineteenth-century façades and a green copper dome mirrored in the still water.
The Dutch-built gateway to the west

Gothenburg was a deliberate act of statecraft. In 1621 King Gustavus Adolphus chartered a city on Sweden's rugged west coast — a foothold on the narrow strip of shore that gave the kingdom its only real window onto the North Sea, safely clear of the Danish-controlled sound. To build it he imported Dutch engineers, who laid out the new town as they knew best: straight canals, ramparts and drainage, a slice of Holland transplanted onto Nordic granite. Traces of that geometry still shape the old centre, and the Vallgraven — the moat-canal we walked beside all afternoon — is its surviving heart.

Three weeks of monuments behind us; here the only thing left on the itinerary was each other.

The sea remained the city's fortune. In the eighteenth century the Swedish East India Company sailed from these quays to Canton and back with tea, silk and porcelain; later came the shipyards, and Volvo, whose name is woven into local identity. But we came for none of that. We came for a person. The train from Copenhagen ran fast and smooth over the strait, and somewhere on that long bridge Denmark quietly became Sweden. At Gothenburg Central the reunion was loud and immediate — a sister not seen in far too long, her family folded around ours, and the whole point of the detour standing right there on the platform. Everything after that was just the happy business of being together.

ALL FIVE · AT THE HEART
★ The Summer Heart

A heart of flowers

Where Kungsportsavenyn meets the canal, the city had built a giant heart of flowers and greenery for the summer. Posters on the buildings counted down to Way Out West, the festival that fills the parks each August. We stood right in the middle of it, of course, and took the picture — all five of us, grinning.

Lions & long light

An old stone lion keeps watch from its granite plinth, unbothered by the trams sliding past. This is the crossroads of central Gothenburg — cafés, bicycles, the avenue climbing toward Götaplatsen. Ten minutes into the walk it was as if no time had passed at all: the kids already a pack, the grown-ups already mid-conversation.

GUSTAVI DOMKYRKA
★ Gothenburg Cathedral

Stone & copper

Gustavi Domkyrka — the city cathedral — stands at the head of its own gravel esplanade: pale stone, a columned portico, and a green copper dome and clock tower rebuilt in neoclassical calm after the old town's fires. The Dutch grid gives the whole centre this same deliberate, unhurried order.

★ Kungsportsavenyn

Up the Avenyn

Gothenburg's grand tree-lined boulevard runs from the canal up toward the art museum, its ornamental fountains and bronze figures marking the way. We wandered it slowly, in no hurry to be anywhere, and let the afternoon set its own pace.

ACROSS THE RIVER · LINDHOLMEN · DAY 26
The new city
on the far bank.
Where the great shipyards once stood, Hisingen's northern shore has become Lindholmen — a science park and campus of glass, colour and new towers, a short hop across the Göta älv from the old town.
KUGGEN · LINDHOLMEN
★ "The Cog"

Kuggen

The landmark of Lindholmen Science Park: a round tower clad in red, orange, pink and green panels that grow wider as they climb, shading the floors below. It anchors Chalmers University's riverside campus — the research-and-tech heart of modern Gothenburg.

LINDHOLMEN WATERFRONT
NORRA ÄLVSTRANDEN
★ Old Yards, New Towers

Flags along the quay read Älvstranden Utveckling — the city company remaking these old riverbanks. Glass towers now rise where cranes and slipways used to be, a whole new district growing out of the water.

★ A Short Hop

Crossing the river is half the fun — a little ferry or a tram over the water, the old town shrinking behind you, and a completely different Gothenburg waiting on the other side.

THE HARBOUR · DAY 26
The harbour
& the long light.
From the far bank the whole city lays itself out along the Göta älv — quays and cranes, church towers on their hills, and beyond them the beginning of the sea. Then a slow dusk that would not end.
Mon 3 Aug 2026Göta älv · the harbourEvening · long northern light
GÖTA ÄLV · FROM THE NORTH BANK
The working harbour from the northern shore — the wide river, the long quays, a red-brick church tower (Masthuggskyrkan) on its hill to the right, and the tall-masted barque moored downstream.

Gothenburg has always faced the water. The river is still busy — ferries, tour boats, the odd working vessel — and the quays keep their cranes as monuments to the shipbuilding century. By evening the whole harbour turns gentle: a slender steel footbridge over the canal, tour boats dozing at their moorings, and a sky that softens to pink and holds there, because this far north in August the light simply refuses to give up. It was past ten and still not properly dark when we finally turned for home.

NORDSTAN · DAY 26
Nordstan &
the little tram.
Right by the central station sits Nordstan, the big covered shopping street — and in the middle of it, a miniature Gothenburg tram running on its own little rails. The kids did not need asking twice.
Mon 3 Aug 2026Nordstan · central GöteborgFamily afternoon
THE NORDSTAN TRAM
All aboard — a model of the blue-and-white Gothenburg tram, complete with a little Swedish flag, parked in front of a scale model of the Central Station. Waving from the windows, arms around each other: the whole point of the trip, right here.

Nordstan is where the practical business of the day happened — the shelter from a grey sky, the coffee, the browsing — but for the children it was the tram that mattered. They climbed aboard the little carriage, waved through the windows, and hugged on the platform beside the rails while the grown-ups pretended to shop. It is a small, ordinary thing, a coin-op ride in a mall. It is also exactly the kind of unhurried, unplanned moment this leg of the trip was for.

No castle on the list today. Just cousins, a toy tram, and a whole afternoon to spend however we liked.

REFLECTIONS · SWEDEN
Family, fika,
and the light
that wouldn't fade.

Sweden was the only stop on this whole trip with nothing to see and everything to do. No history page to fit in, no museum to pre-book — just family and friends, a gentle loop from the old town to the new city across the river and back, and evenings so long and bright they seemed reluctant to end.

We will remember the summer heart of flowers in the square, the colourful tower on the far bank, the children waving from a toy tram, one small blue magnet held up against a real skyline, and the plain, enormous relief of being back among our own people after three weeks on the road. Tomorrow the long way home begins. Tonight, it can wait.

Favourite moment
The reunion — and then every unhurried hour that followed it, family and friends, no clock running.
The one magnet
The Älvsborg Bridge at dusk. One country, one magnet — the rule holds.
If we come back…
A whole summer of it. The archipelago islands. And Stockholm, next time.
— The Munasinghe-Fernando family
