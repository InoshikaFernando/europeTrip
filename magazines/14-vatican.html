No. 14 · A Letter from the Road
A whole
new world.

We crossed a border this morning almost without noticing. One moment we were in Rome; then we walked through Bernini's great curved colonnade — and we were somewhere else entirely. A different country. The smallest in the world. And it truly felt like a whole new world.

Vatican City is barely half a square kilometre, with around 800 residents — yet it has its own head of state, its own flag, stamps, post office and army (the Swiss Guard), and it is the beating heart of a faith shared by 1.3 billion people. A tiny nation wrapped inside Rome, and a universe of its own.

We spent the morning inside its walls: St Peter's Basilica and the tomb beneath it, the Vatican Museums, and Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel — before crossing back into Rome for the Holy Stairs and the Colosseum at dusk.

This issue is short and dense — like the country itself.

— The Munasinghes, 2026

COVER STORY · SISTINE CHAPEL · DAY 14
A ceiling
painted lying
on his back.
Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling between 1508 and 1512. He hated every minute of it. He preferred sculpture. He had not painted in fresco before. The result is one of the most important artworks in human history.
Wed 22 Jul 2026Sistine Chapel, Vatican Museums45 min · silent contemplation
📷 images/vatican/day-14-sistine-chapel-ceiling.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
The Creation of Adam, central panel of the Sistine Chapel ceiling. Michelangelo, 1511. God's finger nearly touching Adam's.

You queue. You snake through the galleries. You pass tapestries and maps and ceilings and frescoes and statues and reliquaries until your legs hurt and your eyes refuse to focus. Then a guard hisses "silenzio" and you push through a heavy curtain and you are inside the Sistine Chapel. And you forget. Everything. You look up.

I have already a wife who is too much for me, and that is this art, which torments me. — Michelangelo on the Sistine ceiling, 1512.

The ceiling is 40.5 metres long by 14 metres wide. 300+ figures. Nine central scenes from Genesis. Michelangelo refused all assistants for most of the work. He invented his own scaffolding so he could paint while standing, not — as the popular myth says — lying on his back. (It was almost worse: he stood with his head tilted back, paint dripping into his eyes, for four years.) He developed permanent neck and back problems. He wrote a sonnet about how miserable it was making him. Then he finished, and the world has never been the same.

★ Michelangelo's Pietà

Carved 1499, age 24

The most famous sculpture in the basilica. Michelangelo carved it from a single block of Carrara marble when he was 23. The only work he ever signed. It is behind bulletproof glass since a 1972 attack.

The world's smallest country
Vatican City is a nation you can walk across in twenty minutes. At just 0.44 square kilometres and around 800 residents, it is the smallest sovereign state on earth — yet its ruler, the Pope, is the spiritual leader of nearly 1.4 billion Catholics worldwide. For over a thousand years the popes governed the sprawling Papal States across central Italy, until a newly unified Italy seized Rome in 1870 and the popes shut themselves away as self-declared "prisoners in the Vatican."
The stand-off lasted until 1929, when the Lateran Treaty carved out this tiny independent enclave and made the Pope a head of state once more. Its heart is St Peter's Basilica, the largest church in the world, raised between 1506 and 1626 over what tradition holds is the tomb of St Peter himself — the fisherman-apostle and first pope. Even the guards are historic: the Swiss Guard, in their Renaissance stripes, have protected the pontiff since 1506.

CASTEL SANT'ANGELO · DAY 14
Emperor Hadrian's tomb,
now a museum.
Built by Emperor Hadrian as his own mausoleum in 134-139 AD. Later a fortress. Later a papal escape route. Later a prison. Now a museum with the best sunset view of Rome.
Wed 22 Jul 2026Lungotevere Castello90 min · climb to terrace at golden hour
📷 images/vatican/day-14-castel-santangelo.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
Castel Sant'Angelo at golden hour. The 14th-century Bridge of Angels (Ponte Sant'Angelo) in the foreground.

Castel Sant'Angelo is one of Rome's most-changed buildings. It was built by Emperor Hadrian as his and his family's mausoleum, between 134 and 139 AD. After his death, the urns of Hadrian, his wife, his sister, and his successor emperors Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, and Caracalla were placed inside. In the 5th century, the building was converted into a fortress to defend against invading Visigoths. In 590 AD, Pope Gregory I had a vision of the Archangel Michael sheathing his sword atop the building — which is how it got its current name. By the Renaissance, the popes had added a secret escape passage (the Passetto di Borgo) connecting it directly to the Vatican.

The smallest country in the world — by area, by population, and by influence. 1.3 billion Catholics worldwide look to here for guidance.

We climbed all six levels at sunset. The Roman terrace at the top gives you St Peter's dome to one side, the Tiber to another, the whole city of Rome to the south. The kids ran from view to view. Avin pointed at the dome and shouted "We were INSIDE that!" Yes. We were.

REFLECTIONS · VATICAN CITY
One day,
one chapel,
one ceiling that
changed everything.

Vatican Day was the most emotionally dense day of the whole trip. The Sistine Chapel produced a 30-second moment of complete silence in all five of us. The Basilica is so large you cannot get your head around it. The Pietà is so beautiful it makes you angry that someone made it at age 23. The Castel Sant'Angelo terrace at sunset showed us all of Rome from above.

We will not forget the Sistine Chapel ceiling. The kids will not forget it either. Avisha, eleven, said quietly afterwards: "I want to learn how to draw like that." We bought her a sketchbook.

Favourite moment
Sistine Chapel ceiling, the silent 30 seconds before the guard hissed silenzio again.
Hardest moment
Queue management. 4 hours total at the museums. We pre-booked but the lines were still endless.
If we come back…
A Wednesday Papal Audience in St Peter's Square. Caravaggio paintings in Rome's churches.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe
