No. 11 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
chocolate country.

Belgium was a single day. We drove down from Amsterdam with our cousin on a Friday morning — 2 hours via the A4/E19 — and parked in central Brussels by 09:30. We had Grand Place by 10:00, the Royal Palace by 11:30, the MIM by 12:30, Atomium and Mini-Europe by 14:00, moules-frites for lunch, and we were back at cousin's home in Amsterdam by 20:00.

Brussels is the headquarters of the European Union. It is also the chocolate capital of the world — 220,000 tonnes produced per year, more than any other country. It is also home to a tiny 60-centimetre statue of a peeing boy that has been the city's mascot since the 1300s.

This issue is short. Belgium was short. But it was exactly what we needed: a fun day, a manageable city, and a lot of chocolate.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · GRAND PLACE · DAY 23
A square covered
in gold.
Brussels' Grand Place is, by international consensus, one of the most beautiful squares in Europe. UNESCO since 1998. Built up in the 1690s after Louis XIV's artillery flattened it.

The Grand Place at 10:00 — the Hôtel de Ville (left, with its 96-metre Gothic tower), the King's House (back), and the 17th-century guild houses (right) covered in gilded statuary.

The Grand Place is a stage set. On three sides you see ornate baroque guild houses — built between 1696 and 1700 by the city's craft guilds (bakers, brewers, butchers, painters, weavers, etc.) — each one taller, gaudier, and more gilded than the next. On the fourth side stands the Hôtel de Ville, a 15th-century Gothic masterpiece with a 96-metre tower. In the middle, every two years (in even-numbered years), the square is covered with a million flower petals arranged in a giant carpet design. In odd-numbered years, like 2026, it is just stone — but the stone has been worn smooth by 700 years of trading and processions.

The capital of Europe — and the chocolate capital of the world.

A short walk from the square is the Manneken Pis — a 60cm bronze statue of a small boy urinating into a fountain. It has been here since at least 1388 (the current version is from 1619). For reasons no one entirely understands, it is dressed in a different costume almost every day; the statue has its own wardrobe of 1,000+ outfits at the City Museum. On the day we visited, he was dressed as a Belgian firefighter. The kids loved it. So did we.

The accidental capital of a continent
For a city now called the capital of Europe, Brussels arrived at greatness almost by accident. Belgium itself is young — breaking from the Netherlands only in 1830 — a nation split between Dutch-speaking Flemings in the north and French-speakers in the south, with the capital an officially bilingual island inside Flanders. Yet this mid-sized, compromise-shaped city proved ideal neutral ground, and in the postwar decades it gathered the institutions of the European Union and the headquarters of NATO, becoming the improbable nerve centre of a continent.
Its beauty, too, was forged in catastrophe. In 1695 the armies of Louis XIV bombarded the city, reducing the medieval Grand Place to rubble; the guilds rebuilt their houses within a few short years in an exuberant, gilded Baroque that dazzles still. Brussels went on to become a world capital of small, inventive pleasures: pralines and waffles, Art Nouveau façades by Victor Horta, and the comic-strip genius that gave the world Tintin.

★ The Atomium

102m, 1958

Built for the 1958 World Fair. Nine stainless-steel spheres connected by tubes, representing an iron crystal magnified 165 billion times. Original engineering by André Waterkeyn.

CENTRAL BRUSSELS · DAY 23
The summer-only
Royal Palace.
The Royal Palace of Brussels is open to the public only between 22 July and 7 September each year. Free entry. The Mirror Room has a ceiling decorated with 1.4 million beetle wings.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Place des Palais 745 min · throne room + Mirror Room
📷 images/belgium/day-23-royal-palace.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
The Royal Palace of Brussels — the King of the Belgians' official Brussels residence. Open to the public only in summer.

The Royal Palace is technically the seat of the King of the Belgians (King Philippe), although he and his family actually live at Laeken Castle on the outskirts of the city. The palace is open to the public only when the king is away — meaning every summer, between 22 July and 7 September. The throne room, the Empire Room, the Goya Room are all on the tour. But the highlight, for us, was Jan Fabre's 2002 art installation Heaven of Delight in the Mirror Room: a ceiling and chandelier decorated with 1.4 million iridescent green beetle wings.

1.4 million beetle wings on one Belgian ceiling. — Jan Fabre, 2002. Looks like jade.

We then walked to the MIM — the Musical Instruments Museum, housed in a stunning art nouveau building (the Old England building, 1899). The MIM has 1,500 instruments. An audioguide automatically plays each instrument's sound as you approach. The kids LOVED this. We stood in front of a 1750 harpsichord and listened to a Scarlatti sonata; we stood in front of an Indonesian gamelan and heard tribal drumming. Two floors. Two hours. Lunch on the rooftop café with a view of the rooftops.

REFLECTIONS · BELGIUM
A gilded square,
a giant iron crystal,
a million beetle wings.

Belgium in one day was a sprint. We did Grand Place, Manneken Pis, the Royal Palace, the MIM, Atomium, Mini-Europe, and moules-frites for lunch. We were exhausted. We were happy.

The Atomium and Mini-Europe combination was the kid highlight of the day. Belgian frites in a paper cone was the parents' highlight. Belgian chocolate was the universal highlight.

Favourite moment
Aviann counting the buildings at Mini-Europe — "The Eiffel Tower! Big Ben! That's the Atomium itself, here, in tiny!"
Hardest moment
The Sablon chocolate row at 13:00. We could have spent €500 and not been satisfied.
If we come back…
Bruges. Ghent. Antwerp. Three Trappist monasteries. A Tomorrowland weekend.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
