No. 04 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
the Queen of
the Danube.

Until 1873, Budapest was two cities. Buda — old, royal, hilly, fortified — sat on the west bank of the Danube. Pest — flat, busy, trading — sprawled across the east. They could not have been more different. The Chain Bridge, opened in 1849, gave them their first permanent connection. Twenty-four years later, an act of parliament merged them, along with the smaller town of Óbuda, into a single capital.

We drove into the city on a hot Thursday and left the car in Pest, then gave the whole day to our feet: first the great gilded dome of St. Stephen's Basilica, then over the Chain Bridge and up the little funicular to the old royal hill — Matthias Church, the white towers of Fisherman's Bastion, and Buda Castle — before turning, as the light softened, to the river itself.

This issue is about a city that learned to be one thing after spending centuries being two — and is still, somehow, both.

— The Munasinghes, 2026

The Journey · Chapter Four
Down the Danube,
into Hungary.

The story so far: from Beijing's Great Wall and Forbidden City we flew west into Europe — a jet-lagged first week through Munich, the salt mines and lake villages of Austria, two golden days in Prague, and then imperial Vienna, which closed our Austrian chapter.

Now the great river carries us on. Hungary is our fourth chapter — Budapest, the "Queen of the Danube," a single capital stitched together in 1873 from hilly, royal Buda on the west bank and flat, trading Pest on the east. This page rides at the front of every issue: where we've been, where we are, and where we're headed next.

PEST · ST STEPHEN'S BASILICA · DAY 08
A basilica for
the first king.
Szent István Bazilika — named for István, the man who made Hungary Christian in the year 1000. Fifty-four years in the building, and not one metre taller than the Parliament across town.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Szent István tér, PestThe day's first stop
Photo · Munasinghe
The west front from Szent István tér. Across the frieze runs the Latin motto Ego sum via veritas et vita — "I am the way, the truth and the life." Two of ours stood on the star-compass set into the square.

Hungary has one patron above all others: Stephen — István — its first Christian king, crowned on Christmas Day of the year 1000 (or New Year's of 1001, depending which chronicle you trust). He dragged a pagan people of horse-archers into the Christian West, founded bishoprics, and died in 1038; within fifty years the church had made him a saint. This basilica, finished nine centuries later, is his monument, and it is named for no one else.

Building it took from 1851 to 1905 — fifty-four years, three architects, and one disaster: in 1868 the half-built dome collapsed in a storm, and the whole thing had to be taken down and begun again. The finished dome stands exactly 96 metres high — precisely level with the dome of the Parliament. No accident: by an old city rule nothing in Budapest may rise above 96 metres, a number that salutes the year 896, when the Magyar tribes first rode into the Carpathian basin.

Equal in height to the Parliament — the crown and the cross, kept level by law.

Inside, it seats eight and a half thousand; it is the largest church in Budapest and, with the Parliament, one of the two tallest buildings in the city. We came in from the heat of the square, and the children went quiet the way children do under a very large ceiling.

INSIDE · UNDER THE DOME · DAY 08
Look up, and
keep looking up.
Ninety-six metres of gilt, marble and mosaic, rising to a cupola painted by the men who painted half of imperial Budapest.
Thu 16 Jul 2026The crossing & cupolaFifty kinds of marble
Photo · Munasinghe
Straight up into the cupola. The dome mosaics were designed by Károly Lotz — the same hand that decorated the Opera House and the Parliament — and show God the Father surrounded by Hungarian saints.

The inside is a warmer thing than the grey stone front lets on: caramel and gold, with more than fifty kinds of coloured marble on the walls and floor. Light falls from the ring of windows at the base of the dome and pools on the crossing far below. Every surface that isn't marble is painted or gilded, and the eye simply gives up trying to count.

At the far end, on the high altar, stands a white Carrara-marble statue of the king himself, carved by Alajos Stróbl, ringed by bronze reliefs of his life. Around the base of the sanctuary runs a Latin prayer to Stephen: intercede for us with the King of Kings. And we climbed it — a lift for most of the way, then a last spiral of stone stairs out onto the colonnade that rings the dome. Even Avin, all of four years old, made it to the very top, and one of the widest views in Budapest was his reward.

A church the colour of honey, lit from a ring of windows a hundred metres up.

THE HOLY RIGHT · SZENT JOBB · DAY 08
A king's right hand,
a thousand years on.
In a side chapel, behind glass, lies the most sacred relic in Hungary — the mummified right hand of St. Stephen, kept and carried for nearly a millennium.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Chapel of the Holy RightFeast day · 20 August
Photo · Munasinghe
The Szent Jobb in its neo-Gothic reliquary of silver, gold and glass — a miniature cathedral built to hold a single relic: the incorrupt right hand of the founding king.

When Stephen's tomb was opened in 1083, the year of his canonisation, his body had decayed — but his right hand, the story goes, was found whole, still bearing the ring of state. Hungary has kept it ever since. It has not been an easy keeping: through invasions and centuries the relic travelled — to Dalmatia, to Ragusa (today's Dubrovnik) for some three hundred years — before Empress Maria Theresa had it brought home to Buda in 1771.

Today it rests in its own chapel, in a glass-sided reliquary shaped like a little Gothic church. Drop a coin and a light comes on for two minutes so you can see it clearly. Once a year, on 20 August — St. Stephen's Day, Hungary's national holiday — the hand is lifted out and carried through the streets of Budapest at the head of a great procession, exactly as it has been for centuries.

A relic that has outlived the kingdom that made it — and is still carried through the city every August.

BUDA · FISHERMAN'S BASTION · DAY 08
Seven towers,
and the whole city.
Halászbástya looks a thousand years old and is barely a hundred. From its terrace the Danube opens up, and the Parliament stares back across the water like a twin.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Halászbástya, Castle HillUp the funicular, then north
Photo · Munasinghe
Halászbástya from Szentháromság tér: the white cloisters and cone-topped towers Schulek raised by 1902, the bronze St. Stephen at their heart. Each of the seven pointed turrets stands for one of the founding Magyar tribes.

Fisherman's Bastion is the great illusion of Budapest. It has the pale stone, the pointed turrets and the cloistered walks of a medieval castle, and it is younger than the telephone. Frigyes Schulek built it between 1895 and 1902 — not as a fortress but as a viewing terrace, a decorative crown for the hill, raised over the old defensive walls for the country's millennium celebrations.

Its seven tent-roofed towers are not random: one for each of the seven Magyar chieftains who led their tribes into the Carpathian basin in 895. And its name is older than the stone — this stretch of wall was, by tradition, defended in the Middle Ages by the guild of fishermen from the little town below, and the fish market stood at its foot.

A hundred-year-old castle that never had to fight — built only to be looked at, and looked from.

We came up from the funicular and walked north along the hill to reach it. Between the Bastion and Matthias Church next door stands a green bronze king on horseback — St. Stephen again, of course; in this city he is never far away. The children ran the length of the white arcade while we stood at the rail and simply looked: Parliament, river, bridges, and the whole flat sweep of Pest laid out under a moving sky.

FISHERMAN'S BASTION · UNDER THE ARCHES · DAY 08
The photograph
everyone climbs for.
Below the open terrace runs a covered walk of white stone arches — each one framing a turret, a lantern, a slice of the river. It is the most photographed spot on the whole hill, and we went back down to find it.
Thu 16 Jul 2026The lower cloister & stepsThe three of them
Photo · Munasinghe
Avisha, Aviann and Avin under one of the Bastion's arches — a turret and the lantern-hung loggia beyond. The little cameras round their necks are their own: three Munasinghe photographers, all shooting at once.

The open terrace up top gives you the view, but the magic of Fisherman's Bastion is down a level, along the covered walk. Schulek built it as a neo-Romanesque cloister — a run of pale arches and stubby columns, with knights standing in the wall niches and wrought-iron lanterns hanging from the vaults. Look through any arch and it frames something: a cone-roofed tower, a flight of steps, the Danube far below.

A grand ceremonial staircase sweeps down from here toward the Watertown, the old fishermen's quarter that gave the bastion its name. Photographers come at dawn to catch these arches empty and gold; at midday we simply waited our turn, shuffled into the frame, and took it.

Every arch a picture frame — and behind this one, three of ours and a thousand-year-old view.

BUDA · THE MILLENNIUM MONUMENT · DAY 08
The king in bronze,
between the towers.
Between Matthias Church and Fisherman's Bastion sits the man both were built to honour: St. Stephen, crowned and mounted, holding the double cross that still sits on Hungary's shield.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Szentháromság tér, Castle HillAlajos Stróbl · 1906
Photo · Munasinghe
St. Stephen in his coronation robes, raising the apostolic double cross. The Romanesque pedestal, with its four corner lions and relief panels, was designed by Frigyes Schulek — the same architect who built the Bastion around it and restored the church beside it.

Cast in bronze by the sculptor Alajos Stróbl and unveiled in 1906, this is the still centre of Castle Hill — the point the whole terrace is arranged around. Stephen sits crowned and robed, not as a warrior but as a founder, holding aloft the apostolic cross that Pope Sylvester II is said to have granted him. That twin-barred cross went on to become the emblem of the country; you will find it on the Hungarian coat of arms to this day.

Look closely at the pale reliefs around the base and you find scenes from Stephen's life — and one small, sly joke. In one panel the architect Schulek has carved himself, kneeling to offer the king a model of the very Fisherman's Bastion he was building. A thousand-year-old saint, accepting a gift from the man who framed his statue: a signature hidden in plain sight.

A founder, not a fighter — the cross in his hand became the badge of a nation.

The children liked the four stone lions best, one at each corner, keeping their patient guard. We circled the plinth once, then turned to the church.

BUDA · MATTHIAS CHURCH · DAY 08
The church of
the coloured roof.
Seven hundred years of coronations, one long spell as a mosque, and a roof of patterned Zsolnay tiles that looks like embroidery laid over stone.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Szentháromság tér, Castle HillAll five of us
Photo · Munasinghe
All five of us under the Matthias Church — its 80-metre Gothic tower on the left, and the famous diamond-patterned roof of glazed Zsolnay tiles from Pécs on the right. Aviann had her little pink camera on; Avin kept his hat on against the sun.

Everyone calls it Matthias Church, but its real name is the Church of Our Lady, and it is far older than the king who lent it his nickname. A church has stood on this spot since the days of St. Stephen; the Gothic building we see now went up in the 14th century. Its by-name comes from Matthias Corvinus, the great Renaissance king, who was married here — twice — and whose black raven crest still turns up in the stonework.

It has not always been a church. When the Ottomans took Buda in 1541 they stripped out the furnishings, whitewashed the frescoes, and turned it into the city's chief mosque for nearly a century and a half. Only after the Christian reconquest of 1686 did it become a church again. The fairy-tale look it wears today — the tiled roof, the spiky pinnacles — came later still, from Frigyes Schulek's grand restoration in the 1870s–90s (the same architect who gave us the Bastion and the bronze king outside).

A mosque for 145 years, and the setting for the last two kings Hungary ever crowned.

This is coronation ground. Franz Joseph and his empress Elisabeth — the beloved "Sisi" — were crowned King and Queen of Hungary here in 1867, with a Mass that Franz Liszt wrote for the day; the last Habsburg coronation of all took place here in 1916. Inside, every wall is painted top to bottom in Hungarian pattern — but by now the children were hot and hungry, so we saved the inside for a slow look and stepped back into the square.

INSIDE MATTHIAS CHURCH · DAY 08
Every surface,
painted gold.
Step through the door and the grey stone falls away. Inside, there is not a bare wall to be found — the whole church is coloured and gilded like the page of a medieval prayer-book.
Thu 16 Jul 2026The nave & sanctuaryLotz & Székely, 1890s
Photo · Munasinghe
Down the nave to the sanctuary. The walls and vaults are covered in Hungarian, Byzantine and folk-inspired ornament by the painters Károly Lotz and Bertalan Székely — the effect is warmer and stranger than any ordinary Gothic church.

For nearly a century and a half this ceiling looked down on Muslim prayer, not Mass: after 1541 the Ottomans whitewashed every image and used the building as Buda's chief mosque. When Frigyes Schulek restored it in the 1870s–90s he did not simply scrub it back — he had the entire interior repainted, floor to vault, in a dense medieval-Hungarian style that no church had ever quite worn before.

The result is unlike anywhere else in the city. Geometric borders run along every rib; saints and Hungarian kings march across the walls; the floor is a carpet of patterned tile. On a bright afternoon the light comes through the stained glass in long visible shafts and lands on all that gold. The children stopped talking, which is how we always know a place has done its work.

Not restored so much as re-dreamed — a medieval Hungary that never quite existed, painted onto real stone.

MATTHIAS CHURCH · THE ROYAL TOMB · DAY 08
The only king
who lies here.
In a side chapel rests Béla III — a medieval king raised in Constantinople, lost for centuries, and brought home to this church in 1898.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Trinity ChapelBéla III · died 1196
Photo · Munasinghe
The white marble tomb of Béla III and his first queen, Anna of Antioch, beneath a carved Gothic canopy crowned with Christ in Majesty. The Hungarian double-cross shield sits on the front of the sarcophagus.

Béla III ruled from 1172 to 1196, and ruled hugely. Raised at the imperial court of Byzantium, he came home to make medieval Hungary one of the richest and best-run kingdoms in Europe, his revenues rivalling those of the kings of France and England. When he died he was buried, like the kings before him, in the great royal basilica at Székesfehérvár.

That church did not survive the Ottoman wars — it was blown up and quarried away, its royal graves scattered. Béla's tomb, and his queen's, happened to be found intact during excavations in 1848; his are the only medieval Hungarian royal remains ever identified with certainty. In 1898 they were carried here and laid under this canopy — making Matthias Church, almost by accident, the last resting place of a king.

A king who out-earned the kings of France — lost for six centuries, and found by chance.

MATTHIAS CHURCH · THE TREASURY · DAY 08
Crown, cross
and queen.
Up a stair from the nave the church keeps its treasures — the regalia of a kingdom, a goldsmith's cross, and the marble face of the queen the Hungarians loved most.
Why the crown matters
A crown treated as a person
The real Holy Crown — Byzantine and Latin enamels, its cross forever askew — is no ordinary jewel. For a thousand years Hungarians held that the nation's authority rested not in the king but in the Crown itself; a ruler was only lawful once it had touched his head.

BUDA · THE ROYAL PALACE · DAY 08
The palace that
would not stay down.
Buda Castle has been built, wrecked and rebuilt more times than anyone likes to count — Mongols, Ottomans, a world war. Its great green dome is younger than it looks, and the royal family it was built for is long gone.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Budavári Palota, Castle HillHungarian National Gallery
Photo · Munasinghe
All five of us below the palace dome. On the left rears the bronze horse of Prince Eugene of Savoy, the general who broke Ottoman power in Hungary; the banners mark the Hungarian National Gallery, which fills the old royal wings today.

King Béla IV raised the first fortress on this hill in the 1240s, straight after the Mongols had burned the towns below. Over the next two centuries it grew into one of the great courts of Europe — a Gothic and Renaissance palace under Matthias Corvinus, its library and humanist scholars the envy of Italy. Then came the Turks, who held Buda for a century and a half, and the terrible siege of 1686 that won it back and left the palace in ruins.

The Habsburgs rebuilt it grander still; the Second World War flattened it once more, in the brutal siege of 1944–45; and the dome we stood beneath is really a child of the 1950s reconstruction. No royal family has lived here for over a hundred years. Instead the vast wings hold the National Gallery, the Budapest History Museum and the Széchényi Library — and a hill full of visitors, us among them, squinting up at a palace that simply refuses to disappear.

Burned, besieged, bombed and built again — and still crowning the hill.

DUSK ON THE DANUBE · DAY 08
Budapest,
from the water.
A glass-roofed boat, a set of headphones each with the story in our ears, and the whole gold-lit city sliding past on both banks — the gentlest possible end to a long day on our feet.
Thu 16 Jul 2026On the Danube8:30pm · sunset sailing
Photo · Munasinghe
All five of us on the glass-topped boat as the sun went down. The audio-guide headphones named each building as it drifted by; through the windows, the Buda embankment turned to gold.

We had walked all day — the Basilica in the morning, the whole castle hill through the afternoon — and there is no better reward than to sit down and let the city come to you. We boarded around half past eight, as the light began to soften, and the boat slipped out onto the Danube just as the low sun set the western façades alight.

The riverbanks of Budapest are a UNESCO World Heritage site, and from midstream you understand why: palace after grand palace along the water, the great bridges passing overhead one by one, green Margaret Island in the middle of the stream. The children put on their headphones and went quiet, watching the buildings they had stood beneath that morning now glide past from the water — and, as dusk came down, begin one by one to light up.

Buda is the romance, Pest is the bustle — and the river, at last, is the rest.

FROM THE WATER · GELLÉRT HILL · DAY 08
Lady Liberty,
on the hill.
The great dolomite cliff of Gellért Hill rises straight from the Buda bank — a martyred saint's hill, crowned by a statue you can see from half the city, with a 450-year-old Turkish bath at its foot.
Thu 16 Jul 2026Gellért-hegy, from the boatSunset sailing
Photo · Munasinghe
Gellért Hill from midstream: the Liberty Statue high on the summit, the Hungarian flag beside it, and — tucked against the rock at the water's edge — the domed Rudas Baths, in business since the 1500s.

The hill is named for St. Gellért, an Italian bishop who helped King Stephen convert Hungary a thousand years ago — and who, by tradition, was martyred here by pagans who sealed him in a barrel and rolled him off this very cliff into the river. On the summit stands the Liberty Statue, a fourteen-metre bronze woman holding a palm frond above her head. Raised in 1947, she has watched over the city ever since, floodlit white against the night sky.

At the hill's foot, right at the waterline, sits one of Budapest's oldest treasures: the Rudas Baths, a Turkish bathhouse built in the 1550s during the Ottoman occupation, its octagonal pool still roofed by the original stone dome. The children, headphones on, watched it all slide past — saint, statue and steam — as the boat carried us down the darkening river.

A saint's hill, a bronze lady, and a Turkish bath still warm after five hundred years.

DUSK ON THE DANUBE · AFTER DARK · DAY 08
And then the
lights came on.
As the last of the sun went, the city changed clothes — the Parliament turned to gold, the bridges strung themselves with lamps, and the whole riverfront became a panorama in the dark.
The day, by the numbers
Twenty-one thousand steps, and one perfect ending
By the time the boat turned for home we had walked over 21,000 steps — the Basilica dome at one end of the day, the whole of Castle Hill in the middle, church after church and tower after tower. And it all closed the gentlest way a day can: sitting down, drifting past a city turned to gold, five pairs of tired feet and nobody wanting it to end.

TASTES OF HUNGARY · DAY 08
Lángos, three ways.
Before the boat, dinner was lángos — Hungary's great deep-fried flatbread — one savoury and cheesy, one piled up like a pizza, and one (for pudding) smothered in chocolate.
What is lángos?
Fried dough, straight from the flame
Lángos is Hungary's favourite street food — a plate-sized round of deep-fried dough, sold hot at every market and festival. The name comes from láng, "flame": it was once baked in the flames at the mouth of the wood oven. The classic is rubbed with garlic and heaped with sour cream and cheese; the pizza and chocolate versions are modern additions the children heartily approved of.

REFLECTIONS · HUNGARY
A basilica, a bastion,
a hill full of kings.

Budapest gives you two cities for the price of one. We spent the morning in flat, busy Pest, under the dome of St. Stephen's; we spent the afternoon on the royal hill of Buda, among churches and white towers and a palace that has been knocked down and raised up more times than we could keep straight. Between them ran the Danube, and every bridge across it a little celebration.

It was hot, and there was a great deal of walking, and a stroller to haul up a great many steps — but the children carried their own small cameras and photographed everything, and nobody complained for long. It was slower than Vienna, gentler than Prague. And tonight, as the light softens, we turn to the river itself, to see the whole city from the water.

Favourite moment
Avin, four years old, climbing every last step to the top of St. Stephen's dome — and the arches of Fisherman's Bastion.
Hardest moment
The midday heat on Castle Hill, and hauling the stroller up flight after flight of Bastion steps.
If we come back…
The Széchenyi thermal baths we ran out of time for, the Parliament from the inside, and a slow day on Margaret Island.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe
