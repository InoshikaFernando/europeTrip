No. 09 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
two Frances.
Day 20 · Tue 28 Jul 2026 · Landed TLS
Wheels down — all five of us on the tarmac at Toulouse-Blagnac, blinking into the southern sun. The France leg begins here.

France was a country of contrasts. The first was Lourdes — a small Pyrenean town that becomes, every summer, one of the world's largest Catholic pilgrimage sites. The second was Paris — the loudest, most photographed, most romantic city on earth.

In Lourdes we did the Torch Procession at the Sanctuary, where thousands of pilgrims carry candles around the Esplanade as the night settles. In Paris we climbed the Eiffel Tower at 22:30 on a Wednesday, with the sparkle show happening below us at 22:00 and the city stretching to every horizon.

Two cities. Two completely different experiences of France. Both unforgettable.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

ALL FIVE · GOLDEN HOUR
The A64 south and the first sight of the Pyrenees · Aviann — our Lourdes — on the Esplanade · and all five of us before the Basilica of the Immaculate Conception at golden hour, candles ready for the Torch Procession.

In 1858 a fourteen-year-old peasant girl named Bernadette Soubirous reported eighteen apparitions of the Virgin Mary in a small grotto by the Gave de Pau river. The Catholic Church investigated for four years and declared the visions authentic. Lourdes was transformed. Today the Sanctuary covers 51 hectares. Six million pilgrims come every year. They come for the Grotto, the spring water Bernadette dug up by hand, the underground Basilica of St Pius X (50,000 capacity), and the Torch Procession.

"I am the Immaculate Conception." — The lady's words to Bernadette, 25 March 1858.

We arrived at 20:45 and bought candles for the children. The procession started in light rain. Within ten minutes thousands of people were moving together around the Esplanade. The Ave Maria was sung in French, then Italian, then English, then Polish, then Spanish, then Portuguese, then Tagalog, then more languages we did not recognise. Avin held his candle in both hands the entire time. He did not say a single word.

How a shy miller's daughter summoned the world
In February 1858 a fourteen-year-old miller's daughter, Bernadette Soubirous, went gathering firewood beside the river Gave and saw, in a rocky grotto at Massabielle, a lady in white who returned eighteen times. The apparitions — which the girl said named herself the Immaculate Conception — drew a scandalised town and a wary Church. Yet within four years Rome had recognised the visions, and the muddy spring Bernadette scratched from the ground made this modest Pyrenean market town second only to Paris in French hotel beds.
Today three to six million pilgrims arrive each year, many of them sick or dying, to drink or bathe in the spring water and join the candlelit Marian procession that flows through the sanctuary each evening. The Church stays strikingly cautious about the healings it is famous for: of thousands of reported cures, only around 70 have been formally declared miraculous after exhaustive medical scrutiny — and that restraint is the source of its authority.

The city that was rebuilt to be seen
The Paris of postcards — those endless honey-stone façades and radiating boulevards — is barely older than the camera. Between 1853 and 1870 Napoleon III unleashed Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann on the medieval city, driving wide, straight avenues through a warren of crooked lanes and imposing uniform heights, grand vistas, parks and sewers. It was as much social engineering as beautification — yet it gave the capital the theatrical coherence we now read as timeless.
Its most beloved landmark was meant to be temporary. Gustave Eiffel's iron tower rose for the 1889 World's Fair marking the Revolution's centenary, and a chorus of writers denounced it as a monstrous, useless skeleton. Scheduled for demolition after twenty years, it was saved by its usefulness as a radio antenna. The older nickname — the "City of Light" — nods both to Paris's early street lighting and to its glow as the capital of Enlightenment thought.

SEEN AT THE LOUVRE · DAY 21
Why is the
cross forked?
A small mystery from the Denon wing — one of the kids spotted it first.
CHRIST CARRYING THE CROSS · c.1500

Among the paintings we passed, one Christ Carrying the Cross stopped us. Christ's cross is not the neat, carpentered cross of most pictures — it is a rough, Y-shaped tree-trunk.

It's deliberate. Painters called this a crux furcata — a "forked cross," or in German a Gabelkreuz — a form that was popular in 14th–16th-century German and Netherlandish religious art.

The fork carries the meaning. The cross is shown as a living, branching tree, casting it as the Tree of Life — the "new tree" that undoes the Fall in Eden. The raw, unhewn timber also nods to realism: the condemned hauling an unfinished beam to the hill.

So the strange shape isn't a mistake — it's a whole theology, hidden in the wood. (A Northern-European Passion panel of around 1500; the Louvre wall label carries the full attribution.)

SEEN AT THE LOUVRE · DAY 21
Why is Egypt
in the Louvre?
The kids wanted to know why a Paris palace is full of sphinxes and mummies.

It starts with Napoleon. In 1798 he invaded Egypt and, unusually, brought some 150 scholars, artists and scientists along with the army. They measured and drew everything, publishing the vast Description de l'Égypte — and set off a wave of "Egyptomania" across France.

Then a Frenchman cracked the code. Jean-François Champollion deciphered hieroglyphics in 1822, using the Rosetta Stone found during Napoleon's campaign. He all but invented Egyptology — and in 1826 the king made him the first curator of a brand-new Egyptian department at the Louvre, which he founded and filled.

The objects came by purchase (the Salt and Drovetti collections), by excavation, and through the antiquities trade of the day. And the deeper reason is the Louvre's very idea: a "universal museum" — like the British Museum — built to hold the art of every civilisation. Egypt is one of its eight departments.

★ The stars to find
  • 🦁 The Great Sphinx of Tanis — you can walk right up to it
  • ✍️ The Seated Scribe — 4,500-year-old painted eyes
  • ⚰️ The mummies & painted sarcophagi
A footnote worth keeping: much of this was collected in the colonial era, and there are real, ongoing debates about how such antiquities were acquired — and whether some should one day return to Egypt.

The Eiffel Tower's 22:00 sparkle show — 20,000 lights, 5 minutes, every hour after dark from 22:00 to 01:00.

You should not climb the Eiffel Tower at noon. The queues are 90 minutes and the city below is white-hot and hazy. You should climb it at 22:30. That is what we did. We watched the 22:00 sparkle from across the river at Trocadéro — 20,000 lights flashing for five minutes — and then walked down to the base and rode the lift up. The city was cool. The crowd was thin. The view stretched from Sacré-Cœur in the north to Notre-Dame in the centre to the gold dome of Les Invalides directly below us.

Paris is always a good idea. — Audrey Hepburn

Avin was four years old, awake at 23:00, watching Paris from the second floor of the Eiffel Tower. We will hold this memory carefully. The tickets were €65 for all five (2 adults €23.50 + 3 children €6.00). The lift went up smoothly. The wind on the platform was cold even in July. The kids leaned over the rail and pointed at the lights — "That one's the Louvre!" "That one's Sacré-Cœur!" — and we let them stay up there as long as they wanted.

REFLECTIONS · FRANCE
A candle in Lourdes,
a lift up the Eiffel,
two ends of France.

France is two countries layered on the same map. The southern pilgrim France of Lourdes — quiet, devout, intimate. The northern capital France of Paris — loud, electric, monumental. We did the long route between them on a TGV, eating a baguette in Coach 12.

The Eiffel Tower at midnight is something we will tell the children about for the rest of their lives. We held them up to the railing. They pointed out the lights of the city. Then we rode the lift back down at 23:30 and Métro'd home, three sleepy children leaning against us.

Favourite moment
Avin's face on the Eiffel's second floor at 22:50. He could see the whole city below him.
Hardest moment
The Lourdes procession ended in rain. We walked back to the hotel soaking wet.
If we come back…
A week in Paris. The Loire châteaux. Brittany. Provence in lavender season.
