No. 07 · Welcome
Welcome to
the country
that didn't fight.

Switzerland is small — you can drive its width in about three hours — yet its mountains are the steepest in Europe, its trains the most punctual in the world, and its chocolate the most eaten, per person, on earth. We gave it three nights, all of them in alpine villages: one high up the Mattertal at Stalden, near car-free Zermatt, and two at Moléson-sur-Gruyères in the green heart of cheese country.

In 1291 three small cantons met in a meadow and swore a defensive pact. That pact grew into the Swiss Confederation — today twenty-six cantons, four official languages, and a nation that has not fought a war since 1815. Neutrality here is not timidity; it is a seven-hundred-year strategy, and you feel its calm everywhere.

This issue is what three days bought us: the Matterhorn seen from the Gornergrat railway at 3,089 m; the Maison Cailler chocolate factory at Broc; the copper vats and ageing cellars of La Maison du Gruyère; and a whole lakeside day in Geneva, the world's meeting place.

COVER STORY · GORNERGRAT
The Matterhorn
from above.
A history written in rock and rope

For centuries the Matterhorn — 4,478 metres of near-vertical rock on the Swiss–Italian border — was thought unclimbable, a peak the valley people believed was guarded by spirits. Then, on 14 July 1865, an English illustrator named Edward Whymper and six companions proved otherwise, reaching the summit after years of failed attempts. Their triumph lasted barely an hour: on the descent a single rope snapped, and four of the seven fell more than a thousand metres to their deaths. The disaster shook Victorian Europe — and turned the Matterhorn, overnight, into the birthplace of modern mountaineering.

Thirty-three years later, in 1898, Switzerland answered the mountain in its own way. The Gornergrat Railway — the country's first fully electric cog railway, and still the highest open-air line in Europe — began carrying travellers from car-free Zermatt up to a bare ridge at 3,089 metres. The climb that once cost lives now takes thirty-three minutes and a train ticket. Even the chocolate in your bag is a tribute: the Toblerone bar, created in Bern in 1908, borrowed its unmistakable triangular shape from this very peak.

We rode that same 1898 railway to the top on our first full Swiss morning — and found the mountain everyone comes for standing clear against a cloudless July sky.

Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The Matterhorn from Gornergrat — 4,478 metres of perfectly triangular rock, clean against a blue July sky. First summited on 14 July 1865.

Zermatt is car-free; you leave the road behind at the valley station and switch to electric taxis or your own feet. The Gornergrat train leaves from a building right beside the main line and climbs 1,500 vertical metres by cogwheel, the Matterhorn swinging into view almost at once and never leaving it. By the summit at 3,089 metres you are no longer looking up at the mountain — you are looking across at it, and a little down.

Neutral since 1815, and wealthy since long before — the country that turned an unclimbable peak into a thirty-three-minute train ride.

We had faultless weather. From the observation platform we counted off the giants: the Monte Rosa, Switzerland's tallest; the Lyskamm; the twin summits of Castor and Pollux; the broad white dome of the Breithorn — twenty-nine peaks above 4,000 metres in a single sweep. We walked the ridge for the better part of an hour, and Avin, four years old, found a patch of summer snow and threw snowballs at his sisters in the middle of July.

MOLÉSON-SUR-GRUYÈRES · OUR BASE
A village
that smells of cheese.
Nine hundred years of Le Gruyère

The green, cow-cropped hills around Gruyères have been turning milk into cheese since at least 1115, when the monks and herders of these valleys first pressed the hard, nutty wheels that the world now knows as Le Gruyère AOP. The recipe has barely changed: raw morning milk, a copper cauldron, rennet, and long months ageing in a cool cellar. The whole district — pastures, chalets, the medieval hill-town of Gruyères itself — grew up around that single craft, and to this day the air here carries the faint, unmistakable sweetness of it.

Our first Swiss night was high in the Mattertal at Stalden, near Zermatt. Then we crossed the country to Moléson — a quiet mountain village that became our base for the chocolate, the cheese and the lake.
24–26 Jul 2026Moléson-sur-GruyèresLa Pierre à Catillon · 2 nights

We stayed at La Pierre à Catillon, in the little alpine village of Moléson-sur-Gruyères — red-shuttered chalets, wooden balconies, and a view straight down the green valley toward the medieval town of Gruyères. It made a perfect base: close to Maison Cailler and to Geneva, and quiet enough to sleep deeply after long days on the road. And from our room, with the window open, we could hear the cowbells ringing across the pasture — proof, if any were needed, that this really is the country of cheese.

From our hotel window, cowbells across the pasture — the sound Switzerland makes when no one is trying to sell you anything.

And here we did reach the cheese. At La Maison du Gruyère, down at Pringy, we walked past the great copper vats and into cellars stacked to the ceiling with thousands of ageing wheels, the whole history of Le Gruyère laid out around us. The one thing we missed was the live morning cheese-making — the cheesemaker stirring some 800 litres of milk over the fire — so that single scene stays saved for next time. A good reason, we decided, to come back to Gruyère.

Heidi — the other Swiss childhood story

If Gruyère is the taste of the Swiss Alps, Heidi is their story. Written by the Swiss author Johanna Spyri in 1881, the tale of a small orphan sent to live with her gruff grandfather on a high alpine pasture — with her goatherd friend Peter, fresh goat's milk, and cheese toasted over the fire — became one of the best-loved children's books ever written, and the world's enduring image of Switzerland: chalets, cowbells and mountain freedom. It is why you'll find Heidi on magnets and chocolate in every Swiss shop, even here in cheese country.

The book is set not in Gruyère but in the east, above Maienfeld in the canton of Graubünden — a region that now proudly calls itself "Heidiland," with a recreated Heidi's Village to visit. It is the same alpine-dairy world all the same: cattle summered on high meadows, the milk, the cheese. It was a story we loved as children — so if we ever come back to Switzerland, Heidi's village is right at the top of the list.

MAISON CAILLER · BROC
A sweet
empire.
From the Aztecs to a Swiss factory floor

Chocolate did not begin in Switzerland. It began with the Aztecs of Central America, who drank cacao bitter, thick and spiced. When Spanish conquistadors reached the New World in the 1500s the beans crossed the ocean to the court of Emperor Charles V — the Habsburg ruler of Spain, master of a realm so vast it was said the sun never set on it — where the bitter drink was sweetened with sugar and kept, for almost a century, a guarded Spanish luxury.

From Spain it spread to France through royal marriages, and by the reign of Louis XIV drinking chocolate was the fashion of Versailles. Yet it stayed a drink for aristocrats — until Switzerland changed everything. François-Louis Cailler opened his chocolate works in 1819, and his son-in-law, Daniel Peter, blended in Nestlé's condensed milk in 1875 to invent milk chocolate. The bean that once belonged to emperors was, at last, for everyone.

Which is how five very happy tasters came to spend an afternoon in Broc — at Cailler, the oldest Swiss chocolate brand still going, founded in 1819.

Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
All five of us at Maison Cailler, Broc — founded in 1819, the oldest Swiss chocolate brand still going, in its grand factory building of 1898.

The tour walks you backward through that whole story — from the pods and beans, past the roasting and conching, to a gallery of the cauldrons and machines that once did the work by hand — and then, at the end, into the tasting room, where the children discovered that "self-guided" is Swiss for "eat as much as you like." We are fairly sure Avin set a personal record.

Two emperors, one name: Charles V brought chocolate to Spain in the 1500s — not to be confused with Charles IV, who built the bridge we crossed in Prague 150 years earlier.

It was the perfect close to a day that had begun at 3,089 metres. And it belonged, more than to any of us, to a certain four-year-old.

A chocolate-shaped day · for Avin
The whole day was bookended by chocolate. We began it at 3,089 metres, holding a giant Toblerone up against the real Matterhorn — the very peak that gave the bar its triangular shape — and we ended it here at Maison Cailler, tasting our way through the factory. For Avin, our four-year-old and the family's devoted chocolate fan, no better day has ever been invented: a Toblerone on the mountain in the morning, and a chocolate factory before dinner.

GENEVA · SUN 26 JUL
The world's
meeting place.
The city that reinvented itself twice

In the 1500s Geneva became the capital of the Protestant Reformation. The French theologian John Calvin arrived in 1536 and remade the city into a strict, self-governing "Protestant Rome" — a refuge for reformers fleeing Catholic Europe and the launch-pad for Calvinism, which would reach Scotland, the Netherlands and, eventually, America. The plain, whitewashed Cathédrale Saint-Pierre — stripped of its statues and gold — is Calvin's church still.

Three centuries later Geneva reinvented itself again, as the world's humanitarian capital. In 1863 a Genevan, Henry Dunant, founded the Red Cross here (its emblem is simply the Swiss flag reversed), and the first Geneva Convention followed in 1864. When the League of Nations chose the city after the First World War, and the United Nations kept its European seat here after the Second, the town that once exported a stern faith became the place where nearly 200 nations meet to keep the peace.

Lakeside and French-speaking, Geneva was our last Swiss day — a whole day on foot and by train, from the flags of the UN to the Jet d'Eau and the cobbles of the Old Town.

The avenue of flags leading to the Palais des Nations — the UN's European home, where nearly 200 nations fly side by side.

Calvin's stern city is long gone; the Geneva we walked is the gentler, later one. We climbed the great avenue of flags to the Palais des Nations, then stood beneath the Broken Chair — a twelve-metre monument balanced on three and a bit legs, a protest against landmines. The children loved that one leg is deliberately smashed off, and asked, reasonably, why no one had fixed it.

Nearly 200 flags, one lawn. Geneva has been the world's meeting room since the Red Cross was founded here in 1863.

Then down to the water: the Jet d'Eau firing 140 metres into the sky, the Flower Clock ticking in the Jardin Anglais, and the cobbled Old Town climbing to the Cathédrale Saint-Pierre. It was a fitting last Swiss day. Tomorrow we point the car north — a stop in medieval Bern, then across the border into Bavaria.

All five at the Palais des Nations gate; the Jet d'Eau over the lake; the family and the boys under the Broken Chair.

The Brunswick Monument

A neo-Gothic mausoleum built in 1879 for Charles II, Duke of Brunswick, who died in Geneva in 1873. He left his entire fortune — around 24 million gold francs — to the city, on the single condition that it build him a grand tomb. Geneva agreed, and the money also helped fund landmarks such as the Grand Théâtre.

REFLECTIONS · SWITZERLAND
Mountains, milk,
and 700 years
of not picking sides.

Switzerland is what happens when geography — impossible mountains — meets policy — neutral since 1815. The result is a country that has kept its old towns, its four languages and its punctual trains, and turned even cheese and chocolate into quiet empires. The villages are small. The peaks are enormous. And a giant Toblerone, it turns out, lines up exactly with the mountain that inspired it.

We did this country in barely four days, all of them among the alps. We could not have done it in fewer — and we could happily have given it forty.

Favourite moment
The Matterhorn holding steady in a cloudless sky as we crested the ridge at Gornergrat — and Avin, at 3,089 m, with chocolate in both hands.
What we missed
The live cheese-making at La Maison du Gruyère — the cheesemaker stirring 800 litres over the fire. Saved, deliberately, for next time.
If we come back…
Heidi's village above Maienfeld, and the Glacier Express end to end — Lauterbrunnen, Mürren, the Jungfraujoch.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
