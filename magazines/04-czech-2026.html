A letter from the road

Who we are, why we travel

Two parents, three children, and one shared dream.

We're the Munasinghe-Fernandos - two parents and three children who love to see the world together. Avi and I share one dream: to travel to as many countries as we possibly can, as a family, while the children are still small enough to want to come with us.

It's never easy. Booking flights and trains and rooms for five is its own small adventure, and we know we can't reach everywhere. But from every country we do get to, we try our very best to see as much as we can - and to hold on to it properly afterwards. This magazine is where we keep all of it.

So these pages aren't a guidebook. They're ours: what we actually saw, what went wrong, what the children noticed, and the small moments we never want to forget. Told, this time, in my words.

- Inoshi, for all five of us · 2026

A family memoir - told in Inoshi's words

Two Czech cities,
and a thousand years the war spared.

We crossed our first European land border at dawn - out of a hilltop Austrian castle and north into Bohemia - and gave Prague two days before turning south for Vienna by the longer road, the one that runs through Brno. Prague was the city of a hundred spires: a castle above the river, a bridge full of saints, a clock six centuries old. Brno was something quieter - a detour made for one of us alone. Our best hours came early, before the crowds woke. This is what actually happened.

Chapter One · Prague Castle

The hill that outlived everything

A thousand years of Bohemian kings - and, to our surprise, it was the cathedrals, not the fortress, that took the words out of us.

The third morning had become a rhythm by now: awake at five, all of us, the children as bright as the adults, down to that enormous castle breakfast one last time, and then the bags hauled back down the lift-less stairs and into the car. We said goodbye to Burg Altpernstein, pointed north, and watched Austria fall away behind us - lakes and salt villages and Sound of Music hills - until the road tipped us over a border and into Bohemia.

We reached Prague from Austria at around four in the afternoon, and we could not wait: we dropped the bags and went straight out to roam. But the city had a surprise waiting. It was unexpectedly, fiercely hot - the old cobbles and stone walls had been soaking up the sun all day and now gave the heat straight back at us - and the three of them wilted fast. So we gave in and did the sensible thing: we climbed aboard a sightseeing bus and let it carry us round the city while we caught our breath. (A thing we worked out the hard way, and pass on: in Prague you buy your tram ticket on board the tram itself - and, to our delight, the children travelled free.)

Prague Castle is, on paper, the largest coherent castle complex in the world - some seventy thousand square metres of palaces and churches founded around the year 880, and still, a thousand years on, the seat of the Czech head of state. We walked its courtyards and stepped into its great churches. And here was the surprise none of us saw coming: for all the fortress's fame, it was the churches that stopped us - grander than the walls that hold them, the true crown of the hill.

First, next door to the cathedral, the older and plainer of the two: St George's Basilica. Through a baroque door and back a thousand years - one long Romanesque room of honey-coloured stone, round arches marching two by two toward the raised choir. After the grandeur still to come, it was the quiet of it that the children liked.

And here was the thread we would keep pulling all day. Under glass in the basilica lies St Ludmila - the first female saint of the Czech lands, murdered, the story goes, in 921 with her own veil. She was grandmother to the boy-king Wenceslas, and within the hour we would meet the two of them again, this time in a blaze of coloured glass in the cathedral next door.

And then St Vitus, and the day quietly ran out of words. The cathedral was begun in 1344 on the order of Charles IV and not finished until 1929 - nearly six hundred years written into one building - and stepping inside, you lose the top of it. Pillars rise higher than seems possible into a ribbed stone vault that runs off into shadow, and the eye is pulled the whole length of the nave to a rose window more than ten metres wide, throwing pools of red and blue and violet onto the gleaming ranks of the organ below. This, we all decided, was the most beautiful room in Prague. It is where the children went quiet.

The pillars rise into the dark, the whole west wall turns to coloured light, and three restless children forget, for a minute, to fidget.
Stone, not paint

What soars overhead here is a Gothic rib vault - the ceiling held up, and made beautiful, by stone itself. The pointed arch and the ribbed vault were a twelfth-century French invention that let cathedrals climb far higher and lighter than Rome had ever built; the masons of Prague, led by the young Peter Parler, turned that engineering into stone lace. It is the north's great answer to a very different southern art - for a ceiling that is a painting, a whole sky opened overhead in fresco, no one has ever matched Italy.

Among all the medieval leaded glass hangs one window that glows differently. Alfons Mucha - Bohemia's most famous painter, the master whose posters defined Art Nouveau in Paris - painted his design straight onto the glass in 1931. At its heart the boy St Wenceslas kneels with his grandmother St Ludmila; around them swirl the brothers Cyril and Methodius, who brought the Slavs their first alphabet. It is the only stained-glass window Mucha ever made.

Then the silver. In a side of the cathedral stands the tomb of St John of Nepomuk - close to two tonnes of worked silver, angels bearing up a great red-and-silver canopy, the saint kneeling on top with the martyr's cross in his arms. He was a priest thrown from the Charles Bridge into the Vltava in 1393, on the order of the king. We stood and marvelled, and had no idea we would be standing on the very spot the story began the next morning.

For all the silver and coloured glass, some of the quietest things in St Vitus are the dark ones. This bronze group stands out in the open beneath one of the great arches, three figures worked in metal gone almost black with age, so that against the honey-pale stone they seemed to glow from the inside. The children circled it slowly - the way you do when you are not quite sure the figures won't move - before we stepped back out, blinking, into the afternoon.

Beyond the churches lie the government rooms - the Old Royal Palace, where for centuries a whole kingdom's memory was kept in painted books and painted shields. We found the Land Rolls, the Desky zemské: leather volumes that held Bohemia's laws and lawsuits, each spine marked with a number and a bright little badge so a clerk could pull the right one down again. A medieval filing system, and a beautiful one.

And so to the throne itself: a slim gold Gothic chair under a fall of dark velvet, guarded by the silver double-tailed lion that is the badge of Bohemia, with benches of deep red for a court that has not sat for more than a century. No king has been crowned here in over a hundred years, and the room stands quiet now behind its ropes - but for a thousand years this hill had been the seat of kings, and here, at last, was the seat.

Beside the throne, behind glass, blazed the coronation regalia - the Crown of St Wenceslas, made for Charles IV in 1347 and thick with rubies and old blue sapphires. You cannot simply walk in and see the real one: it lives behind a door with seven locks, whose seven keys are held by seven different people - the President and the Prime Minister and the Archbishop of Prague among them - and is shown perhaps once in a decade. Three small noses pressed to the glass all the same.

By the time we came down off the hill, everyone was hungry - and we never did sit down to a proper Czech feast. We grazed instead, on our feet, on the long walk down: one trdelník and one spiral potato passed hand to hand until there was nothing left but sticky fingers and sugar round three small mouths.

What we were really eating

The trdelník is sold on every Prague corner as "old Bohemian," but it really belongs to the Transylvanian kürtőskalács - a ribbon of dough wound round a spit, grilled and rolled in cinnamon sugar; the ice-cream stuffed inside is a modern tourist flourish. The spiral potato travelled further still: Korean street food, now somehow a fixture of the Prague lanes. We ate both, happily, and asked no questions until later.

  • St Vitus Begun in 1344, finished in 1929 - nearly six centuries in one building.
  • The crown The Crown of St Wenceslas, 1347, kept behind a door with seven locks.
  • Us A whole afternoon on tired legs, rescued by a little train - and silenced by the cathedral.
Chapter Two · Charles Bridge

The bridge before the crowds wake

Prague's most famous crossing, 515 metres of baroque saints - and for one early hour, very nearly ours alone.

The secret to the Charles Bridge is simple: go early. By mid-morning its 515 metres run shoulder to shoulder; but leave before the day fills up and it becomes a different place entirely. We left the apartment at eight and were out on the bridge by a quarter past - and to our delight, at that hour it was almost empty.

The most famous crossing in Prague, near-deserted: just us, the thirty baroque saints who have guarded the parapets since the 1700s, the mist lifting off the Vltava, and the towers at each end catching the first real light. The bridge has stood since 1357. We walked its full length across to the Malá Strana side and back, and by the time we turned toward the Old Town the first tour groups were only arriving. We had beaten them by an hour, and it was worth every minute of the early start.

Halfway across we stopped at the one saint everyone comes to find. The day before, up in St Vitus, we had stood beside his glittering silver tomb; now here was the very spot the story began - a brass cross and a worn relief set into the parapet, marking where St John of Nepomuk was thrown into the river in 1393, polished bright by centuries of hands. Rub the brass, they say, and one day Prague will call you home again. So five hands went onto the warm brass, and one shared wish: that Prague would not be a place we saw only once. Above it the grille has filled, lock by lock, with other people's promises.

The city war forgot

Ask why Prague dazzles where Dresden, Warsaw and Berlin were reduced to rubble, and the answer is a rare stroke of historical luck: it escaped both the systematic bombing and the final scorched-earth battle that flattened so much of Central Europe. The result is a thousand years of architecture standing shoulder to shoulder - Romanesque cellars beneath Gothic spires, baroque palaces beside swirling Art Nouveau fronts. Much of that grandeur is owed to Charles IV, the Bohemian king and Holy Roman Emperor who made Prague his imperial capital in the 1300s, founded Central Europe's first university, and laid the stone bridge that still bears his name.

Charles Bridge · at a glance
  • Built Begun in 1357 under Charles IV - 515 metres, lined with thirty baroque saints.
  • Spared Prague's old core escaped the bombing that flattened Dresden and Warsaw.
  • Legend Rub the brass where Nepomuk fell in 1393, and one day you will return.
  • Us On the bridge by 8:15, an hour ahead of the crowds - and five hands on the brass.
Chapter Three · The Old Town

A city that looks like King's Landing

A clock that has walked on the hour since 1410, a square too majestic to photograph, and lanes you don't march through but drift.

From the bridge we walked back into the Old Town Square for the thing everyone comes to see: the Astronomical Clock - the Orloj - which has hung on the wall of the Old Town Hall since 1410, making it the oldest clock of its kind still working anywhere on earth.

Getting all five of us into one frame is its own small expedition - someone is always blinking, or wandering off. But here, in the crush of the square with the great tower rising behind, we managed it: Avinesh on the stick, Avisha in his shades, Aviann laughing under her hat, Avin just tall enough to sneak into the corner, and the six-hundred-year-old clock glowing over our shoulders. The tower behind is older still, begun in 1364; you can climb it for the view, but the show on the hour and a photograph together were enough for us.

It is really three instruments in one: a clock for the hour, a golden calendar dial that turns once a year through the signs of the zodiac, and an astronomical dial that maps the sun and moon against the heavens. We joined the crowd on the hour and watched the show - two small windows opening, the twelve Apostles parading across in turn while beside the dial a skeleton, Death himself, tolled his bell. A one-minute performance that has drawn this square together for six centuries; when it ended, everyone looked up at the same moment, and smiled.

Six hundred years of clockwork, and still, every hour on the hour, the Apostles walk and the whole square looks up at once.

There is a word for Prague's Old Town, and the word is majestic. Standing in the great square, ringed by the twin Gothic spires of the Týn Church and the pastel palaces, I kept thinking of King's Landing - the capital from Game of Thrones - because that is exactly what Prague looks like: a fantasy city made real, all towers and turrets and red roofs climbing the hills. Except Prague is not a film set. It has stood, and been loved, and survived, for a thousand years.

We tried, and failed, to capture it - every photograph came back smaller than the thing itself. You have to stand in the middle of it, turn slowly, and let the spires close in around you. And there is a second kind of grandeur here: the Old Town streets are lined with every luxury name you could think of, the shopfronts as polished and stately as the palaces above them - and, tucked among them, the Czech shops we came to love, like Manufaktura with its window full of beer cosmetics and honey soap. Every lane is cobbled; at the end of almost every one, a pair of green baroque domes seems to be waiting for you. There are cities you march through and cities you drift through. Prague, we decided, is one to drift.

Why the lanes run so narrow

Not for the heat - that trick belongs to far hotter cities. Prague's Old Town was simply never planned: it grew lane by lane through the Middle Ages, following old market paths and the tight property lines of a walled town where every foot of land was dear. Stranger still, the ground itself was raised - in the thirteenth century, to escape the Vltava's floods, the townsfolk lifted the streets several metres, burying the Romanesque houses whose ground floors survive today as cellars. The medieval city, quite literally, lies beneath your feet.

The Old Town · at a glance
  • The Orloj The oldest astronomical clock still working anywhere - ticking since 1410.
  • Two churches The Gothic Týn, its towers 80 metres high, and baroque St Nicholas, finished 1735.
  • Protected The layered Old Town became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1992.

Chapter Four · Josefov

The city I couldn't leave

A second walk I hadn't planned - into the old Jewish Quarter, where a wall the colour of sand handed me back my own childhood.

It was half past three when we got back to the room, and I found I was not ready to be finished with Prague. The city had got under my skin. So we went out again, the whole family, and walked - into Josefov, the old Jewish Quarter, one of the oldest and best-preserved in Europe, its synagogues and ancient cemetery folded into a handful of narrow, remarkable streets.

And there, unexpectedly, the city handed me a memory. Walking those streets I was suddenly somewhere else entirely - back in Saudi Arabia, where I spent part of my childhood. It was the colours that did it: the yellow walls, the warm sand tones of the stone glowing in the afternoon light, so like the country of my younger years. That is the strange gift of travel - how a wall the colour of sand, on a street you have never walked before, can hand you back your own beginnings. We had come out for a little more of Prague; Prague gave me Saudi Arabia instead.

A wall the colour of sand, in the middle of Europe, and suddenly I was a child in Saudi Arabia again.

Of everything in the old Jewish Quarter, though, the thing the children will not forget is a clock. The Jewish Town Hall - a pretty rose-coloured building beside the oldest working synagogue in Europe - carries two of them. The upper one, with Roman numerals, behaves itself. The lower one is marked with Hebrew letters, and because Hebrew is read from right to left, its golden hands turn the other way: anticlockwise, backwards, the wrong way round. We stood and watched it a while, quietly delighted - a whole quarter that has survived everything history could throw at it, keeping its own time, in its own direction, exactly as it always has.

Josefov · at a glance
  • Oldest The Old Jewish Cemetery, in use from 1439 to 1787, with the dead laid up to twelve deep.
  • Backwards The Jewish Town Hall's Hebrew clock runs anticlockwise, right to left.
  • Legend Rabbi Judah Loew lies here - the scholar bound to the tale of the Golem of Prague.
  • Us A second walk we hadn't planned - and a wall that gave me back Saudi Arabia.
Chapter Five · Brno

A detour south, for a memory

The longer road out of the Czech Republic, taken for the sake of a city Avi had carried with him since 2014 - and the great cathedral at the top of it.

We left Prague at eight in the morning and pointed the car south - and not by the fastest road, but by the one that ran through Brno. Twelve years earlier, back in 2014, Avi had come to this city for work, and he had wanted, ever since, to stand in its streets again before we grew any older.

That is the kind of detour a family makes and a guidebook never would: kilometres added to a long driving day for the sake of a memory. We reached Brno and did the least romantic thing imaginable - dropped a load of travel-worn clothes at a laundry - and then, free of it, set out on foot to walk the city Avi remembered.

What he had come back for stands at the top of the old town: the Cathedral of Sts Peter and Paul, the Gothic church whose twin spires rise over every view of Brno. Avi had photographed it on that first trip in 2014; twelve years later he brought the five of us to stand in front of it together.

This time we went inside. After Prague's St Vitus we thought we knew what a Gothic nave could do, and still the height of it caught us: clustered pillars rising into a pale ribbed vault, the long aisle drawing the eye down to the high altar, and a dark baroque organ riding overhead on a loft of carved angels.

Down in the lower town we came to Zelný trh, the Cabbage Market - Brno's oldest square, a working market for some eight centuries, ringed by pastel houses and the old Reduta theatre. In the middle of it rose a Baroque column of saints and gilded cloud, and around its railings the ordinary business of a market day went on exactly as it must always have.

The bells that ring noon at eleven

Brno's cathedral keeps a stubborn little tradition: its bells ring noon an hour early, at eleven. The story goes back to 1645, when a Swedish army besieging the city vowed to give up if it had not fallen by midday. The defenders simply rang noon at eleven - the Swedes, true to their word, packed up and left - and Brno has rung its false noon ever since.

Brno · at a glance
  • Second city Brno is the Czech Republic's second-largest city and the historic capital of Moravia.
  • Petrov The Cathedral of Sts Peter and Paul crowns the city - and famously rings its noon bells at eleven.
  • Zelny trh The Cabbage Market has traded for some eight centuries, beside the theatre where young Mozart played in 1767.

Reflections

What we carried out

What we will remember most about Prague is the detail. We loved the cathedral above everything - but it was never alone: every building around it was carved and moulded and ornamented with a thousand small things, as though no surface in the whole city was ever allowed to be plain. A city that looks like a crown, and rewards you for looking closer.

And Prague taught us a lesson we carried for the rest of the trip: in a July heatwave, you start early. We learned it the hard way on our very first afternoon, when the heat baking off the cobbles drove us straight onto a sightseeing bus. Reach the sights by first light instead, and by one o'clock you have covered almost everything - and beaten both the sun and the crowds. When we crossed the Charles Bridge at a quarter past eight it was nearly empty; the evening before, that same bridge had run shoulder to shoulder. Early is everything.

Worth Remembering
  • Our favourite - St Vitus, and the endless carving on every building around it. Nothing in Prague was ever plain.
  • The hardest - the July heat, and small legs on the castle hill. The trick we learned: start at dawn, and by one o'clock you've seen almost everything.
  • If we come back - a cooler month, the river cruise we let go this time, and maybe a proper medieval dinner.

We rubbed the brass on the Charles Bridge and made the old wish - that Prague would not be a place we saw only once. A family that leaves a little of its hope locked onto a bridge fully intends to come back for it. Until then, the city of a hundred spires keeps our place.
