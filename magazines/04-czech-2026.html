A family memoir — told in Inoshi's words

One city, two days,
and a thousand years the war spared.

We crossed our first European land border at dawn — out of a hilltop Austrian castle and north into Bohemia — and spent two days inside the single city the whole country seems to fold into: Prague, the city of a hundred spires. A castle above the river, a bridge full of saints, a clock six centuries old. Our best hours came early, before the crowds woke. This is what actually happened.

Chapter One · Prague Castle

The hill that outlived everything

A thousand years of Bohemian kings — and, to our surprise, it was the cathedrals, not the fortress, that took the words out of us.

The third morning had become a rhythm by now: awake at five, all of us, the children as bright as the adults, down to that enormous castle breakfast one last time, and then the bags hauled back down the lift-less stairs and into the car. We said goodbye to Burg Altpernstein, pointed north, and watched Austria fall away behind us — lakes and salt villages and Sound of Music hills — until the road tipped us over a border and into Bohemia.

Prague gave us tired legs before it gave us anything else. We reached the city in the afternoon and climbed straight for the hill and the castle — and three small pairs of legs can only climb so far. The rescue, when the tiredness set in, was a little tourist train on rubber wheels that carried us gently round the sights and explained everything in English as it went. (A thing we worked out the hard way, and pass on: in Prague you buy your tram ticket on board the tram itself — and, to our delight, the children travelled free.)

Prague Castle is, on paper, the largest coherent castle complex in the world — some seventy thousand square metres of palaces and churches founded around the year 880 and still, a thousand years on, the seat of the Czech head of state. We walked its courtyards and stepped into its great churches. And here was the surprise none of us saw coming: for all the fortress's fame, it was the churches that stopped us — grander than the walls that hold them, the true crown of the hill.

First, next door to the cathedral, the older and plainer of the two: St George's Basilica. Through a baroque door and back a thousand years — one long Romanesque room of honey-coloured stone, round arches marching two by two toward the raised choir. After the grandeur to come, it was the quiet that the children liked.

And then St Vitus, and the day quietly ran out of words. The cathedral was begun in 1344 on the order of Charles IV and not finished until 1929 — nearly six hundred years written into one building — and stepping inside, you lose the top of it. Pillars rise higher than seems possible into a ribbed stone vault that runs off into shadow, and the eye is pulled the whole length of the nave to a rose window more than ten metres wide, throwing pools of red and blue and violet onto the gleaming ranks of the organ below. This, we all decided, was the most beautiful room in Prague. It is where the children went quiet.

The pillars rise into the dark, the whole west wall turns to coloured light — and three restless children forget, for a minute, to fidget.
Stone, not paint

What soars overhead here is a Gothic rib vault — the ceiling held up, and made beautiful, by stone itself. The pointed arch and the ribbed vault were a twelfth-century French invention that let cathedrals climb far higher and lighter than Rome had ever built; the masons of Prague, led by the young Peter Parler, turned that engineering into stone lace. It is the north's great answer to a very different southern art — for a ceiling that is a painting, a whole sky opened overhead in fresco, no one has ever matched Italy.

Beyond the churches lie the government rooms — the Old Royal Palace, where for centuries a whole kingdom's memory was kept in painted books and painted shields. We found the Land Rolls, the Desky zemské: leather volumes that held Bohemia's laws and lawsuits, each spine marked with a number and a bright little badge so a clerk could pull the right one down again.

And so to the throne itself: a slim gold Gothic chair under a fall of dark velvet, guarded by the silver double-tailed lion that is the badge of Bohemia, with benches of deep red for a court that has not sat for more than a century. For a thousand years this hill had been the seat of kings — and here, at last, was the seat.

By the time we came down off the hill, everyone was hungry — and we never did sit down to a proper Czech feast. We grazed instead, on our feet, on the long walk down: one trdelník and one spiral potato passed hand to hand until there was nothing left but sticky fingers and sugar round three small mouths.

What we were really eating

The trdelník is sold on every Prague corner as "old Bohemian," but it really belongs to the Transylvanian kürtőskalács — a ribbon of dough wound round a spit, grilled and rolled in cinnamon sugar; the ice-cream stuffed inside is a modern tourist flourish. The spiral potato travelled further still: Korean street food, now somehow a fixture of the Prague lanes. We ate both, happily, and asked no questions until later.

Chapter Two · Charles Bridge

The bridge before the crowds wake

Prague's most famous crossing, 515 metres of baroque saints — and for one early hour, very nearly ours alone.

The secret to the Charles Bridge is simple: go early. By mid-morning its 515 metres run shoulder to shoulder; but leave before the day fills up and it becomes a different place entirely. We left the apartment at eight and were out on the bridge by a quarter past — and to our delight, at that hour it was almost empty.

The most famous crossing in Prague, near-deserted: just us, the thirty baroque saints who have guarded the parapets since the 1700s, the mist lifting off the Vltava, and the towers at each end catching the first real light. The bridge has stood since 1357. For a little while that morning, it was almost entirely ours. By the time we turned back toward the Old Town, the first tour groups were only arriving — we had beaten them by an hour, and it was worth every minute of the early start.

Halfway across we stopped at the one saint everyone comes to find. The day before, up in St Vitus, we had stood beside the glittering silver tomb of St John of Nepomuk; now here was the very spot the story began — a brass cross and a worn relief set into the parapet, marking where he was thrown into the river in 1393. Rub the brass, they say, and one day Prague will call you home again.

The city war forgot

Ask why Prague dazzles where Dresden, Warsaw and Berlin were reduced to rubble, and the answer is a rare stroke of historical luck: it escaped both the systematic bombing and the final scorched-earth battle that flattened so much of Central Europe. The result is a thousand years of architecture standing shoulder to shoulder — Romanesque cellars beneath Gothic spires, baroque palaces beside swirling Art Nouveau fronts. Much of that grandeur is owed to Charles IV, the Bohemian king and Holy Roman Emperor who made Prague his imperial capital in the 1300s, founded Central Europe's first university, and laid the stone bridge that still bears his name. The continuity held even through 1989, when the peaceful Velvet Revolution ended communism without shattering a windowpane.

Chapter Three · The Old Town

A city that looks like King's Landing

A clock that has walked on the hour since 1410, a square too majestic to photograph, and lanes you don't march through but drift.

From the bridge we walked back into the Old Town Square for the thing everyone comes to see: the Astronomical Clock — the Orloj — which has hung on the wall of the Old Town Hall since 1410, making it the oldest clock of its kind still working anywhere on earth.

It is really three instruments in one: a clock for the hour, a golden calendar dial that turns once a year through the signs of the zodiac, and an astronomical dial that maps the sun and moon against the heavens. Six hundred years of gears, and it still keeps perfect time. We joined the crowd on the hour and watched the show — two small windows opening, the twelve Apostles parading across in turn while beside the dial a skeleton, Death himself, tolled his bell. A one-minute performance that has drawn this square together for six centuries; when it ended, everyone looked up at the same moment, and smiled.

Six hundred years of clockwork — and still, every hour on the hour, the Apostles walk and the whole square looks up at once.

There is a word for Prague's Old Town, and the word is majestic. Standing in the great square, ringed by the twin Gothic spires of the Týn Church and the pastel palaces, I kept thinking of King's Landing — the capital from Game of Thrones — because that is exactly what Prague looks like: a fantasy city made real, all towers and turrets and red roofs climbing the hills. Except Prague is not a film set. It has stood, and been loved, and survived, for a thousand years.

We tried, and failed, to capture it — every photograph came back smaller than the thing itself. You have to stand in the middle of it, turn slowly, and let the spires close in around you. And there is a second kind of grandeur here: the Old Town streets are lined with every luxury name you could think of, the shopfronts as polished and stately as the palaces above them — and tucked among them the Czech shops we came to love, like Manufaktura, its window full of beer cosmetics and honey soap.

Why the lanes run so narrow

Not for the heat — that trick belongs to far hotter cities. Prague's Old Town was simply never planned: it grew lane by lane through the Middle Ages, following old market paths and the tight property lines of a walled town where every foot of land was dear. Stranger still, the ground itself was raised — in the thirteenth century, to escape the Vltava's floods, the townsfolk lifted the streets several metres, burying the Romanesque houses whose ground floors survive today as cellars. The medieval city, quite literally, lies beneath your feet.

Chapter Four · Josefov

The city I couldn't leave

A second walk I hadn't planned — into the old Jewish Quarter, where a wall the colour of sand handed me back my own childhood.

It was half past three when we got back to the room, and I found I was not ready to be finished with Prague. The city had got under my skin. So we went out again, the whole family, and walked — into Josefov, the old Jewish Quarter, one of the oldest and best-preserved in Europe, its synagogues and ancient cemetery folded into a handful of narrow, remarkable streets.

And there, unexpectedly, the city handed me a memory. Walking those streets I was suddenly somewhere else entirely — back in Saudi Arabia, where I spent part of my childhood. It was the colours that did it: the yellow walls, the warm sand tones of the stone glowing in the afternoon light, so like the country of my younger years. That is the strange gift of travel — how a wall the colour of sand, on a street you have never walked before, can hand you back your own beginnings.

A wall the colour of sand, in the middle of Europe — and suddenly I was a child in Saudi Arabia again.

We stood and watched it a while, quietly delighted. A whole quarter that has survived everything history could throw at it, keeping its own time, in its own direction, exactly as it always has.

Reflections

What we carried out

What we will remember most about Prague is the detail. We loved the cathedral above everything — but it was never alone: every building around it was carved and moulded and ornamented with a thousand small things, as though no surface in the whole city was ever allowed to be plain. A city that looks like a crown, and rewards you for looking closer.

And Prague taught us a lesson we carried for the rest of the trip: in a July heatwave, you start early. Reach the sights by first light and by one o'clock you have covered almost everything — and beaten both the sun and the crowds. When we crossed the Charles Bridge at a quarter past eight it was nearly empty; the evening before, that same bridge had run shoulder to shoulder. Early is everything.

Worth Remembering
  • Our favourite — St Vitus, and the endless carving on every building around it. Nothing in Prague was ever plain.
  • The hardest — the July heat, and small legs on the castle hill. The trick we learned: start at dawn, and by one o'clock you've seen almost everything.
  • If we come back — a cooler month, the river cruise we let go this time, and maybe a proper medieval dinner.

We rubbed the brass on the Charles Bridge and made the old wish — that Prague would not be a place we saw only once. A family that leaves a little of its hope locked onto a bridge fully intends to come back for it. Until then, the city of a hundred spires keeps our place.
