No. 03 · Welcome
Welcome to
the Salt Country

In one country we saw three radically different Austrias. Hallstatt was the oldest — a lakeside village whose salt mines named an entire Iron Age culture. Salzburg was the most musical — where a four-year-old Mozart began composing in a yellow townhouse off Getreidegasse. Vienna was the most imperial — where six hundred years of Habsburg power left palaces in every direction.

These three cities are bound by mountains, by lakes, by the Danube and the Salzach, and — quietly, in the way Austria does these things — by classical music piped from every café terrace.

This issue is about what happens when one small country gives you a lake you will come back for, a Sound of Music garden, an emperor's palace, and a cathedral that leaves you wordless — all within four hundred kilometres of each other.

SALZBURG · THE CITY, THEN THE SONG
Do-Re-Mi,
in the garden at last.
First, the city that salt built
Salzburg means, quite literally, "salt fortress" — and salt made it rich. For centuries the "white gold" hauled from nearby Alpine mines financed something unusual: a small state ruled not by a king but by prince-archbishops, churchmen who were also sovereign princes, answerable to neither emperor nor, in practice, wholly to Rome. With their salt fortunes they hired Italian architects to reshape a medieval town into a jewel of the Baroque — "the Rome of the North."
From that gilded backdrop emerged, in 1756, its most famous son: Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, born on the Getreidegasse and chafing, in time, against the archbishop's stifling employ. Two centuries later Hollywood arrived to film The Sound of Music among its gardens and hills — cementing a curious dual identity: a Baroque city-state built on salt, beloved worldwide for a prodigy who fled it and a film the locals scarcely watch. That film, though, is where our own Salzburg story begins.
The Sound of Music was my childhood — acted out with my sister and a row of dolls. To walk its garden now, with a husband and three children of my own, was a dream I never dared to dream.
Sat 11 Jul 2026Mirabell Gardens, SalzburgTwo hours behind schedule
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
At the Mirabell garden gate — the very steps where the von Trapp children line up in the last verse of "Do-Re-Mi", the fortress on its hill behind. The moves I'd copied for thirty years, this time for real.

The Sound of Music was one of my favourite films as a child. When I was six or seven, my sister and I would act it out for hours — I was always Maria, she was Liesl, and a row of dolls stood in for the rest of the von Trapp children. I knew every song. I must have watched that film more than a hundred times, and every single time, in my mind, I was already here — already in Austria. To stand in the real Salzburg now, decades later, with a husband beside me and three children of my own, was a dream far beyond anything that little girl ever dared to dream.

It was noon by the time we reached the city, and after Beijing, a red-eye flight and a morning in Munich, we arrived with tired legs — Aviann most of all. So we did Salzburg gently. We walked into the Mirabell Gardens, the very place where Maria and the children skipped and sang "Do-Re-Mi". And there, among the flowerbeds and fountains with the fortress watching from its hill, I stood at last where I had only ever pretended to stand — the garden gate itself, right where the von Trapp children line up in the last verse of the song, the pose I had struck for a row of dolls a hundred times over. Thirty years of dreaming, and this time the gate was real, and my own children were the ones beside me.

The girl who once played Maria with a row of dolls never dreamed she would stand in this garden one day — with children of her own.

We could happily have stayed until dusk. But with a two-hour delay to make up and three children wilting in the July heat, we let the garden be enough on its own — no checklist, no rushing from one thing to the next. Just one unhurried hour in the place a small girl with a row of dolls had dreamed of her whole life, with a husband beside me and my own three running the paths. The rest of Salzburg — the Old Town, just across the river — could wait a few minutes more.

SALZBURG · THE OLD TOWN · IN THREE SQUARES
Beyond the garden,
the city itself.
The film has the gardens; the real Salzburg is just across the river — a knot of Baroque squares, guild-sign lanes and the house where Mozart was born, taken at an ice-cream pace.
Sat 11 Jul 2026Salzburg AltstadtA gentle hour
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Partway up the fortress path — all five of us with the Salzburg Cathedral's twin green-copper domes and the Old Town roofs spread out below, the forested hill still climbing above us.

Across the Salzach from the Mirabell gardens lies the Altstadt — the Old Town — a UNESCO-listed tangle of domes and squares packed tight beneath the fortress. We crossed the river and let it lead us: Residenzplatz with its great fountain, the cathedral square beside it, and the marble front of the Residenz where the prince-archbishops once held court.

We turned into the Getreidegasse, Salzburg's most famous lane — narrow and tall, strung end to end with the wrought-iron guild signs that once told a mostly illiterate street who sold what: a pretzel for the baker, a key for the locksmith, a golden horse for the inn. At number 9, behind a bright yellow façade, Mozart was born in 1756; we passed the doorway in the crush without going in, and promised the children the music another day.

Three tired children and a July sun — so we did what Salzburg does best in the heat, and bought ice-cream.

Already running late for the evening's Mass, we took the city at a stroll rather than a march — a scoop of ice-cream each, a pause at the horse-pond fountain, and the fiakers waiting patiently for a fare under the cathedral towers. Not the grand tour Salzburg deserves, but an hour among its beautiful bones — and enough to know we'd want to come back.

SALZBURG · THE OLD TOWN · THE MEDIEVAL CITY
The Old Town,
lane by lane.
Across the Salzach from Mirabell lies the Altstadt — a UNESCO-listed tangle of tall, thin houses and guild-sign lanes, squeezed between the river and the cliff of the Mönchsberg, and paid for, every inch, in salt.
Sat 11 Jul 2026Salzburg AltstadtTwo towns in one

Why the lanes are so narrow. Squeezed between the river Salzach and the sheer cliff of the Mönchsberg, medieval Salzburg had almost nowhere to grow. So it grew up and back instead: the merchants' houses on the Getreidegasse are tall and astonishingly thin at the front but run deep into the block, courtyard behind courtyard, their guild signs hung out over lanes barely wide enough for a cart. The old rule still holds — every shop must hang a wrought-iron sign, so even the modern chains, McDonald's included, swing a hand-forged emblem over the street exactly as the bakers and locksmiths once did.

Paid for in salt. All of it was bought with salt. The Salzach — the "salt river" — carried the white gold mined in the mountains downstream to the world, and the tolls on it made the archbishops some of the richest princes in the Holy Roman Empire. What they could not spend on armies, they spent on stone.

The fortress that made it possible. Above it all broods the Hohensalzburg, begun in 1077 and never once taken by siege — one of the largest fully preserved medieval castles in Europe. Under its protection the archbishops, secure and rich, could rebuild the town below exactly as they pleased.

Two towns, two banks. The Salzach splits the city in two: the left bank, tight beneath the Mönchsberg, was the archbishops' own — cathedral, Residenz and grand squares; the right bank was the burghers' quarter of craftsmen, inns and the house where Mozart later lived. We crossed from the Mirabell side and never really left the old one again.

SALZBURG · THE OLD TOWN · THE BAROQUE CITY
Grand squares,
and a reckoning.
Then came the salt money, and the ambition: out of the dark lanes the prince-archbishops cleared a Baroque stage of squares and domes — a beauty the twentieth century very nearly took away.
Salzburg Altstadtc. 1600 · 1944 · 1996Architecture & a reckoning

Why the squares are so grand. From about 1600 the prince-archbishops, above all Wolf Dietrich von Raitenau, set out to remake Salzburg as "the Rome of the North." Whole medieval quarters were pulled down to open the squares we crossed — Residenzplatz, Domplatz, Kapitelplatz — and the Italian architect Santino Solari raised the vast domed cathedral above them between 1614 and 1628. That is the contrast you feel with every step: out of a dark, close lane, and the city suddenly opens into Italian light.

A skyline of domes. The result is the profile you see from every bridge: the cathedral's great dome, the green cupola of the Franciscan church, and the twin towers of the Kollegienkirche — a masterpiece by Fischer von Erlach — with the fortress on its rock above them all. It is a skyline scarcely changed in three hundred years.

Step out of a narrow medieval lane and the city opens, all at once, into Italian light — that is Salzburg's whole story in a single stride.

And what the last century did to it. The twentieth century nearly took it all. Austria was annexed in the Anschluss of 1938; that November the synagogue was wrecked in the Kristallnacht pogrom, and — as The Sound of Music tells — the real von Trapp family fled rather than serve the new regime. From October 1944 Allied bombers struck the city fifteen times, killing some 550 people: the cathedral's dome was pierced and collapsed, and close to half of Salzburg was damaged before American troops arrived in May 1945. Yet the heart of the Altstadt came through, was patiently rebuilt, and in 1996 was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site — the layered survivor we walked through today.

Our first Austrian evening taught us two lessons the hard way: on a Saturday, the shops shut at six — and a genuine medieval castle has stairs, not elevators.
Sat 11 Jul 2026Salzburg → Burg AltpernsteinMass to midnight
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Burg Altpernstein — our first night in Austria: a genuine hilltop castle, kept historical to the core, right down to the stairs where a lift might have been.

Mass finished at a quarter to eight, and we stepped out into a problem we hadn't seen coming. It was Saturday, and in Austria the shops close at six — so our plan to pick up groceries for dinner had quietly expired two hours earlier, while we were still in the pews. A tired family, three hungry children, and a town that had rolled up its shutters for the weekend.

We found a restaurant just before it, too, closed for the night, and ordered pizza and pasta almost at the door. It cost more than the supermarket dinner we had planned — an unexpected dent in the day's budget — but it was hot, it was food, and after the day we'd had, nobody was complaining.

In Austria on a Saturday, the shops shut at six. We learned that with three hungry children and a Mass that ran until a quarter to eight.

Then, at last, the castle. We were staying at Burg Altpernstein, a genuine hilltop fortress — and a genuine fortress, it turns out, has no elevator. We hauled our heavy bags up to the third floor by stair, breath by breath, and we couldn't even mind: you don't keep a castle's medieval soul by drilling a lift shaft through it. A hot bath washed the long day away, and we fell into bed more tired than we had been at any point on this trip. Day one in Austria, done.

HALLSTATT · DAY 2 · IN OUR OWN WORDS
The salt mine was closed —
so we walked the lake.
We woke at five, fresh at last, filled up on a castle breakfast, and drove to the world's most photographed village. The famous salt mine was shut — but the lake, the boats and the cool of the morning were ours.
Sun 12 Jul 2026Hallstatt, SalzkammergutArrived 10:30 · village + boat
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Hallstatt from the lakeshore — pastel houses stacked up the cliff above the Hallstättersee, the church spire and mountain behind: the view on a thousand postcards.

We woke at five o'clock — all of us, even the children, and for once wide awake and bright. The hot bath the night before had done its quiet work, and the exhaustion of the long journey had finally lifted. We were at the breakfast buffet by a quarter past seven, fifteen minutes before it opened, and when it did we fell on it happily: cheese and ham, salads and dips, fruit juice and cereal, more than we could finish. Full and ready, we drove out to Hallstatt.

The salt mine was shut, the lanes were packed — and still, in the morning light, Hallstatt was every bit the postcard.

We reached the village around half past ten, early enough that the fierce midday heat had not yet arrived. Hallstatt is astonishing — pastel houses stacked up a cliff above an emerald lake, the whole scene doubling in the still water. It is also, we quickly discovered, one of the most crowded small places on earth: the narrow lanes ran shoulder to shoulder with visitors. The mountain, sadly, was against us that day — both the famous salt mine, the oldest in the world, and the Skywalk viewpoint above it were closed. We made one more skip by choice: the Bone House, the little chapel of painted skulls, felt a step too far for a four-year-old, so we spared Avin the crypt. We let those go and took the village on its own gentler terms.

So we did the simplest thing of all — we walked. Down the lanes and along the waterfront, in the cool before the midday heat, taking Hallstatt slowly and on foot while we still had the morning to ourselves.

HALLSTATT · THE MOST PHOTOGRAPHED VILLAGE
Shoulder to shoulder
in a postcard.
They call Hallstatt the most photographed village on earth — and on a July morning, with the lanes packed and the lake shining, we could see exactly why.
Sun 12 Jul 2026Hallstatt, SalzkammergutVillage + boat
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
All five of us at the classic Hallstatt viewpoint — the village stacked up the mountain, the lake below, the shot the whole world comes here to take.

By mid-morning the lanes ran shoulder to shoulder — Hallstatt is tiny, and half the world seemed to have come to photograph it at once. We joined them happily, wandering the waterfront and the market square with its Trinity column, and lining up along the lakeside railing for the photograph everyone comes here to take: all five of us, the whole village stacked up the mountain behind.

Then we did the one thing that beats any viewpoint — we took a boat out onto the Hallstättersee, and watched the village pull slowly into frame from the water, the mountains climbing behind it. Someone had a postcard of the same view under winter snow; we held it up against the summer lake — same church, same cliff, same impossible prettiness — and took a picture of the picture.

We never ate in Hallstatt itself — that enormous castle breakfast lasted us all day — and it wasn't until the drive back that we finally tried the dish Austria is famous for, a proper Wiener Schnitzel. Then it was back up the hill to the castle, a hot bath, and bed — the second day folded away as gently as the first. Not the day we'd planned, with the salt mine shut. A better one.

After the lakes and Salzburg we crossed the northern border into Czechia and gave two whole days to Prague — Charles Bridge almost empty in the early light, the castle and cathedral up on the hill, the old astronomical clock striking the hour — before turning south again for the grand finale of this chapter.

IMPERIAL VIENNA · SCHÖNBRUNN
The Habsburgs built
a city for themselves.
Six hundred years of empire left Vienna with more palaces per square kilometre than any other European city. We reached just one — Schönbrunn — and it was more than enough for a single afternoon.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Schönbrunn Palace, ViennaArrived 4pm · rooms till 6
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
A Schönbrunn postcard held up against the real thing — the printed golden façade and the Gloriette on the hill, matched to the palace rising behind. The Habsburgs' summer home, all 1,441 rooms of it.

You cannot understand Vienna without understanding the Habsburgs. From 1278 until 1918 — six hundred and forty years — this one family ran an empire that at various points reached across Austria, Hungary, Bohemia, Croatia, parts of Italy and Poland, all of the Holy Roman Empire, and for a few decades even Spain and Mexico. They built like people who knew they would never leave: the Hofburg sprawling across the city centre, and out to the west their summer palace, Schönbrunn, with its 1,441 rooms.

Vienna is the city of dreams. — Sigmund Freud, who lived a few streets from the imperial palaces.

We came late, after the long detour through Brno, and passed under Schönbrunn's gates at four o'clock. That gave us the palace but not its gardens: we spent two hours indoors, room after gilded room, and stepped back out at six with the light already softening — too late to climb the hill to the Gloriette, or to lose the children among the parterres. We saw the gardens only from the terrace, stretching away to the arch on the ridge. Some things you leave for next time. And inside those rooms we found the story that would follow us all evening — the one about the emperor and the reluctant young empress he chose.

The city that married an empire
Vienna's improbable rise rested less on the battlefield than on the wedding altar. The Habsburgs, whose modest Austrian duchy became the seat of a sprawling multi-ethnic realm, mastered a strategy immortalised in the motto: "Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry." Through shrewd dynastic unions they gathered crowns from Spain to Hungary to the Low Countries, ruling Czechs, Poles, Italians, Croats and Magyars from a single Danube city.
In 1683 that fortune nearly ended. An Ottoman army of some 150,000 besieged Vienna for two months — until a Polish-led relief force under King Jan Sobieski swept down from the Kahlenberg heights in a thundering cavalry charge and broke the siege. Vienna had marked Islam's furthest advance into central Europe. The city that survived would soon become the world's capital of music — cradle to Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms and the waltzing Strauss dynasty.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE STATE ROOMS · IN OUR OWN WORDS
The hall built to
take your breath away.
Forty-three metres of white, gold and mirror under a painted sky — Maria Theresa's grand hall, where an empire held its banquets and balls, and where, two centuries later, the Cold War paused for a summit.
Wed 15 Jul 2026The Great Gallery, SchönbrunnThe state apartments
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Aviann and Avin, small under all that gold, stopped in the middle of the Great Gallery to stare up at the ceiling — exactly as the room was built to make you do.

The Great Gallery is the ceremonial heart of Schönbrunn: nearly forty-three metres long, walled in mirrors and gilded stucco, lit by great crystal chandeliers and roofed by an enormous ceiling fresco. This was Maria Theresa's room for the grandest occasions — state banquets, receptions, and balls that ran until dawn.

The ceiling was painted in 1760 by the Italian master Gregorio Guglielmi: a swirling allegory glorifying the reign — the crown lands, the arts and sciences, the good government of the monarchy. Its central panel was destroyed by a bomb in 1945 and painstakingly restored after the war.

Balls for the Congress of Vienna were danced here in 1814; and in 1961 Kennedy met Khrushchev beneath this same painted sky.

History keeps passing through it. During the Congress of Vienna in 1814–15, the powers redrawing Europe waltzed in this gallery. And in June 1961 the room hosted the summit between John F. Kennedy and Nikita Khrushchev — the two superpowers, face to face, under Maria Theresa's ceiling.

SCHÖNBRUNN · A HISTORY IN LIGHT
And then the walls
came alive.
Before the state rooms, a darkened chamber where three centuries of the palace's story were thrown across the rococo panelling — the yellow facade, the gardens, cavalry massing in the great courtyard.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Schönbrunn · the multimedia roomProjection show
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The palace's own front, thrown across its inner walls — the parade ground filling with horses and cannon as the story played out around us.

Schönbrunn began as a hunting lodge, wrecked by the Turks and then rebuilt. It was Maria Theresa who turned it into the palace we walked today — a summer residence of some fourteen hundred rooms, its walls washed in the warm ochre that Austria still calls "Schönbrunn yellow." The gardens, the Gloriette on the hill, the menagerie that grew into the world's oldest zoo — all of it took shape under her.

The projection carried the family's whole story: the great receptions, the six-year-old Mozart who played here for the empress in 1762, and the darker turns as well.

Only one foreigner ever slept as master in these rooms — and he arrived with an army.

That foreigner was Napoleon, who made Schönbrunn his headquarters twice, in 1805 and 1809, dictating peace to the Habsburgs from their own palace. In a final twist, his only legitimate son — the little "King of Rome," grandson of the Austrian emperor — grew up here and died in these rooms in 1832, at just twenty-one. The children watched it all move across the gold, wide-eyed.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE FACE ON THE WALL
The woman who
made this place.
Every corridor at Schönbrunn leads back to her — Maria Theresa: the only woman ever to rule the Habsburg lands in her own right, mother of sixteen, and the maker of the palace as it stands.
Schönbrunn, ViennaMaria Theresa · 1717–1780Portrait
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Maria Theresa — sovereign of Austria, Hungary and Bohemia for forty years, and ancestress of half the royal houses of Europe.

When her father died in 1740 leaving no son, twenty-three-year-old Maria Theresa inherited an empire that half of Europe promptly tried to carve up. She fought them off through the long War of the Austrian Succession and held the lands together for four decades, reforming the army, the treasury and the schools as she went.

She married for love — Francis Stephen of Lorraine — and bore sixteen children in twenty years, marrying them across the continent to bind it by blood. The youngest daughter, Maria Antonia, went to France and became Marie Antoinette. Schönbrunn was the family's beloved summer home, and it is her taste, her yellow and her ceilings that fill it still.

SCHÖNBRUNN · HOW AN EMPIRE LIVED AT HOME
Gilt clocks, and a table
laid for the family.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The rococo hours

Even telling the time was an occasion. This gilt-bronze mantel clock — all scrolls, flowers and tumbling putti — is pure eighteenth-century court rococo, built to dazzle as much as to keep the hour. Rooms like these held hundreds of such pieces: porcelain, ormolu, lacquer and mirror, a language of luxury spoken fluently only by the very rich.

The imperial table

The Habsburgs dined under a strict "Spanish court ceremonial," every plate and place governed by rank and ritual. Yet Maria Theresa also prized something rarer for royalty — ordinary family meals, the children at the table, warmth behind the pomp. These settings, crystal and porcelain on white damask, hold both the grandeur and the domesticity this house was known for.

IMPERIAL VIENNA · SCHÖNBRUNN · THE STORY WE HEARD
The emperor who chose
the wrong sister.
At Schönbrunn today the tour told us the story the whole city seems to keep — Franz Joseph and his Sisi: a love match that began in a summer garden and ended, forty-five years later, on a lakeside in Geneva.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Schönbrunn Palace, ViennaThe Sisi story

In the summer of 1853, in the spa town of Bad Ischl, a twenty-three-year-old emperor was supposed to fall in love to order. Franz Joseph I already ruled the Habsburg empire, and his mother, the formidable Archduchess Sophie, had chosen his bride: her niece Helene of Bavaria, the sensible elder sister. But when the family arrived, Franz Joseph barely noticed Helene. His eye had gone straight to her younger sister — fifteen-year-old Elisabeth, whom everyone called Sisi. Within five days they were engaged.

They married in Vienna the following spring, and Sisi walked into the most rigid court in Europe. She was famous almost at once for her beauty — a tiny waist she guarded with fierce discipline, and hair so long it fell past her ankles and took her hairdresser three hours to arrange. But the gilded protocol of the Hofburg suffocated her. She escaped it constantly, travelling for months at a time — to Hungary, which she loved, to Corfu, to anywhere the court was not — and filled private notebooks with melancholy poems.

He was matched with the elder sister and chose the younger — and in sixty-eight years upon the throne, he never loved another.

Yet Sisi was more than ornament. She loved Hungary above every other land of the empire, learned its language when almost no one at court would, and lent her influence to the Compromise of 1867 that made her husband King of Hungary as well as Emperor of Austria — and crowned her a queen in Budapest. It stayed the achievement she was proudest of.

Sorrow shadowed the fairy tale. In 1889 their only son, Crown Prince Rudolf, died at his hunting lodge at Mayerling, and Sisi wore mourning black for the rest of her life. Nine years later, on a Geneva quay in 1898, an anarchist stepped from a crowd and stabbed her with a sharpened file; she walked on a few steps before she fell. Franz Joseph reigned alone until 1916 — sixty-eight years in all — outliving his wife, his son, and very nearly his empire.

Everything about her became legend: the ankle-length hair washed with egg and cognac, the diamond stars pinned through it, the exercise rings fixed in her palace rooms, the notebooks of verse. A century on, the films turned her into a sugared sweetheart — "Sissi," with a double S — but the real Elisabeth was stranger, sadder and far more interesting than the girl on the screen.

The children listened with wide eyes. It is a strange thing to explain to a child: that the most admired woman in Europe, who lived in a golden palace of a thousand rooms, was for much of her life quietly unhappy.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE PALACE, AND ITS EMPRESS
A summer palace
for an empress.
Schönbrunn began as a hunting wood and a "beautiful spring." It took one extraordinary woman — Maria Theresa — to turn it into the golden heart of the Habsburg empire.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Schönbrunn Palace, Vienna1,441 rooms

The Habsburgs had hunted these woods for centuries, and it was here, so the story goes, that an emperor found a schöner Brunnen — a "beautiful spring" — that gave the place its name. But the palace we walked through belongs almost entirely to one ruler: Maria Theresa.

She came to the throne in 1740, at twenty-three — the only woman ever to rule the Habsburg lands — and held it for forty years. She reformed the army, the schools and the law, fought wars to keep her crown, and still raised sixteen children, marrying them into half the courts of Europe. One daughter, Marie Antoinette, would become Queen of France.

One woman, forty years, sixteen children and 1,441 rooms — Schönbrunn is Maria Theresa's palace, and it never lets you forget it.

It was she who rebuilt Schönbrunn as the imperial summer residence: 1,441 rooms wrapped in rococo white and gold, above formal gardens climbing to the Gloriette on the hill. Only a few dozen rooms are open today, and we followed the one-way route through them, hall opening onto hall.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE GREAT GALLERY
The room where
Mozart played.
Forty-three metres of mirrors, gold and painted sky — the Great Gallery was the palace's ballroom, and the stage for three centuries of history.
Wed 15 Jul 2026The Great Gallery1762 · 1814 · 1961

The Great Gallery is the palace at its most dazzling: a ballroom the length of the building, white and gold from end to end, tall windows on one side answered by mirrors on the other, and a ceiling of painted sky where gods and allegories tumble through the clouds.

History happened in this room. In 1762 a six-year-old boy named Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart played here for Maria Theresa — and, the story goes, leapt into the empress's lap afterwards. In 1814 the statesmen of the Congress of Vienna waltzed here while they redrew the map of Europe. And in 1961 Kennedy met Khrushchev beneath these frescoes at the height of the Cold War.

A six-year-old Mozart played in this room in 1762 — and, they say, jumped straight into the empress's lap.

We stood a long moment under the chandeliers, trying to imagine the candlelight, the music and the dancing these walls have held.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE IMPERIAL APARTMENTS
Where the last
emperors lived.
Beyond the state rooms lie the private ones — where Franz Joseph rose before dawn at a plain iron bed, and his Sisi kept her salons of silk and portraits.
Wed 15 Jul 2026The Imperial Apartments1830 · 1916

For all its gold, Schönbrunn was also a home. The imperial apartments are where the last great Habsburgs actually lived — most of all Franz Joseph, who was born in this palace in 1830 and died here, still emperor, in 1916.

The tour lets you feel the contrast. Franz Joseph's own rooms are almost severe — a soldier's routine, a plain bed, a desk where he worked from four each morning. His wife Elisabeth — Sisi — kept rooms of another temper altogether, hung with silk and portraits, a private world she guarded fiercely.

He worked from four each morning at a plain desk; she filled her rooms with silk and slipped away whenever she could.

Salon opened onto salon — red damask, blue chairs, gilded sitting rooms, and a table forever set for a breakfast that had ended a hundred years before.

SCHÖNBRUNN · THE FINEST ROOMS, AND THE END
Treasures, and
the end of it all.
The rarest rooms hold the empire's wealth — and its final act. Here the monarchy signed itself away in 1918; today, light and sound bring the whole story roaring back.
Wed 15 Jul 2026The Millions Room & beyond1918 · UNESCO 1996

The finest rooms are the smallest. The Millions Room — said to have cost a million guilders — is panelled in rosewood and set with Indo-Persian miniatures; the lacquer cabinets and mirrored galleries around it hold the concentrated wealth of an empire at its height.

But every empire ends. In November 1918, in this very palace, the last emperor, Karl I, renounced all part in the affairs of state, and six centuries of Habsburg rule were over. Schönbrunn passed to the republic — and then to the world, a UNESCO site since 1996.

In November 1918, in these rooms, six hundred years of Habsburg rule quietly came to an end.

The palace tells all of this now in light. In one darkened hall its own facade and painted skies are thrown across the walls, cavalry filling the courtyard, the whole story alive around you. The children stood open-mouthed — the perfect last room.

VIENNA · 1529 · A LITTLE HISTORY
The autumn Vienna
stopped an empire.
Long before the palaces and the waltzes, Vienna was a frontier — and in the wet October of 1529 it was the wall against which the largest empire on earth finally broke.
Sept–Oct 1529The First Siege of ViennaThen & now

In September 1529 the most powerful man in the world arrived at the gates of Vienna. He was Suleiman the Magnificent, sultan of the Ottoman Empire at the very height of its reach — a realm that ran from the walls of this city to the deserts of Arabia. Three years earlier his army had destroyed the kingdom of Hungary in a single afternoon at Mohács; now he had marched the better part of a thousand miles to take the Habsburg capital itself.

To understand why Vienna trembled, you have to go back to that marsh at Mohács. There, in August 1526, the Ottoman army had annihilated the kingdom of Hungary in under two hours; the young Hungarian king drowned as he fled the field, and a wealthy Christian kingdom simply ceased to exist. The road north lay open after that, and everyone in Vienna knew it.

Suleiman set out from Istanbul in May with a vast army and a great train of cannon, but the same wet spring that would later undo him slowed the march from the start. His light horsemen, the akıncı, swept ahead and put the countryside to the torch; frightened villagers crowded in behind the walls, and the defenders burned their own suburbs so the enemy would find no shelter near the city.

Inside stood a patchwork of the age — German pikemen, Spanish musketeers sent by the emperor's brother Charles V, Bohemian and Austrian troops — perhaps twenty thousand in all, thrown together under old Count Salm to hold a medieval wall against the most modern army on earth.

Vienna was badly outnumbered. The archduke, Ferdinand I, was elsewhere, and the city's defence fell to an elderly soldier, Count Niklas von Salm, with a garrison dwarfed by the host outside the walls. But the city had two allies the sultan could not defeat. The first was the weather — an unusually wet autumn that turned the roads to mud, stranded his heavy siege cannon somewhere back in the Balkans, and left the Ottoman army cold and hungry so far from home.

The soldiers held the walls, and the rain did the rest. Vienna became the frontier that would not fall.

The second ally was time. The numbers were badly lopsided — the sultan's host several times the size of the garrison — and the fiercest fighting happened out of sight, underground. Ottoman sappers dug mines to blow breaches in the walls; the defenders dug down to meet them, listening for the scrape of spades in the dark and fighting hand to hand beneath the city. Count Salm was wounded in those last assaults, and would die of it the following spring.

By mid-October, with winter closing in and his supply lines failing, Suleiman broke off the siege and turned for home. He told the world it had never been a serious attempt — but he never took Vienna, then or later. A century and a half on, in 1683, a second and even greater Ottoman army stood before these same walls, and was broken by a thunderous charge of Polish and allied cavalry down from the Kahlenberg hills. Twice the tide of empire reached this city; twice it went out again. Everything gilded and imperial that we saw today was built, in the end, behind a line that held.

THE WIDER WORLD · EMPIRES AT THE GATE
Everyone came
for Vienna.
Vienna sat on the hinge of Europe — where the Danube road ran and Christendom met the Ottoman east. For five centuries the wider world would not leave the Habsburgs alone; it kept arriving at their gates.
The Ottomans · 1529 & 1683
Twice the Ottoman Empire — then the mightiest state on earth — marched up the Danube to these walls. In 1529 Sultan Suleiman was turned back by autumn rain and stout stone. In 1683 the city held for two months until a Christian relief army, led by the Polish king Jan Sobieski, swept down the Kahlenberg in the largest cavalry charge in history and broke the siege. It was the high-water mark of Ottoman Europe; from that day the Turks retreated, and the Habsburgs pushed south into Hungary. (Legend even hands Vienna its coffee houses and the crescent croissant from that morning.)
Napoleon · 1805 & 1809
The French emperor took Vienna twice, and both times slept in the Habsburgs' own beds at Schönbrunn. In 1806 he abolished the thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire outright — the Habsburg ruler had to settle for merely "Emperor of Austria." Then, to gild his upstart crown with old blood, Napoleon married the Habsburg princess Marie Louise, great-niece of Marie Antoinette.
The Congress of Vienna · 1814–15
When Napoleon fell, the powers of Europe came to Vienna to put the continent back together. For nine months tsars, kings and chancellors danced and bargained in Habsburg ballrooms and redrew the whole map of Europe. "The Congress does not advance," went the joke; "it dances."
Sarajevo · 1914
And one more shot from outside ended it all: the murder of the heir Franz Ferdinand in Bosnia lit the fuse of the First World War. Four years later the six-hundred-year empire was gone, and Vienna awoke the modest capital of a small republic.
Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry. — the old Habsburg motto, for a family that built an empire on weddings and lost it to other people's wars.

AN EVENING IN VIENNA · STEPHANSDOM
The cathedral at
the heart of it all.
As the palaces closed and the light turned gold, we walked the old town to St. Stephen's — the "Steffl" — the Gothic giant that has stood at the centre of Vienna for eight hundred years.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Stephansplatz, ViennaEvening
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
St. Stephen's Cathedral — the south tower soaring 136 metres, and the famous tiled roof blazing in the evening light.

St. Stephen's — Stephansdom — is Vienna's soul in stone. A church was first consecrated on this spot in 1147, and the great Gothic cathedral rose over the centuries that followed. Its south tower, 136 metres tall, is affectionately nicknamed the "Steffl," and for generations it was the tallest thing for miles around.

The roof is its glory: nearly a quarter of a million glazed tiles laid in dazzling zig-zags, and on the south side the double-headed eagle of the empire picked out in colour — a coat of arms you can really only read from the sky.

The tower the watchmen climbed to scan for fire — and for the Ottoman armies that twice reached the walls.

Below lie the catacombs, where the bones of tens of thousands of Viennese still rest. The cathedral has survived siege, plague and the last terrible days of the Second World War, when fire gutted its roof in 1945. The Viennese rebuilt it — every province of Austria helping to pay — and it reopened, whole again, in 1952. We reached it as the crowds thinned and the stone went honey-gold, and simply stood in the square looking up.

INSIDE STEPHANSDOM · IN OUR OWN WORDS
Honestly — I had
no words.
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The nave

You step out of the bright, busy square into a cool half-dark, and the ceiling simply climbs away from you — ribbed Gothic vaults vanishing into shadow, stone pillars like a forest of trunks, the long chequerboard floor drawing your eye to the far altar. After a whole day of gold built to impress you, this is a space built to make you feel small. It works. We stood there, all five of us, and none of us could speak.

Light and shadow

Seven centuries of prayer are soaked into these walls. Somewhere in the gloom is the great stone pulpit carved around 1500, its rail crawling with lizards and toads; the delicate Wiener Neustädter altar with its folding wings of gilded saints; the vast red-marble tomb of Emperor Frederick III. In the side chapels the last evening light falls in coloured shafts through the stained glass and pools on the old stone. You don't really photograph a place like this — you just stand in it.

VIENNA · THE VOTIVKIRCHE
The church built
to say thank you.
One of Vienna's tallest churches was raised not for a saint or a victory, but for a near-miss — the winter morning a young emperor felt an assassin's knife turn on his collar.
Wed 15 Jul 2026Ringstrasse, ViennaTwin spires · 99m
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The two of us under the Votivkirche — twin openwork spires of pale stone climbing 99 metres over the Ringstrasse, one of the purest neo-Gothic churches in Europe.

On the 18th of February 1853, the young Emperor Franz Joseph was walking the city bastion with an officer when a Hungarian tailor named János Libényi rushed at him and drove a knife toward his neck. The blade caught on the stiff gilt collar of his uniform, a passer-by wrestled the attacker down, and the emperor walked away with little more than a graze. An empire that had almost lost its ruler breathed out.

A cathedral raised on a stiff collar and a lucky inch.

In thanks, the emperor's younger brother — Archduke Maximilian, later the doomed Emperor of Mexico — called for a votive church "in gratitude for the salvation of His Majesty," paid for by donations gathered across the whole empire. A 26-year-old architect, Heinrich von Ferstel, won the competition; the church rose between 1856 and 1879 in soaring French Gothic, and became one of the first great monuments of Vienna's brand-new Ringstrasse. We found it almost empty, and stood a long while just looking up.

A thank-you of our own
We hadn't known, standing there, that this whole church was built as one long thank-you — raised in gratitude for a life spared. It felt right, then, that in the quiet of it we said a thank-you of our own: not for surviving an assassin, but simply for this — the great opportunity to be here at all, together, seeing the world as a family. A church built on gratitude seemed exactly the place for ours.

A TRAVELLER'S DILEMMA · WRITTEN IN VIENNA
Prague, or Vienna?
Standing in one city, torn about another — the happiest impossible question of the whole trip.
Wed 15 Jul 2026ViennaIn our own words

Somewhere on our Vienna evening the question crept up and refused to leave: which one do we love most — Prague, or Vienna? We still can't answer it, and we've decided that's the point.

Prague stole our breath like a fairy tale. It was the almost-empty Charles Bridge at eight in the morning, the Old Town that made us say out loud, "this looks like King's Landing," the golden spires and the un-photographable majesty of it, St. Vitus soaring over the castle, a tornado potato and a trdelník in a little park. A city you don't visit so much as walk into, like a story.

One is the fairy tale you wander into; the other is the crown that knows it's a crown.

Vienna took our breath a grander way. Gold and mirrors at Schönbrunn, Maria Theresa's ceilings, the love story of Franz Joseph and his Sisi, six hundred years of empire laid out on the walls — and then a Gothic cathedral at dusk that left us without a single word. And it has the majesty that suits a palace, because Vienna was built to be a capital of emperors, and it knows it.

So we won't choose. Prague is beauty found — a thousand years that simply happen to take your breath away. Vienna is beauty commissioned — every gilded inch built on purpose to say an empire lived here. Loving them differently isn't indecision. It's just having a heart big enough for both.

Ask us again tomorrow and the answer will have moved. That is the quiet gift of a journey like this one: you don't come home with a single favourite — you come home with a heart full of them, and you carry them all at once.
