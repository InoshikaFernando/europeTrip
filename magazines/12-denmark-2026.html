Who we are,
why we travel.

We're the Munasinghe-Fernandos — two parents and three children who love to see the world together. My husband and I share one dream: to travel to as many countries as we possibly can, as a family.

It's never easy — booking flights, trains and rooms for five is its own small adventure, and we know we can't reach everywhere. But from every country we do get to, we try our very best to see as much as we can, and to bring the children along for all of it. This magazine is where we keep it — one issue for every country, in the order we reach them.

No. 12 · Welcome
Welcome to
hygge.

Copenhagen was a one-night stop on the way to the sister visit in Gothenburg. We flew in from Amsterdam on a Sunday, dropped our bags at the Danhostel, and gave the city one big, sunny afternoon. In it we walked Nyhavn, took the canal-boat cruise, found Bishop Absalon on his horse, and let the children loose in Tivoli with clouds of pink candyfloss.

But the day really began at the airport. We had come up from the Netherlands; my sister and her family, and our parents, came the other way — by train from Sweden — and we all met on the arrivals concourse. I hugged my sister for the first time in four years. Aviann could barely wait to be introduced to her cousin, and the children were fast friends almost at once.

The city is small — 660,000 people, 380 km of bicycle lanes, a cycling rate of 62% against Amsterdam's 38%. Copenhagen has been voted the world's happiest city more often than anywhere else, and you feel why within ten minutes: it is clean, it is calm, the colours are bright but never loud, and people smile at you on the bus.

This issue is a short one. We were here for a single golden afternoon — and a little "I ❤ Copenhagen" postcard magnet came with us to every stop — held up to match each iconic view, the way we like to collect a place.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · NYHAVN · DAY 25
A canal of
painted houses.
Where the merchants' harbour learned contentment

The name says everything: København, the merchants' harbour — a Viking-age landing where the herring were fat and the sound between the islands funnelled every ship bound for the Baltic. Nyhavn itself — "New Harbour" — was dug by Swedish prisoners of war in the 1670s and worked as a sailors' quay for three centuries. Hans Christian Andersen lived in three of these houses at different points in his life. Restored and pedestrianised in the 1980s, it is now the most photographed view in Denmark.

Our first afternoon began where every Copenhagen story does — at the row of painted 17th-century townhouses, and out onto the water on the canal boat.
Sun 2 Aug 2026Nyhavn · Inner HarbourCanal cruise + quay
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
Copenhagen's inner harbour from the water — the long quay of gabled houses, the masts of Nyhavn's old wooden ships, and a low footbridge over the channel. This is the harbour the whole city grew up around.

We queued for the canal boat and slid out under the low bridges, the harbour opening up around us and the old wooden ships rocking at their moorings, while the children trailed their hands in the water and argued over who could spot the most flags. The light was clean, the water calm, the whole city the colour of a paintbox.

More bikes than cars, more parks than shopping malls, and the colour-coded waterfront of Nyhavn.

Back on the quay we ate an early dinner at a canal-side table, watched the tour boats come and go, and let the afternoon stretch. It is touristy, and none of that matters once the light turns gold on those façades.

TIVOLI GARDENS · DAY 25
Candyfloss and
the Flying Trunk.
The pleasure garden that taught Disney

Tivoli opened on 15 August 1843, when the showman Georg Carstensen talked King Christian VIII into the land just outside the ramparts — "when the people are amusing themselves, they do not think about politics." It is the world's second-oldest amusement park still running. Hans Christian Andersen was a regular, and the gardens fed straight into his tales — one ride here is still named Den Flyvende Kuffert, "The Flying Trunk," after his story. And in 1951 a visiting Walt Disney wandered Tivoli, took notes on its lights and music, and carried the feeling home.

By late afternoon we were inside the gardens — the Chinese Tower mirrored in the lake, the Dæmonen coaster looping behind it, and the thing the children had been promised all day: pink clouds of candyfloss bigger than their heads.
Sun 2 Aug 2026Tivoli · city centreAfternoon · rides + treats
Photo · Munasinghe-Fernando
The heart of Tivoli — the Chinese Tower mirrored in the lake, the Dæmonen coaster looping behind, and swans drifting past the terrace pavilions in the late afternoon sun.

Tivoli after a hundred and eighty years of practice is exactly what you'd hope: manicured gardens, fairy-tale pavilions, the smell of roasted almonds, small shrieks carried from the rides. We queued for the gentle ones, watched the older two brave the fast ones, and walked past Den Flyvende Kuffert, its blue-and-white front lit like a storybook.

When the people are amusing themselves, they do not think about politics. — Georg Carstensen, 1843

The candyfloss did not survive long. Neither did the children's energy — which was exactly the plan, with a single short morning and a train to catch tomorrow.

A photo essay · The Old Town
Bishop Absalon's city.
On Foot · Højbro Plads
Between the harbour and Tivoli we crossed the old town on foot — over Højbro, past Absalon on his horse, under the copper spires that give the skyline its green crown.
One bishop, one building king — and one magnet

Copenhagen has two founders. Bishop Absalon raised the first fortress on the islet of Slotsholmen in 1167, and his statue has ridden over Højbro Plads since 1902. Five centuries later Christian IV, Denmark's great building king, gave the city its green-spired skyline — Rosenborg, the Round Tower, the Stock Exchange's dragon-tail spire. We didn't have time to climb them all — but we did what we always do: we bought a single "I ❤ Copenhagen" postcard magnet, a little tile printed with the city's icons — the Little Mermaid, Tivoli, the palaces — and at each place we held it up to match the real thing. It is how we like to collect a memory of everywhere we go: one iconic view at a time.

REFLECTIONS · DENMARK
A sister at the gate,
painted houses,
pink candyfloss.

The magnet and the pastries were never quite the point. Copenhagen was where the family came back together — my sister and her family and our parents down by train from Sweden, us up from the Netherlands, all of us meeting at the airport after four years apart. The cousins were inseparable within minutes. That is the part we will keep.

Around it, the calmest, sunniest stop of the trip — Nyhavn from the water, Absalon on his horse, and Tivoli with the children trailing clouds of candyfloss. The "I ❤ Copenhagen" postcard magnet we bought on the quay ended up in every photo, held up to match the real thing — the way we like to collect a place.

Because there is plenty left. The Little Mermaid, the palaces, Tivoli lit up after dark, Strøget under Christmas lights — reasons enough to return, probably in winter, when the Danes do hygge properly.

Favourite moment
Meeting my sister at the airport after four years — and the cousins becoming friends before we'd left the terminal.
Our tradition
An "I ❤ Copenhagen" postcard magnet, held up to match each iconic view — how we collect a memory of every place.
