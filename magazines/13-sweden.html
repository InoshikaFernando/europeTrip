No. 13 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
family.

After three weeks of cities, monuments, museums and history pages, we stepped off the X2000 at Gothenburg on a Monday afternoon — Copenhagen behind us, the Øresund Bridge behind us — and there she was on the platform: the sister we had not seen in two years. This was the family stop.

There was nothing to tick off here. No Forbidden City, no Colosseum, no Eiffel Tower — only a small house in a quiet Gothenburg suburb, a garden, three cousins waiting for our three, and summer evenings that refuse to go dark before 23:00.

It is the warmest issue we've made. No historical features, no queues. Just family, and fika — the Swedish ritual of coffee and a cinnamon bun — and one boat out to an archipelago island, where we ate herring and rye bread on a granite rock by the sea.

— The Munasinghes, 2026

A boat,
a rock,
two slices of rye.
None of the 20-plus islands in Gothenburg's southern archipelago allow a single car. We rode the ferry out to Vrångö, walked its granite shore, and ate herring and rye bread on a rock three billion years old.
Tue 4 Aug 2026Saltholmen → Vrångö4 hrs · ferry + 6 km island walk
📷 images/sweden/day-27-vrango-archipelago.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
Vrångö island in the southern Gothenburg archipelago — 350 residents, no cars, pine forest, granite shore, summer huts.

Scatter twenty islands the size of small farms across cold North Sea water, leave them ten thousand years, forbid cars, and you have Gothenburg's southern archipelago. You take the ferry from Saltholmen, step off at the harbour of a one-village island, and walk. The granite underfoot is three billion years old, glacier-smoothed, warm in the August sun; pine covers the higher ground, and the summer huts — red cottages with white window frames — line the whole path.

Sweden made peace with itself in 1815, and has not raised a sword since. The country has spent its energy on forests, design, and quiet.

My sister spread the picnic on a flat granite outcrop above the water: rye bread (rågbröd, dense and dark), pickled herring (sill — mustard, dill, onion), boiled new potatoes, hard-boiled eggs, slices of Västerbotten cheese, lemon-curd cake to finish. The children ran ahead and came back with fists full of sea-smoothed glass. The wind swung south. Nobody hurried. We were happy.

★ Haga District

Wooden houses, 1648

Gothenburg's oldest quarter, and it wears its age well: two-storey wooden houses from the 17th and 18th centuries, cobblestones underfoot, a café in every doorway. On Haga Nygata the famous cinnamon buns arrive the size of a dinner plate.

★ Fika

The national pause

Fika is the Swedish coffee-and-something-sweet break, kept faithfully around ten and again at three — a verb as much as a ritual. In two days we managed four.

The X2000 high-speed train crosses the Øresund Bridge — 8 km of bridge from Copenhagen to Malmö, then 4 km of tunnel under the strait — and carries on north, Malmö to Gothenburg in 2h 40m.

HERO PHOTO · TBD
The Øresund Bridge — 8 km of cable-stayed bridge connecting Denmark and Sweden, opened 2000. Featured in the TV crime drama "The Bridge".

The Øresund Bridge is one of Europe's great feats of engineering, thrown across the strait that divides Denmark from Sweden. The half nearer Sweden rides high above the water; the half nearer Denmark dives into a tunnel under the seabed and surfaces on Peberholm, an island built for the purpose. Sixteen kilometres in all. The train slips through without fuss — a slight sway in the wind, container ships sliding past below.

A bridge, a tunnel, an artificial island — Europe's longest road-and-rail bridge connection.

We boarded the X2000 at Copenhagen Central at 13:00. The instant the train's GPS chimed mid-water, Aviann shouted "We just left Denmark!"; Avisha leaned to the glass to photograph the container ships far below; Avin slept through the whole crossing. At Malmö the line swung north, and by 16:40 we rolled into Gothenburg Central, where my sister stood waiting with three children and one very large dog. The reunion was loud.

REFLECTIONS · SWEDEN
Family, fika,
and a granite rock
in the August sun.

Sweden was the one country we gave no schedule to. No history page to fit in, no museum to pre-book — only two days at my sister's, our three children and hers running the garden ragged, and far too many coffees across far too many fikas. It was perfect.

We will remember the granite warm under our hands on Vrångö, Aviann and her cousin raising a fort out of pine cones, and the long bright evenings that would not go dark until almost 23:00.

Favourite moment
The Vrångö picnic — warm granite, rye bread and herring, the cousins running on ahead.
Hardest moment
The goodbye on Tuesday evening. Sister and her kids drove us to the airport. We all cried.
If we come back…
A summer at the sister's. Stockholm. Lapland and the Northern Lights in winter.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe
