No. 13 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
family.

Sweden was the family stop. After three weeks of cities, monuments, museums, and history pages, we arrived in Gothenburg on a Monday afternoon — the X2000 train from Copenhagen, crossing the Øresund Bridge — to spend two days with the sister we had not seen in two years.

There were no monuments to see here. No Forbidden City, no Colosseum, no Eiffel Tower. There was a small house in a quiet Gothenburg suburb, a garden, three cousins for our kids, and the long Swedish summer evenings that don't get dark until 23:00.

This issue is the warmest in tone of any so far. There are no historical features. There is family. There is fika (the Swedish coffee + cinnamon bun tradition). There is one boat trip to an archipelago island where we ate herring and rye bread on a granite rock by the sea.

— The Munasinghes, 2026

Gothenburg's southern archipelago has 20+ islands, none with cars. We caught a ferry to Vrångö, walked the granite shore, and ate herring with rye bread sitting on a 3-billion-year-old rock.

HERO PHOTO · TBD
Vrångö island in the southern Gothenburg archipelago — 350 residents, no cars, pine forest, granite shore, summer huts.

The Gothenburg archipelago is what happens when 20 islands the size of small farms are scattered across cold North Sea water for ten thousand years. None of the southern islands allow cars. You take a ferry from Saltholmen, you arrive at the harbour of a one-village island, and you walk. The granite is 3 billion years old, smoothed by glaciers, warm in the August sun. The pine forest covers the higher ground. The summer huts — red cottages with white window frames — are everywhere along the path.

Sweden made peace with itself in 1815, and has not raised a sword since. The country has spent its energy on forests, design, and quiet.

My sister brought a picnic. Rye bread (rågbröd, dense and dark), pickled herring (sill, in three flavours: mustard, dill, onion), boiled new potatoes, hard-boiled eggs, slices of Västerbotten cheese, and lemon-curd cake for dessert. We sat on a flat granite outcrop overlooking the water. The kids ran ahead and came back with handfuls of sea-smoothed glass. The wind shifted to the south. We were happy.

The Dutch-built gateway to the west
Gothenburg was a deliberate act of statecraft. In 1621 King Gustavus Adolphus chartered the city on Sweden's rugged west coast — a foothold on the narrow strip of shore that gave the kingdom its only real window onto the North Sea, safely clear of the Danish-controlled sound. To build it he imported Dutch engineers, who laid out the new town as they knew best, with straight canals, ramparts and drainage: a slice of Holland transplanted to Nordic granite. Traces of that geometry still shape the old centre today.
The sea remained its fortune. In the eighteenth century the Swedish East India Company sailed from these quays to Canton, returning with tea, silk and porcelain that made Gothenburg rich. Later it became a city of shipyards and of Volvo, whose name and factories are woven into local identity. Beyond the harbour lies its wild reward: a scatter of rocky, car-free islands — the southern archipelago, where the west coast dissolves into sea.

The X2000 high-speed train crosses the Øresund Bridge — 8 km of bridge from Copenhagen to Malmö, then 4 km of tunnel under the strait. From Malmö it runs north to Gothenburg in 2h 40m.

The Øresund Bridge — 8 km of cable-stayed bridge connecting Denmark and Sweden, opened 2000. Featured in the TV crime drama "The Bridge".

The Øresund Bridge is one of the great pieces of European engineering. It crosses the Øresund strait — the narrow stretch of water that divides Denmark from Sweden. For its eastern half (the part nearer Sweden) it is an above-water bridge. For its western half (nearer Denmark) it dives into a tunnel under the seabed, surfacing on the artificial island of Peberholm. The total crossing is 16 km. The train slips through smoothly. You feel the bridge sway slightly in the wind. You see container ships passing below.

A bridge, a tunnel, an artificial island — Europe's longest road-and-rail bridge connection.

We took the X2000 from Copenhagen Central at 13:00. Aviann sat next to the window for the bridge crossing and announced "We just left Denmark!" the second the train's GPS notification chimed. Avisha photographed the container ships from above. Avin slept through the bridge entirely. At Malmö Central the train turned north. By 16:40 we were at Gothenburg Central, where my sister was waiting with three kids and one large dog. The reunion was loud.

REFLECTIONS · SWEDEN
Family, fika,
and a granite rock
in the August sun.

Sweden was the only country on this trip where we did not have a tight schedule. No history page to fit in. No museum to pre-book. Just two days at my sister's house, with our three kids playing in her garden with her three kids, and us drinking too many coffees in too many fikas. It was perfect.

We will remember the granite warm under our hands on Vrångö. We will remember Aviann and her cousin building a fort out of pine cones. We will remember the long bright evenings that did not get dark until almost 23:00.

Favourite moment
Vrångö island picnic. Granite, rye bread, herring, and the cousins running ahead.
Hardest moment
The goodbye on Tuesday evening. Sister and her kids drove us to the airport. We all cried.
If we come back…
A summer at the sister's. Stockholm. Lapland and the Northern Lights in winter.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe
