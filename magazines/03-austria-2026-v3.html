A family memoir — told in Inoshi's words

Three cities, small enough to drive between,
large enough to remember.

We came into Austria by road from Bavaria and left it five days later through Brno on the way to Vienna. In between were a lake village the whole world photographs, an evening cathedral that stopped us mid-sentence, and — first — a garden I had been waiting thirty years to stand in. This is what actually happened.

Chapter One · Salzburg

The girl who played Maria

Thirty years of a film I knew by heart — and then the real garden gate.

At the top of the Mirabell steps, I lifted my arm and pointed up at the fortress on its hill, and for a moment I wasn't a mother of three in the midday heat — I was a girl again, in a living room on the other side of the world, acting out a film I had watched more than a hundred times.

When I was six or seven, my sister and I used to perform The Sound of Music for hours. I was always Maria; she was Liesl; a row of dolls stood in for the rest of the von Trapp children. I knew every song. And every single time, in my head, I was already here — already in Austria. Every time, I was in Austria in my mind. To arrive in the real Salzburg now, decades later, with my husband beside me and three children of my own, was a dream I had never quite dared to dream aloud.

The truth is we did Salzburg on tired legs. It was already noon when we reached the city — behind us were Beijing, a red-eye flight and a morning in Munich — and Aviann, eight years old, was flagging hardest. So we kept it gentle. We walked into the Mirabell Gardens, to the Pegasus fountain and the beds where Maria and the children once skipped and sang, and there I did the small things I had only ever pretended: the little splash at the fountain, the pause at the garden gate, the moves I had copied as a girl. This time the water was real and our children were the ones watching.

We never climbed to the Hohensalzburg itself; we only saw it from below. Mozart's birthplace stayed a doorway we walked past without stopping. We were already running two hours behind, with a church to reach before the day was gone, so we wandered the streets we could and let that be enough. It was hot — the kind of heat that makes everything an effort — and nothing in the world feels better on a day like that than a cup of ice-cream. So we bought one each for three weary children. After the morning they'd given us, they'd earned it. Not a checklist — a stolen hour in the one place I had waited my whole life to see.

We found the ice-cream down one of the narrow old-town lanes — and here the lanes really are narrow. Salzburg's old town is wedged onto a thin strip of flat ground between the Mönchsberg cliff on one side and the Salzach river on the other, with nowhere to spread outward. So the medieval city grew upward and inward instead: tall houses leaning close together, the streets kept narrow, the sky a bright ribbon far overhead. We wheeled Avin down the middle of one of them, his cup held carefully in both hands.

And there was the Residenzbrunnen, the grand horse-fountain in the old town — and in the film, this is the one Maria splashes as she sings that she has confidence. Standing in front of it now, I found that I did too. Confident I could see this whole trip through, with my three little musketeers beside me.

Travel Reality

We saw the fortress only from the valley floor. We passed Mozart's front door without going in. We were two hours behind before we'd really begun. None of it mattered: the garden was the whole point, and the garden we reached.

Chapter Two · Burg Altpernstein

A closed city, and a castle with no lift

The night Austria quietly taught us its opening hours.

Mass finished at a quarter to eight, and we stepped out into a problem we hadn't seen coming: a town that had already rolled up its shutters for the weekend.

It was Saturday, and in Austria the shops close at six. Our plan to buy groceries for dinner had expired two hours earlier, while we were still in the pews — a detail nobody tells you until a tired family with three hungry children is standing on a quiet street at dusk. We found a restaurant just before it, too, closed for the night, and ordered pizza and pasta almost at the door. It cost more than the supermarket dinner we'd planned. After the day we'd had, nobody was complaining.

Then, at last, the castle. We were staying at Burg Altpernstein, a genuine hilltop fortress — and a genuine fortress, it turns out, has no elevator. We carried the heavy bags up to the third floor by stair, breath by breath, and found we couldn't even mind: you don't keep a castle's medieval soul by drilling a lift shaft through the middle of it. A hot bath washed the long day away, and we fell into bed more tired than we had been at any point on the trip.

What we didn't yet know was that the bath would do more than wash off a long day. It would hand us the next morning completely new.

Chapter Three · Hallstatt

The day nothing went to plan

The salt mine was shut. The lanes were packed. And it was still the best morning we'd had.

We woke at five o'clock — all of us, even the children, and for once wide awake and clear-headed. The jet lag that had dragged at us since Beijing had simply lifted in the night, as though the castle had taken it.

We were at the breakfast buffet a quarter of an hour before it opened, and when it did we fell on it happily: cheese and ham, salads and dips, fruit juice and cereal, far more than we could finish. It was so generous it would carry us, as it turned out, clean through the day. Full and awake, we drove out to Hallstatt and reached it around half past ten, early enough that the fierce midday heat hadn't yet arrived.

And then the mountain turned us down. The salt mine — the oldest in the world, the thing we'd come for — was closed. So was the Skywalk viewpoint above it. We made one more subtraction by choice: the Bone House, the little chapel of painted skulls, felt a step too far for a four-year-old, so we spared Avin the crypt. Hallstatt, meanwhile, was doing what Hallstatt does — the narrow lanes ran shoulder to shoulder, one of the most crowded small places on earth.

So we let the plan go and took the village on its own gentler terms. We walked the waterfront, and then we took a boat out onto the Hallstättersee — which turns out to be the finest way to see the place at all: from the water, in slow motion, the pastel houses and the church spire stacking up the cliff behind. We never ate in Hallstatt; that enormous breakfast stayed with us all day. But on the drive back we finally tried the dish Austria is famous for — a proper Wiener Schnitzel, golden and thin and bigger than its plate.

The children spent the morning behind their own little toy cameras — Aviann's a bright plastic pink, the boys' slung on lanyards around their necks — photographing a version of the day only they will ever have seen. It was not the day we had planned. With the salt mine shut, it was a better one.

And Hallstatt gave me something I hadn't expected. Long before we ever came, I had carried a picture of Europe in my head — calm, and green, and blue. That isn't really Europe, as it turns out; most of it is busier and grander than that. But Hallstatt never broke that picture. It was exactly the Europe I had imagined: green and blue, and quiet, and still.

Would We Go Back?

Yes — and we already know what for. The Salzwelten salt mine, and the Skywalk panorama above the lake: both closed the morning we came, and both, as far as this family is concerned, still waiting for us.

Chapter Four · Vienna

Four o'clock at Schönbrunn

A detour into memory, a palace against the clock, and a cathedral that left us with nothing to say.

We left Prague at eight in the morning and pointed the car south — not by the fastest road, but by the one that ran through Brno, because twelve years ago Avi had come to that city once for work, and wanted to stand in its streets again before we grew any older.

That is the kind of detour a family makes and a guidebook never would: kilometres added to a long driving day for the sake of a memory. From Brno we carried on to Vienna, and it was already four o'clock by the time we passed under the gates of Schönbrunn. Four o'clock bought us the palace but not its gardens. We spent two hours indoors instead, room after gilded room — the Great Gallery where the children tipped their heads right back to stare up at the painted ceiling — and stepped out again at six with the light already softening. The famous gardens we saw only from the terrace, stretching away to the Gloriette on its ridge. We left the climb for next time.

Schönbrunn was the first palace any of us had ever set foot in, and even at a half-run its long butter-yellow front stopped us in the forecourt. There was really only one word for it, and all five of us were thinking the same one: wow.

The truth is we arrived flustered. We were later than we'd meant to be and worried the palace would stop letting people in, so we parked in a rush and half-ran for the entrance — and only inside did we realise we'd left Avin's stroller behind at the car. There was no fetching it, and they allow no water in the state rooms either, so somewhere among all that gilt our four-year-old grew thirsty and heavy-legged. Avinesh swung him up onto his shoulders and carried him from there, room after room. Avin saw the rest of Schönbrunn from up above everyone's heads.

The Kids

Avisha (11) and Aviann (8) documented the entire palace on their little toy cameras — Avisha most of all, already dreaming up the YouTube channel he wants to make. Between them they captured a Schönbrunn no guidebook has: every chandelier, every ceiling, every gilded door, filmed from about four feet off the ground.

What stays with me, though, isn't any single treasure in those rooms — it's the size of them. Hall after gilded hall, walled in mirrors and crystal, each one seeming grander than the last, and so vast you could only guess they were built for one thing: music, and hundreds of guests, and dancing. We were right. The largest were the palace's ballrooms, where the court once danced until dawn — and where, long after the Habsburgs were gone, the powers of Europe waltzed their way through the Congress of Vienna.

One name hangs over all of it: Maria Theresa. She was the only woman ever to rule the Habsburg lands in her own right, and it was she who turned Schönbrunn from a hunting lodge into this — room after room of it — and raised sixteen children inside these walls. Standing under all that gold, I had the thought I think everyone has here: you only build on this scale if you believe your family will go on living in it forever. They didn't; the empire was gone by 1918. But the rooms are all still here, still waiting, as though the family might walk back in at any moment.

Inside those rooms we met the story that would follow us for the rest of the day — Emperor Franz Joseph and Elisabeth, the reluctant young empress the whole country still calls Sisi. There is a neatness to it that I noticed at once: one woman's story making Austria vivid for me here, just as another woman's story had in Salzburg four days before.

By the evening Avin had his stroller back — reunited with it at the car, and very pleased with himself after a whole palace spent on his father's shoulders. We walked into the old town toward the Stephansdom, past the State Opera, the green-and-gold front of the Secession, and the horse-drawn fiaker cabs waiting in their long row. With that many horses, every so often the evening carried a certain reminder — and Aviann, eight years old, put the question to half the Graben: "What is that disgusting smell?"

On the Graben we stopped at the Pestsäule — the Plague Column, a great tangle of gilded clouds and saints that Emperor Leopold had raised to thank God when the plague of 1679 at last let the city go. I stood under it and thought that we had our own version of that gratitude. This whole journey — all of it — felt to me like a chance to stop and say thank you, God, for the experience.

And then we went inside the cathedral, and the day ran out of words. Honestly, I had no words — the dark Gothic height of it, the hush, the great tiled roof we'd seen catching the sun from outside. Some places you describe. That one you just stand in.

Prague or Vienna? One a beauty you stumble on; the other a beauty built on purpose. We never did settle it — and decided a family's heart is big enough for both.
Reflections

What we carried out

Austria is small — you can drive its width in an afternoon — and it kept surprising us anyway. What we'll carry home isn't the thing we planned for in any of the three cities. It's me at a garden gate I'd waited thirty years to reach. It's the shimmer of the Hallstättersee from a little boat, on the morning the mine was shut. And it's a cathedral in Vienna that left the two of us with nothing to say.

Worth Remembering
  • Our favourite moment — the Mirabell gate, pointing our children up at the fortress, finishing a sentence I'd started as a six-year-old.
  • The hardest — that first night: shops shut at six, dinner nearly lost, and the long haul up a liftless castle in the dark.
  • If we come back — the salt mine and the panorama we never got into. We owe Hallstatt a second visit.

The postcard is still in a bag somewhere — the winter Hallstatt we held up against the summer one. A picture of a picture, of a place we'll be back to finish properly. That feels about right for a family that drove an hour out of its way just to stand in a street Avi once knew.
