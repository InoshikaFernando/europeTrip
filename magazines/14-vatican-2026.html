No. 14 · A Letter from the Road
A whole
new world.

We crossed a border this morning almost without noticing. One moment we were in Rome; then we walked through Bernini's great curved colonnade — and we were somewhere else entirely. A different country. The smallest in the world. And it truly felt like a whole new world.

Vatican City is barely half a square kilometre, with around 800 residents — yet it has its own head of state, its own flag, stamps, post office and army (the Swiss Guard), and it is the beating heart of a faith shared by 1.3 billion people. A tiny nation wrapped inside Rome, and a universe of its own.

We spent the morning inside its walls: St Peter's Basilica and the tomb beneath it, the Vatican Museums, and Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel — before crossing back into Rome for the Holy Stairs and the Colosseum at dusk.

This issue is short and dense — like the country itself.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · SISTINE CHAPEL · DAY 14
A ceiling
painted lying
on his back.
Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling between 1508 and 1512. He hated every minute of it. He preferred sculpture. He had not painted in fresco before. The result is one of the most important artworks in human history.
Wed 22 Jul 2026Sistine Chapel, Vatican Museums45 min · silent contemplation
The Vatican Museums on the way in — corridor after gilded corridor of tapestries, maps and frescoes, all funnelling toward one small, silent room.

You queue. You snake through the galleries. You pass tapestries and maps and ceilings and frescoes and statues and reliquaries until your legs ache and your eyes refuse to focus. Then a guard hisses silenzio, you push through a heavy curtain — and you are inside the Sistine Chapel. And you forget everything. You look up.

I have already a wife who is too much for me, and that is this art, which torments me. — Michelangelo on the Sistine ceiling, 1512.

The ceiling runs 40.5 metres by 14, carries more than 300 figures and nine central scenes from Genesis, and Michelangelo painted almost all of it refusing assistants. He built his own scaffolding to work standing — not, as the myth goes, on his back, but with his head tilted up and paint dripping into his eyes for four years. He left with a ruined neck and a bitter sonnet about the whole ordeal. Then he finished, and the world's ceilings were never judged the same way again.

Why there's no photo of the ceiling here
Photography is forbidden inside the Sistine Chapel — a guard hisses the moment a phone appears. So the one picture we carried out is the one in our heads: The Creation of Adam, God's finger a hair's breadth from Adam's. Some things you're meant to keep only in memory.

ST PETER'S BASILICA · DAY 14
The world's
largest church.
★ Built 1506–1626

120 years

The basilica took 120 years and the hand of nearly every great architect of the Renaissance — Bramante, Raphael, Michelangelo, Bernini. The dome is Michelangelo's; the great bronze baldachin over the high altar (left), its twisted Solomonic columns four storeys tall, is Bernini's. The result is simply impossible to take in.

Michelangelo's Pietà — carved from a single block of Carrara marble at 24, the only work he ever signed, behind glass since a 1972 attack.
Straight up into Michelangelo's dome — 136 metres to the lantern, the letters of the frieze each two metres tall.
The world's smallest country
Vatican City is a nation you can walk across in twenty minutes. At just 0.44 square kilometres and around 800 residents, it is the smallest sovereign state on earth — yet its ruler, the Pope, is the spiritual leader of nearly 1.4 billion Catholics worldwide. For over a thousand years the popes governed the sprawling Papal States across central Italy, until a newly unified Italy seized Rome in 1870 and the popes shut themselves away as self-declared "prisoners in the Vatican."
The stand-off lasted until 1929, when the Lateran Treaty carved out this tiny independent enclave and made the Pope a head of state once more. Its heart is St Peter's Basilica, the largest church in the world, raised between 1506 and 1626 over what tradition holds is the tomb of St Peter himself — the fisherman-apostle and first pope. Even the guards are historic: the Swiss Guard, in their Renaissance stripes, have protected the pontiff since 1506.

ANCIENT ROME · DAY 14
Out of the smallest country,
into the oldest city.
We walked back out through Bernini's colonnade and across the river — and traded the newest wonder of the Renaissance for the greatest ruin of the ancient world. From the Pope's basilica to the emperors' arena in a single afternoon.
Wed 22 Jul 2026Colosseo · Arco di CostantinoEvening · ancient Rome on foot
All five of us in front of the Colosseum — nearly two thousand years old, and a thousand years older than the church we had stood inside that morning.

The Colosseum was begun by the emperor Vespasian around AD 72 and opened by his son Titus in AD 80 with a hundred days of games. Four tiers of travertine arches held between 50,000 and 80,000 spectators, filed in and out through eighty numbered gates — the same idea as a modern stadium, because this is more or less where the idea was invented. Beneath the floor ran the hypogeum, a warren of tunnels, cages and lifts that delivered animals and gladiators up into the arena.

We were inside St Peter's this morning — and now we're standing where the Romans watched the games. All in one day.

A short walk away stands the Arch of Constantine, raised in AD 315 to mark the emperor's victory at the Milvian Bridge — the largest Roman triumphal arch still standing, and the one that would decide that the empire, and eventually all of Europe, turned Christian. The children, who had been reverent and quiet in the Vatican all morning, were suddenly loud again, tearing between the arches in the warm evening light. Which felt exactly right.

REFLECTIONS · VATICAN CITY
One day,
one chapel,
one ceiling that
changed everything.

Vatican Day was the most emotionally dense day of the whole trip. The Sistine Chapel produced a 30-second moment of complete silence in all five of us. The Basilica is so large you cannot get your head around it. The Pietà is so beautiful it makes you angry that someone carved it at twenty-three. And then, in the very same day, the Colosseum — older than the church by a thousand years — rising out of the evening while the children ran between its arches.

We will not forget the Sistine Chapel ceiling. The kids will not forget it either. Avisha, eleven, said quietly afterwards: "I want to learn how to draw like that." We bought her a sketchbook.

Favourite moment
Sistine Chapel ceiling, the silent 30 seconds before the guard hissed silenzio again.
Hardest moment
Queue management. 4 hours total at the museums. We pre-booked but the lines were still endless.
If we come back…
A Wednesday Papal Audience in St Peter's Square. Caravaggio paintings in Rome's churches.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
