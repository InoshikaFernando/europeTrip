A letter from the road

Who we are, why we travel

Two parents, three children, and one shared dream.

We're the Munasinghe-Fernandos - two parents and three children who love to see the world together. Avi and I share one dream: to travel to as many countries as we possibly can, as a family, while the children are still small enough to want to come with us.

It's never easy. Booking flights and trains and rooms for five is its own small adventure, and we know we can't reach everywhere. But from every country we do get to, we try our very best to see as much as we can - and to hold on to it properly afterwards. This magazine is where we keep all of it.

So these pages aren't a guidebook. They're ours: what we actually saw, what went wrong, what the children said, and the small moments we never want to forget. Told, this time, in my words.

- Inoshi, for all five of us · 2026

Hungary · 16-17 July 2026
A family memoir - told in Inoshi's words

Two cities on either side of a river,
and one long day to walk them both.

We left Vienna at half past seven on a Thursday morning and drove east into Hungary. In Budapest we parked the car in Pest and gave the city everything we had left in our legs: the great dome of St Stephen's in the morning, then across the Chain Bridge and up the little funicular to the royal hill of Buda, and at the end of it all, a boat on the Danube as the lights came on. The next morning we were on the road again, bound for Italy. One day was all we had. This is what we did with it.

Chapter One · Pest

A basilica for the first king

Ninety-six metres of dome, and our youngest climbing every last step of it.

We left the car in Pest, on the flat east bank of the river, and walked the few streets to Szent István tér. Our first stop in Budapest was a church, and before we were done with it we would be standing on top of its dome.

It is named for István - Stephen - the king who made Hungary Christian. He was crowned in the year 1000, a thousand years and more before we arrived, and within fifty years of his death the Church had made him a saint. In this city he is never far away; we would meet him again in bronze that same afternoon, on the far side of the river.

The basilica itself is far younger than its king. It took from 1851 to 1905 to build, and in 1868 the half-finished dome fell in and had to be started again. When it was done it stood exactly 96 metres high - level with the dome of the Parliament across town, and by an old rule nothing in Budapest may stand taller. The number is a salute to the year 896, when the Magyars first came into this land.

Inside, the grey stone gives way to caramel and gold: marble on every wall, gilding wherever there isn't marble, and at the far end of the nave a white statue of Stephen himself standing over the high altar. High above, the cupola is ringed with windows, and the light from them falls a very long way before it reaches the floor.

We didn't only look up at that dome. We went up it - a lift for most of the way, and then the last of it on stairs, out onto the walkway that rings the top. And Avin, who is four, made it all the way to the very top.

The children had their own little cameras with them all day, and they used them. Between the three of them they photographed nearly everything we saw in Budapest, from the first church of the morning to the last lights on the river.

In a side chapel, behind glass, is the most precious thing in the building: the Szent Jobb, the Holy Right - the right hand of Stephen himself, kept for nearly a thousand years. When his tomb was opened in 1083, the story goes, the body had gone to dust but the hand was whole.

It has travelled a long way since. For centuries it was kept in Ragusa - today's Dubrovnik - until Maria Theresa had it brought home to Buda in 1771. She is the same empress who built Schönbrunn, the palace we had walked through in Vienna only days before. On 20 August every year, Hungary's national day, the hand is still carried through the streets of Budapest at the head of a procession.

Chapter Two · Castle Hill, Buda

A castle built only to be looked from

Over the river on foot, up the hill by funicular, and a stroller up a great many steps.

From Pest we walked to the river and crossed it on the Chain Bridge, on foot. It was the first bridge ever to join the two halves of the city, opened in 1849, when Buda and Pest were still two separate towns; before it, the only way across was a boat or a pontoon bridge that had to be taken down every winter. On the Buda side the hill rises almost straight from the water, and we let the little funicular carry us up it.

The Budavári Sikló has been climbing that slope since 1870 - two small wooden cars on one cable, one going up as the other comes down. At the top we turned north along the hill, towards a row of white towers.

Fisherman's Bastion looks a thousand years old and is barely older than a hundred. Frigyes Schulek built it between 1895 and 1902, not as a fortress but as a terrace to look out from, raised along the old walls for Hungary's millennium celebrations. Its seven pointed towers stand for the seven Magyar tribes who arrived in 896. The name is older than the stone: by tradition this stretch of the medieval wall was defended by the fishermen's guild from the town below.

Below the open terrace runs a covered walk of pale arches and short, thick columns, built in the round-arched Romanesque style of Hungary's first Christian kings - a millennium monument dressed in the architecture of the nation's own beginnings. Every arch frames something: a tower, a lantern, a slice of the river. It is the most photographed spot on the hill, and this is ours.

And from the rail, the whole of Pest lay flat across the water, with the Parliament stretched along the far bank. It was built from 1885 to 1904, its long front and pinnacles modelled on the Houses of Parliament in London, and its dome, like the basilica's, rises exactly 96 metres.

Everything up here comes with steps. Avin's stroller came up the hill with us, and the Bastion has a great many flights of stairs between its towers, so we carried that stroller up them, flight after flight, in the midday heat. Of everything in Budapest, that was the hardest part of the day.

Between the Bastion and the church next door stands the king we had met that morning. This bronze was made by Alajos Stróbl, the same sculptor who carved the white Stephen over the basilica's altar, and unveiled in 1906. He sits not as a warrior but as a founder, holding up the double-barred apostolic cross. Schulek designed the stone pedestal beneath him, with a lion at each corner - the architect of the Bastion around it, and of the restoration of the church beside it.

Chapter Three · Castle Hill, Buda

Where Sisi was crowned

A roof like embroidery, a hundred and forty-five years as a mosque, and a king found by chance.

Next to the Bastion stands the church the whole hill seems arranged around, with a tall Gothic tower and a roof of glazed tiles laid in diamonds of colour. Everyone calls it Matthias Church, after Matthias Corvinus, the Renaissance king who was married in it twice. Its real name is the Church of Our Lady, and the building we stood under went up in the 1300s.

It has not always been a church. When the Ottomans took Buda in 1541 they whitewashed the walls and made it the city's main mosque, and so it stayed for a hundred and forty-five years, until Buda was taken back in 1686. The look it wears now - the tiles, the spiky pinnacles - came from Frigyes Schulek's long restoration at the end of the 1800s, the same Schulek who built the Bastion outside.

This is also coronation ground. In 1867 Franz Joseph and his empress Elisabeth were crowned King and Queen of Hungary here, with a Mass that Franz Liszt wrote for the day. It was the same Sisi who had lived at Schönbrunn, the palace we had walked through in Vienna: there she was an empress, and in this church she became a Hungarian queen. The last Habsburg coronation of all took place here too, in 1916.

Inside, there is no plain stone at all. When Schulek restored the church, the painters Károly Lotz and Bertalan Székely covered it from floor to vault in pattern - borders along every rib, saints and Hungarian kings along the walls, a floor of patterned tile - in a medieval Hungarian style that no church had ever quite worn before. Lotz again: the man who designed the basilica's dome mosaics that same morning.

In a side chapel lies the only king buried in the church. Béla III ruled Hungary from 1172 to 1196, and in his time the kingdom was one of the richest in Europe. He was buried at Székesfehérvár, in the old royal basilica, but that church was destroyed in the Ottoman wars and its royal graves were lost. His tomb, and his queen Anna's, were found by chance during excavations in 1848, and in 1898 they were brought here and laid under this carved canopy.

Up a stair from the nave, the church keeps its treasury, and among its treasures are a copy of the Holy Crown, its little cross bent crooked, and a white marble bust of Sisi. For a thousand years Hungarians held that the authority of the kingdom lived in the Crown itself, and that a king was only truly king once it had touched his head.

Chapter Four · Castle Hill, Buda

The palace that would not stay down

Mongols, Ottomans, a world war - and a green dome younger than it looks.

At the south end of the hill is the palace. The first fortress here was raised by King Béla IV in the 1240s, straight after the Mongols had burned the towns below, and under Matthias Corvinus it grew into one of the great Renaissance courts of Europe.

Then came the Ottomans, and the siege of 1686 that won Buda back and left the palace in ruins. The Habsburgs rebuilt it larger than before; the siege of 1944-45 wrecked it again; and the dome we stood under is really a product of the rebuilding after the war. No king has lived here for more than a hundred years. The wings hold the Hungarian National Gallery now, and the Budapest History Museum.

The statue beside us in that photograph is Prince Eugene of Savoy, the general whose victories drove the Ottomans out of Hungary at the end of the 1600s. A little further along the hill, outside Sándor Palace, the President of Hungary's residence, a guard stood at his sentry box in the red, white and green.

Chapter Five · The Danube

Lángos, and the river at dusk

After a whole day on our feet, we sat down and let the city come to us.

Dinner came first, and it was lángos: Hungary's deep-fried flatbread, a round of dough the size of a plate, fried golden and eaten hot. We had three. One was rubbed with garlic and heaped with sour cream and cheese, the classic way; one was built up like a pizza, with prosciutto, mozzarella and basil; and one was for pudding, under a layer of Nutella. The name is said to come from láng, "flame", for the fire of the oven it was once baked beside.

Then, at about half past eight, we boarded a glass-roofed boat for a sunset cruise. We each had a set of headphones with the story of the city playing in our ears, and the buildings we had stood beneath that day began to slide past on both banks: the palace, the church, the white towers of the Bastion, all of them now seen from the water.

The banks of the Danube here are a UNESCO World Heritage site, and from the middle of the river it is easy to see why - palace after palace along the water, the bridges passing overhead one at a time, and green Margaret Island in the middle of the stream.

From the boat we passed under Gellért Hill, the cliff that rises straight out of the Buda bank. It is named for Gerard - Gellért - a bishop who helped Stephen bring Christianity to Hungary and who, by tradition, was killed on this hill. On the summit stands the Liberty Statue, a bronze woman holding a palm leaf over her head, put up in 1947. At its foot, right on the water, are the Rudas Baths, built by the Ottomans in the sixteenth century over the hot springs and still in use today - a legacy Vienna, which the Ottomans besieged but never took, never received.

And then the lights came on. The Parliament, which had been a pale shape across the water from the Bastion that afternoon, turned to gold from end to end - all 268 metres of it - and its reflection lay on the river beside the boat.

The day, by the numbers

By the time the boat turned back we had walked more than 21,000 steps. Avin, at four years old, walked a full five kilometres of them without his pram.

Reflections

Twenty-one thousand steps

What we carried out of Budapest.

Budapest gave us two cities in a single day. We spent the morning in flat, busy Pest, under the dome of St Stephen's; we spent the afternoon on the royal hill of Buda, among churches and white towers and a palace that has been knocked down and built up again more times than we could keep straight. Between them ran the Danube, and in the evening we finally sat down on it.

It was hot, there was a great deal of walking, and there was a stroller to carry up a great many steps. But the children carried their own cameras the whole way and photographed everything, and the smallest of them climbed to the top of the dome and then walked five kilometres of the city on his own feet.

Worth Remembering
  • Our favourite moment - Avin, four years old, climbing every last step to the top of St Stephen's dome; and the arches of Fisherman's Bastion.
  • The hardest - the midday heat on Castle Hill, and carrying the stroller up flight after flight of the Bastion's steps.
  • If we come back - the Széchenyi thermal baths we ran out of time for, the Parliament from the inside, and a slow day on Margaret Island.

We slept one night in the city. At half past seven the next morning we were back in the car, and by evening Hungary was behind us: we drove across Slovenia without stopping and on into Italy, towards Venice. Budapest was one day long, and it began and ended with a 96-metre dome: the basilica's that we climbed in the morning, and the Parliament's, lit gold across the water, at night.
