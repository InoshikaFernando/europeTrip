The Theme of the Trip · 2026
History & World Heritage
Every year, our family travels to a theme. Last year, all across Japan, it was theme parks — a summer of roller-coasters and cartoon castles. This year we turned the other way, into the deep past: historic cities and UNESCO World Heritage Sites, one country at a time. No. 11 · Welcome
Welcome to
chocolate country.

Belgium was a single day. We drove down from Amsterdam with our cousin on a Friday morning — 2 hours via the A4/E19 — and parked in central Brussels by 09:30. We had Grand Place by 10:00, the Royal Palace by 11:30, the MIM by 12:30, Atomium and Mini-Europe by 14:00 — and we were back at cousin's home in Amsterdam by 20:00. We never even found time to sit down and eat.

Brussels is the headquarters of the European Union. It is also the chocolate capital of the world — 220,000 tonnes produced per year, more than any other country. It is also home to a tiny 60-centimetre statue of a peeing boy that has been the city's mascot since the 1300s.

This issue is short. Belgium was short. But it was exactly what we needed: a fun day, a manageable city, and all of Europe shrunk into one afternoon.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · GRAND PLACE · DAY 23
A square covered
in gold.
Brussels' Grand Place is, by international consensus, one of the most beautiful squares in Europe. UNESCO since 1998. Built up in the 1690s after Louis XIV's artillery flattened it.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Grand Place / Grote Markt90 min · square + Manneken Pis
The Grand Place at 10:00 — the Hôtel de Ville (left, with its 96-metre Gothic tower), the King's House (back), and the 17th-century guild houses (right) covered in gilded statuary.

The Grand Place is a stage set. On three sides you see ornate baroque guild houses — built between 1696 and 1700 by the city's craft guilds (bakers, brewers, butchers, painters, weavers, etc.) — each one taller, gaudier, and more gilded than the next. On the fourth side stands the Hôtel de Ville, a 15th-century Gothic masterpiece with a 96-metre tower. In the middle, every two years (in even-numbered years), the square is covered with a million flower petals arranged in a giant carpet design. In odd-numbered years, like 2026, it is just stone — but the stone has been worn smooth by 700 years of trading and processions.

The capital of Europe — and the chocolate capital of the world.

A short walk from the square is the Manneken Pis — a 60cm bronze statue of a small boy urinating into a fountain. It has been here since at least 1388 (the current version is from 1619). For reasons no one entirely understands, it is dressed in a different costume almost every day; the statue has its own wardrobe of 1,000+ outfits at the City Museum. On the day we visited, he was dressed as a Belgian firefighter. The kids loved it. So did we.

★ A hidden stop
St. Nicholas Church
Tucked just behind the Bourse, a minute from the Grand Place, the Église Saint-Nicolas is the old merchants' church — one of the oldest in Brussels, its walls curved to follow the medieval market street and half-swallowed by the houses around it. Inside we found this astonishing hand-built diorama of old Brussels: hundreds of tiny figures, a market, a sailing boat, and a tightrope walker strung high above the rooftops.

CENTRAL BRUSSELS · DAY 23
The summer-only
Royal Palace.
The Royal Palace of Brussels is open to the public only between 22 July and 7 September each year. Free entry. The Mirror Room has a ceiling decorated with 1.4 million beetle wings.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Place des Palais 745 min · throne room + Mirror Room
📷 images/belgium/day-23-royal-palace.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
The Royal Palace of Brussels — the King of the Belgians' official Brussels residence. Open to the public only in summer.

The Royal Palace is technically the seat of the King of the Belgians (King Philippe), although he and his family actually live at Laeken Castle on the outskirts of the city. The palace is open to the public only when the king is away — meaning every summer, between 22 July and 7 September. The throne room, the Empire Room, the Goya Room are all on the tour. But the highlight, for us, was Jan Fabre's 2002 art installation Heaven of Delight in the Mirror Room: a ceiling and chandelier decorated with 1.4 million iridescent green beetle wings.

1.4 million beetle wings on one Belgian ceiling. — Jan Fabre, 2002. Looks like jade.

We then walked to the MIM — the Musical Instruments Museum, housed in a stunning art nouveau building (the Old England building, 1899). The MIM has 1,500 instruments. An audioguide automatically plays each instrument's sound as you approach. The kids LOVED this. We stood in front of a 1750 harpsichord and listened to a Scarlatti sonata; we stood in front of an Indonesian gamelan and heard tribal drumming. Two floors. Two hours. Lunch on the rooftop café with a view of the rooftops.

MONT DES ARTS · DAY 23
The Knight King,
cast in bronze.
At the crest of the Mont des Arts, King Albert I rides out in weathered green bronze — the monarch Belgians still call le Roi-Chevalier, the Knight King who led his army through the First World War.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Mont des ArtsEquestrian bronze · Alfred Courtens
★ Albert I · 1875–1934

Le Roi-
Chevalier

Albert I reigned from 1909 to 1934 and became a national hero for personally commanding the Belgian army through the First World War, when the small country refused to let German troops pass. He died in 1934 in a rock-climbing accident. Belgians have called him the Knight King ever since.

The equestrian statue at the Mont des Arts, sculpted by Alfred Courtens and unveiled in 1951 — his name is cast into the base of the plinth.

The Mont des Arts — the "Hill of the Arts" — is the landscaped slope that links Brussels' lower town to the royal upper town, a short walk uphill from Gare Centrale. Its geometric gardens frame one of the finest views in the city: straight down the hill to the spire of the Town Hall on the Grand Place. Around it stand the Royal Library of Belgium and a cluster of museums, which give the hill its name.

The statue itself is the work of Alfred Courtens (1889–1967), one of Belgium's foremost early-20th-century sculptors. The bronze has aged to the soft green verdigris you see today — horse and rider caught mid-stride against the sky, the King bare-headed and looking out over the capital he helped hold together.

REFLECTIONS · BELGIUM
A gilded square,
a giant iron crystal,
a million beetle wings.

Belgium in one day was a sprint. Grand Place, St. Nicholas Church, Manneken Pis, the Royal Palace, the MIM, the Atomium and Mini-Europe — all before the drive home, and we never even stopped to eat. We were exhausted. We were happy.

The Atomium and Mini-Europe combination was the kid highlight of the day — spotting the places we'd already visited, shrunk to 1:25. For the parents, it was the golden Grand Place at first sight, and the hidden diorama tucked inside St. Nicholas Church.

Favourite moment
Aviann counting the buildings at Mini-Europe — "The Eiffel Tower! Big Ben! That's the Atomium itself, here, in tiny!"
Hardest moment
Running Brussels on snacks and adrenaline — we never found a moment to sit down and eat before the long drive back.
If we come back…
Bruges. Ghent. Antwerp. Three Trappist monasteries. A Tomorrowland weekend.
