No. 11 · A Letter from the Road
One country,
one long day.

Belgium arrived as a single, headlong day. We left our cousin's house in Amsterdam a little after seven, crossed a border before the coffee had worn off, and were parked in central Brussels by half past nine — five of us, one borrowed morning, and a list that was frankly too long.

What a list, though. Brussels is the administrative capital of the European Union and, by happier tradition, the chocolate capital of the world — some 220,000 tonnes a year, more than anywhere else on earth. It is also home to a sixty-centimetre bronze boy who has been cheerfully weeing into a fountain since the Middle Ages, and to a silver molecule the height of a skyscraper. We meant to see all of it.

We nearly did. Grand-Place by ten, the Royal Palace by half eleven, the Musical Instruments Museum by half twelve, the Atomium and a whole shrunken continent by two — and back at the Amsterdam kitchen table by eight, having somehow never once sat down to eat. It was a sprint. It was one of our favourite days.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

Five of us on the Grand-Place at ten in the morning, a chocolate-shop postcard already in hand — the day barely begun.

COVER STORY · GRAND PLACE · DAY 23
A square covered
in gold.
By common consent one of the loveliest squares in Europe — a UNESCO site and a piece of pure civic theatre: a stage of gilded guild houses raised in the 1690s, after Louis XIV's artillery had flattened the medieval one.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Grand Place / Grote Markt90 min · square + Manneken Pis
The Grand Place at 10:00 — the Hôtel de Ville (left, with its 96-metre Gothic tower), the King's House (back), and the 17th-century guild houses (right) covered in gilded statuary.

The Grand-Place is a stage set, and means to be. Three sides are lined with baroque guild houses — bakers, brewers, butchers, boatmen, painters — thrown up between 1696 and 1700, each a little taller and more gilded than its neighbour, as if the trades were still competing for the eye. The fourth side is older and graver: the Hôtel de Ville, a Gothic town hall begun in the 1440s beneath a 96-metre tower. In even-numbered years a carpet of nearly a million begonias is laid across the cobbles; 2026 being odd, we had the bare stone instead — worn to a soft shine by seven centuries of markets and processions.

The capital of Europe — and the chocolate capital of the world.

A few streets away stands the city's smallest celebrity: the Manneken-Pis, a sixty-centimetre bronze of a little boy relieving himself into a basin, cast in 1619 and beloved far past all reason. He keeps a wardrobe of more than a thousand costumes and is dressed by hand throughout the year; the morning we came by, he had been turned out as a Belgian firefighter — to the children's enormous delight, and ours.

★ A hidden stop
St. Nicholas Church
Tucked behind the Bourse, a minute from the square, the Église Saint-Nicolas is the old merchants' church — among the oldest in Brussels, its walls bent to follow a vanished market lane and half-swallowed by the houses that grew up around it. Inside we found an astonishing hand-built diorama of old Brussels: hundreds of tiny figures, a harbour and sailing boat, a market in full cry, and a tightrope walker strung high above the rooftops.

NORTHERN BRUSSELS · DAY 23
A giant iron crystal,
and a tiny continent.
Out at Heysel, on the city's northern edge, two of Brussels' oddest attractions stand side by side: a 102-metre model of an atom, and a park where the whole of Europe has been shrunk to the size of a garden.
★ The Atomium · 1958

Nine spheres,
102 metres.

Built for the 1958 World's Fair and never taken down, the Atomium is a single iron crystal magnified 165 billion times — nine stainless-steel spheres slung on steel tubes, drawn up by the engineer André Waterkeyn. Six of the spheres are open; escalators and Europe's fastest 1958 lift carry you to a panorama sphere ninety-odd metres up. Standing beneath it, necks craned, the sky wheeling in the mirrored steel, we felt very small indeed.

Looking straight up into the Atomium from the forecourt café — the lower sphere and its diagonal tubes, mirror-polished and full of moving cloud.
★ Mini-Europe

350 monuments,
1:25 scale.

Next door, Mini-Europe gathers 350 landmarks from every corner of the continent, each built to one-twenty-fifth of life. The Eiffel Tower to the shoulder, Big Ben at the knee, a Vesuvius that erupts on the quarter-hour — and, best of all, the places we had already stood in for real this summer, now small enough to step over. The children were gone the moment we passed the gate.

The whole family folded into one photo beneath the miniature Eiffel Tower — a fraction of the height of the one we had climbed in Paris, and every bit as popular.

◆ The Royal Palace

The Royal Palace is the official seat of King Philippe — though the family actually lives out at Laeken, on the city's northern edge. That quirk is exactly why the public gets in at all: the doors open only while the King is away, a seven-week window each summer, and entry is free.

We walked the Throne Room, the Empire Room and the Goya Room, all crimson and gilt. But the room nobody forgets is the Hall of Mirrors, where in 2002 the artist Jan Fabre had the ceiling and a great chandelier tiled with 1.4 million jewel-green jewel-beetle wing-cases. From the floor it reads as rippling jade — an entire heaven of beetle wings above your head.

◆ The MIM · Musical Instruments Museum

Ten minutes downhill, the Musical Instruments Museum fills the Old England building — a 1899 art-nouveau department store of black wrought iron and glass, a monument in its own right.

Inside wait some 1,500 instruments, and the trick that made the children gasp: an infrared audioguide that plays each one the instant you step in front of it. A 1750 harpsichord eased into Scarlatti here; an Indonesian gamelan shivered awake there. Two floors, two hours, and lunch on the rooftop café over a grey sea of Brussels slate.

A ceiling of beetle wings, and a museum that sings as you cross it — Brussels hides its strangeness behind very grand doors.

MONT DES ARTS · DAY 23
The Knight King,
cast in bronze.
At the crest of the Mont des Arts, King Albert I rides out in weathered green bronze — the monarch Belgians still call le Roi-Chevalier, the Knight King who led his army through the First World War.
Fri 31 Jul 2026Mont des ArtsEquestrian bronze · Alfred Courtens
★ Albert I · 1875–1934

Le Roi-
Chevalier

Albert I reigned from 1909 to 1934 and became a national hero for personally commanding the Belgian army through the First World War, when the small country refused to let German troops pass. He died in 1934 in a rock-climbing accident. Belgians have called him the Knight King ever since.

The equestrian statue at the Mont des Arts, sculpted by Alfred Courtens and unveiled in 1951 — his name is cast into the base of the plinth.

The Mont des Arts — the "Hill of the Arts" — is the landscaped slope that links Brussels' lower town to the royal upper town, a short walk uphill from Gare Centrale. Its geometric gardens frame one of the finest views in the city: straight down the hill to the spire of the Town Hall on the Grand Place. Around it stand the Royal Library of Belgium and a cluster of museums, which give the hill its name.

The statue itself is the work of Alfred Courtens (1889–1967), one of Belgium's foremost early-20th-century sculptors. The bronze has aged to the soft green verdigris you see today — horse and rider caught mid-stride against the sky, the King bare-headed and looking out over the capital he helped hold together.

REFLECTIONS · BELGIUM
A gilded square,
a giant iron crystal,
a million beetle wings.

Belgium in one day was a sprint. Grand Place, St. Nicholas Church, Manneken Pis, the Royal Palace, the MIM, the Atomium and Mini-Europe — all before the drive home, and we never even stopped to eat. We were exhausted. We were happy.

The Atomium and Mini-Europe combination was the kid highlight of the day — spotting the places we'd already visited, shrunk to 1:25. For the parents, it was the golden Grand Place at first sight, and the hidden diorama tucked inside St. Nicholas Church.

Favourite moment
Aviann counting the buildings at Mini-Europe — "The Eiffel Tower! Big Ben! That's the Atomium itself, here, in tiny!"
Hardest moment
Running Brussels on snacks and adrenaline — we never found a moment to sit down and eat before the long drive back.
If we come back…
Bruges. Ghent. Antwerp. Three Trappist monasteries. A Tomorrowland weekend.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
