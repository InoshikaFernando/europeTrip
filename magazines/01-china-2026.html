A letter from the road

Who we are, why we travel

Two parents, three children, and one shared dream.

We are the Munasinghe-Fernandos: two parents and three children who love to see the world together. Avi and I share one dream, to travel to as many countries as we possibly can, as a family, while the children are still small enough to want to come with us.

It is never easy. Booking flights and trains and rooms for five is its own small adventure, and we know we cannot reach everywhere. But from every country we do get to, we try our very best to see as much as we can, and to hold on to it properly afterwards. This magazine is where we keep all of it.

China bookended the whole journey. We began in Beijing, and a month later, all five of us squeezed into that one gondola, we rode up to the Great Wall on our very last morning abroad, Avisha, Aviann and Avin grinning at the towers on the ridge above. Our children are young. Four-year-old Avin will not remember the Forbidden City, and even the older two will lose the details. So we write it all down and pin the pictures to the page, and turn a month of memory into something they can hold. Told, this time, in my words.

- Inoshi, for all five of us · 2026

Chapter One · Beijing

Rain, no raincoats, and a map that lied

Our first hour in China was a crash course in how to actually travel here.

China begins the moment you land, and it does not wait for you to catch up. We touched down at half past four in the morning, bleary and wired, three children draped over us like coats.

One small mercy in the dark: we had packed a single hand-luggage bag for exactly this stopover, so while other families queued at the belts, we walked straight past baggage claim and out into the cool, wet Beijing morning. A message to our hotel over WeChat, and a shuttle appeared to carry us in. Half past four in the morning, three sleeping children, one bag between us. The China-only carry-on earned its place before the sun was even up.

Then the first plan came undone. Our guide messaged ahead, it is raining at the Forbidden City, bring your raincoats, and ours, of course, were folded neatly inside the main luggage we had just sailed past. So our very first purchase in China was a fistful of cheap ponchos. Paying for them was its own adventure: WeChat Pay refused several of our cards, and we fell back on AliPay, grateful we had loaded different cards onto different apps before we left. Here cash is nearly extinct; your phone is your wallet, and a backup wallet is not paranoia, it is planning.

The last lesson came on the road. Google Maps promised the collection point was thirty minutes away. Ninety minutes later we were still crawling through wet Beijing traffic, because Google cannot see China's live congestion at all. It was the Didi app, checked on a hunch, that told us the truth. Rule one, learned before breakfast: in China, trust Didi, not Google. Ponchos on and phones charged, we drove on into the heart of the old empire.

To reach the Forbidden City you first cross the largest public square on earth, and on our day it lay grey and glistening under a steady summer rain. Tiananmen, the Gate of Heavenly Peace, is the ceremonial front door to the palace, Chairman Mao's portrait hung between two slogans: long live the People's Republic, and long live the unity of the world's peoples. We posed beneath it in the drizzle, selfie-stick up, five small figures under a very large idea.

The square itself is enormous and, in that weather, nearly empty. Beyond the flags stand the Monument to the People's Heroes and the National Museum, and near them marble reliefs of marching figures, the square's newer, revolutionary chapter, laid out on the same grand axis the emperors once used. We wandered it in our ponchos, five little smudges of blue on all that wet grey stone, and the children thought the whole business of the ponchos tremendously funny.

Chapter Two · The Forbidden City

A childhood dream, room by room

The palace I had only ever seen on a screen, suddenly real all around us, in the rain.

Six centuries ago the Ming emperor Yongle raised the largest palace on earth at the very centre of Beijing, and forbade the world from setting foot inside it. By the time we reached its gates the rain had eased to a grey hush, and the ponchos finally came off.

We had booked a private guide, half for the history, told well as we walked, and half for the practicalities: tickets are released only about a week ahead and sell out fast, and a guide made securing five of them effortless. We reached the meeting point four minutes early; our guide waited another five for the stragglers. Then the gates opened, and everything I had hoped for was on the other side.

I grew up on Chinese and Korean historical dramas, on palace intrigues and throne rooms and emperors in silk. To walk through the real thing, hall after hall, courtyard after courtyard, every room on our list, was a dream years in the making.

The palace is almost past imagining in size: some 980 surviving buildings and, by tradition, close to nine thousand rooms, across 72 hectares, ringed by a moat 52 metres wide and walls 10 metres tall. More than a million workers laboured for fourteen years to raise it, floating whole trunks of precious wood down rivers from the south-west and dragging marble across frozen winter roads. The emperor was the Son of Heaven, and only Heaven was allowed ten thousand rooms, so, the story goes, he stopped at 9,999. From 1420 until 1912 no commoner could pass its gates without imperial leave. What was sealed against the world for five centuries is now the Palace Museum, open at last to the very millions once barred at its gates.

I grew up watching emperors on a screen. Today I walked through their palace, one room at a time.

The weather, oddly, was on our side. Beijing in July can be punishing, but the low grey sky kept the worst of the heat off us, even if by the end we were damp with sweat. To protect the ancient timber floors we padded through the halls in blue cloth shoe-covers, which the children thought hilarious. Our one real mistake was Avin's stroller: we had left it behind, so our four-year-old walked the entire palace on his own small legs. He managed. Barely. So did we.

Every colour, number and beast in the Forbidden City means something, and our guide read them for us as we went. The glazed roof-tiles are imperial yellow, the colour of earth and the centre, reserved for the emperor alone; the walls are vermilion, for power and joy. Every great hall faces south down a single mile-long axis, so the ruler sat with his back to the north, facing his realm. The palace falls into two worlds: the outer court, where the state was governed, and behind it the private inner court, where the emperor and his family actually lived.

The palace keeps its finest work where you have to look for it: carved into the ceilings high overhead, worn smooth underfoot, hidden in the quiet courtyards where the court actually lived. A single ramp of marble between two halls is cut all over with dragons and clouds, and no foot ever touched it; the emperor was carried over the top. Look up inside a throne hall and a gilded well of brackets spirals into the roof, a coiled dragon at its heart, directly above where he sat.

A pair of bronze lions guards every important gate, and whether a lion's eyes, ears or mouth are carved open or closed is a quiet lesson: open means watch closely, pay attention; closed means look away, let it pass. The male rests a paw on a ball, the emperor's power over the world; the female on a cub, the line that must go on. The children loved them best of everything, and so did I - small lessons in bronze, set down six hundred years ago for anyone who stopped to read them.

One thing surprised us on the ground. Look closely and the palace is almost all timber: wooden post-and-beam frames carrying those sweeping tiled roofs on interlocking brackets, with barely a true arch or dome anywhere. Where Europe built up in stone for permanence and prized its ruins, China built out in wood, low single-storey halls ranked along one north-south axis, valuing renewal and repair over eternal monuments. Buildings meant to be rebuilt, not left as romantic ruins. We would carry that thought all the way across Europe, and keep measuring one against the other.

The Kids Said...
Three little photographers

Each of the three had packed a little camera of their own, and in the Forbidden City they used them, framing the halls and the moat and each other with enormous seriousness. Avisha and Aviann framed the halls with great care; Avin, four, mostly pointed his camera at his own shoe-covers and the backs of people's knees. Somewhere on three little memory cards is the whole Forbidden City, shot at knee height, slightly blurred, and absolutely theirs.

The adventure came on the way home. We ordered a Didi back to the hotel, but Beijing has a dozen hotels with almost the same name, and the one we tapped was the wrong one. Our driver, who spoke no English, delivered us confidently to the wrong door. What could have been a crisis became the kindest moment of the day: he simply phoned our real hotel, got the address, drove us there himself, and refused a single yuan extra. Not every wrong turn ends badly. Some end in a stranger's quiet kindness.

The day had begun, hours earlier, at the hotel's breakfast buffet, a cheerful spread where fried rice and steamed buns sat beside waffles, fried eggs and little sausages. The children built plates that made no culinary sense whatsoever and were entirely delighted with them. Later, back at the hotel and finally dry, we did the most rewarding thing a traveller can do: we walked out with no plan and let the neighbourhood decide.

A few streets away we found a small restaurant, sat down, and ate one of the best lunches of the whole stopover: a tiny Cantonese place, no menu we could read, everything pointed at from the pictures on the wall. Hand-pulled beef noodle soup, fried rice, dish after dish, fresh and generous and so cheap we checked the bill twice. Then a hot bath, and a grateful nap, because the day was far from over.

Our flight out left at ten to three in the morning, and the hotel shuttle ran only until half past nine at night, so we set an alarm, peeled ourselves off the beds, and rode out into the dark. It was at the airport that the day turned tender. Avin said he felt cold, so I pulled a jumper over him. Only after security did a health officer approach us gently: the thermal scanners had flagged that our littlest was running a minor fever. Poor Avin, four years old, halfway around the world, quietly running a temperature. We held him closer as we boarded. There was nothing to fear in the end. He was simply exhausted; we let him sleep the whole flight, kept the water coming, and by Germany the fever had lifted and our littlest was himself again.

That flight carried us out into a whole summer of the world. We would not set foot in China again until the very last morning of the trip, when we flew back into Beijing at dawn and, at long last, climbed the Great Wall.

  • Size 72 hectares, some 980 buildings, close to 9,999 rooms by tradition.
  • Protected A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987; now the Palace Museum.

Chapter Three · The Great Wall · Mutianyu

Stone, soldiers and smoke signals

On the last morning of a month abroad, one restored mile of the longest wall on earth - in brilliant blue, and a merciless heat.

A photograph tells you the Great Wall is long. What it cannot tell you is that the Wall is steep: that it does not lie across the mountains of Huairou so much as climb them, hand over fist, in staircases that tip you forward and set every calf burning.

We came to it sideways. Ours was not a pilgrimage but a homecoming, the last morning of a month away, flown into Beijing before dawn on the red-eye and driven straight north rather than left to wilt in the airport. Mutianyu is among the best-restored stretches of the whole two-thousand-year system, rebuilt in hard brick under the Ming, and it wears its history lightly: arrow-slits still frame the valley, smoke-signal platforms still crown the towers, and the little vaulted rooms where soldiers once slept still hold the cool of the stone.

We left the city at breakfast and drove an hour north, the tower blocks thinning into orchards and then into the steep green hills of Huairou. A private driver, not a tour bus: it cost about the same for five of us, let us leave when we liked, and meant someone was minding the stroller and the bags at the bottom while we climbed. Off a red-eye, with a flight to catch that same night, that mattered more than we knew.

At the foot of Mutianyu we traded our legs for a cable. All five of us packed into one swaying gondola, the doors clunking shut, the mountain tilting up ahead, and nobody quite believing yet what was waiting at the top. The cable ran clean across the whole valley, carrying us up in a slow, steady hum, the gentlest possible way to meet the eighth wonder of the world. Beneath our feet the treetops fell away into an unbroken carpet of forest, and the car gave a little rock each time it passed a pylon, the children shrieking happily on cue.

And there it was. The gondola bumped into its station, the safety bar lifted, the doors slid open, and we stepped straight out of the machinery of the twenty-first century onto six centuries of hand-laid Ming brick. The Wall ran off in both directions along the spine of the hills, tower giving way to tower until the stone dissolved into blue distance. We stood at Tower 14 and, for a long moment, simply looked - the kind of looking you do when a thing you have only ever seen in photographs turns out to be entirely, solidly real.

Watchtower to watchtower, the Wall rode the very ridgeline into the haze, climbing where the land climbed, pouring down where it fell, refusing every easy line the mountain offered. You could follow it with your eye until your eye gave out, and still it ran on, out there into provinces we would never see. Standing over that drop, the arithmetic finally lands: human hands carried these bricks up this mountain, and up the next hill, and the one after that, for two thousand years. A wall you could walk for months and never finish.

By eleven it read 39 degrees. The grey brick threw the heat straight back up at us, not a metre of shade on the whole rampart. Of thirty days and fourteen countries it would rival only our furnace afternoon in the ruins of Pompeii, weeks later - a comparison Avisha made entirely himself, wilting against a parapet and pronouncing the place, with weary eleven-year-old authority, "mini Pompeii." The name stuck for the rest of the climb.

We did not go far - a few hundred metres, two watchtowers, and back, with a four-year-old riding high on his father's shoulders past the crenellations. But you do not have to walk the whole Great Wall to be undone by it; you have only to stand on it once. In that heat, that was ambition enough. Lay a palm on a wall six hundred years old, look out through an archer's slit at that same ridgeline, and for a moment the distance between a Ming sentry's morning and ours simply closes.

There was a quiet weight to standing there that I had not expected. Twenty years earlier my own father had made his own journey through China; now, two decades on, his three grandchildren stood here on the oldest stone of all - the same country, the same wonder, one generation between. I thought about that a great deal on the way down.

At the bottom the gondola set us down onto a souvenir street roofed with a bright canopy of painted parasols. And that was the Wall: the final wonder of the whole trip, ticked quietly off on the very last morning. We rode the ropeway down, drove back to the airport, boarded the night flight home, and found that the Wall had somehow followed us there, running on behind our eyes long after Beijing had gone dark below the wing.

Field Notes · A Mutianyu Day with Kids
  • Take a private driver over a group tour - about the same for five, and you keep your own plan. Ask for Mutianyu, not Badaling.
  • Buy tickets at the entrance: admission, shuttle bus and cable car together. Under 1.2 metres rides free.
  • Take the enclosed cable car both ways to Tower 14 - steady, quick, and the least-tiring way to stand on the Wall off a red-eye.
  • Leave the stroller in the car. It is all steep stone steps up top; carry the little one for the short climb.

  • Ancient First walls raised from around 220 BC; the brick we climbed is Ming work.
  • Vast Some 21,000 km across all dynasties - a border, not a town's last wall.
  • Wonder A UNESCO site since 1987, and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

Reflections

Two stopovers, one generation

We had been told China would be a stopover, a bracket around the real trip. That framing turned out to be wrong. Beijing held the trip's biggest history lesson, and the Great Wall its biggest moment of I-cannot-believe-this-is-real. What we will carry home: the drum of rain on cheap ponchos in the Forbidden City, the cable car swaying up to the Wall on our last morning, and the driver who took us to the wrong hotel, then the right one, and refused a single yuan for the trouble.

My father first travelled to China in 2006. Twenty years later, in 2026, I walked the same country with my own three children. Two visits, one family, a generation between them, and the same wonder passed quietly down the line. I think that is the thing I will keep longest of all - not the palace or the Wall themselves, but the sense of standing exactly where he once stood, with the next three standing beside me.

Worth Remembering
  • Our favourite moment - walking into the Forbidden City at last, the palace I had only ever seen in the historical dramas I grew up on, suddenly real all around us, in the rain.
  • The hardest - the fever scare at the airport on the way out, our littlest flagged by the thermal scanner, and the long night flight spent willing his temperature back down.
  • If we come back - a whole week. Xi'an's terracotta army, and the Great Wall with no plane to catch at the end of it.

One tip we would give anyone following us: come in spring or autumn. Summer is the popular season, but Beijing in July was punishingly hot and shoulder-to-shoulder crowded, and the milder months are far kinder on little legs. Whatever the season, pack for rain - the weather turns without warning, as our blue ponchos will always remind us. And whatever China is called, a stopover is not it. It was the first great adventure of the whole summer, and it gave the trip its biggest history lesson before Europe had even begun.
