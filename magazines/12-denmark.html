No. 12 · A Letter from the Road
Welcome to
hygge.

Copenhagen was a one-night stop on the way to the sister visit in Gothenburg. We arrived on a Sunday evening and left on a Monday afternoon. In between we packed in the things you cannot miss: Nyhavn, the Little Mermaid, the Strøget shopping street, Tivoli Gardens, and as much smørrebrød as we could manage.

The city is small. 660,000 people. 380 km of bicycle lanes. The bike rate is 62% (compared to 38% in Amsterdam). Copenhagen has been voted the world's happiest city more times than any other city, and you can feel why within ten minutes: it is clean, it is calm, the architecture is colourful but never garish, and people smile at you on the bus.

This issue is a short one. We were in Copenhagen for 22 hours. We made them count.

— The Munasinghe-Fernandos, 2026

COVER STORY · NYHAVN · DAY 25
A canal of
painted houses.
Nyhavn — "New Harbour" — was built in the 1670s by Danish sailors and pirates. Today its colourful 17th-century townhouses, packed restaurants, and old wooden boats are the most photographed view in Denmark.
Sun 2 Aug 2026Nyhavn, Inner Harbour90 min · canal walk + dinner
📷 images/denmark/day-25-nyhavn-sunset.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
Nyhavn at 19:00 — the painted 17th-century townhouses turn gold in the low Danish sun. Hans Christian Andersen lived at no. 67.

Nyhavn was a working harbour for three centuries. Sailors arrived here from across the Baltic. Hans Christian Andersen lived in three of these houses (numbers 18, 20, and 67) at different points in his life. The canal was dug by Swedish prisoners of war. By the 1960s the area was so rough that the cafés closed by 8pm. In the 1980s it was restored, repainted, pedestrianised. Today it is the postcard Copenhagen — and yes, it is touristy, and yes, the restaurants are expensive, but the colour is unbelievable, and the wooden boats moored along the canal are 100+ years old.

More bikes than cars, more parks than shopping malls, and the colour-coded waterfront of Nyhavn.

We ate dinner at a canal-side table — smørrebrød open sandwiches, a beer for me, a sparkling elderflower for Lana, sodas for the kids — and watched the sky go from pale blue to peach to deep gold. Tour boats passed slowly. Cyclists chimed their bells. A jazz duo played at the end of the dock. The kids ran along the cobblestones and threw imaginary pebbles into the water. It was, in a word, hygge.

Where the merchants' harbour learned contentment
The name says everything: København, the merchants' harbour — a Viking-age landing where the herring were fat and the sound between the islands funnelled every ship bound for the Baltic. Trade and fortification made it a capital, seat of one of the oldest continuous monarchies on earth, its royal line stretching back more than a thousand years. But Copenhagen's modern genius has been to turn hard northern practicality into something gentler: a city built for bicycles rather than cars, for clean design and long winters softened by candlelight and the untranslatable comfort the Danes call hygge.
It is also a city that traffics in wonder. Hans Christian Andersen wrote his fairy tales here, and his Little Mermaid still watches the harbour from her rock. Tivoli Gardens opened in 1843 — an early pleasure garden of lanterns and rides that a visiting Walt Disney is said to have loved, carrying its enchantment home. Few capitals wear their happiness so lightly, or so deliberately.

STRØGET + LITTLE MERMAID · DAY 26
The walking street
+ the smallest landmark.
Strøget is one of Europe's longest pedestrian shopping streets. The Little Mermaid is a 1.25-metre-tall bronze statue on a rock, easily one of the world's least impressive famous landmarks. We loved both.
Mon 3 Aug 2026Strøget → Langelinie3 hrs · walk + statue + train station
📷 images/denmark/day-26-stroget-morning.jpg
HERO PHOTO · TBD
Strøget at 09:30 — 1.1km of pedestrian street, lined with cafés and Scandinavian design shops. The morning light slants down between the buildings.

The Little Mermaid statue sits on a rock in the harbour. She is 1.25 metres tall. She has been beheaded twice. She has been blown up once. She has been painted red, painted pink, dressed in a hijab, dressed in a niqab, draped with sex-toys (twice), labelled "racist fish" by activists. Every time, Copenhagen restores her. Every time, tourists come back. She has sat there since 1913. Sculpted by Edvard Eriksen. Inspired by the Hans Christian Andersen fairy tale and modelled (allegedly) on Eriksen's wife.

The world's most photographed disappointment. — and we mean that with affection.

Strøget, by contrast, was a delight. 1.1 kilometres of pedestrian shopping street. Royal Copenhagen porcelain. Bang & Olufsen. Lego flagship store. The kids took twenty minutes inside Lego to build a small Copenhagen-themed model. Then we ate cinnamon buns and walked back via the city hall square. By noon we were at the central station, boarding the X2000 train south to Sweden, via the Øresund Bridge.

REFLECTIONS · DENMARK
Painted houses,
cinnamon buns,
a small bronze mermaid.

Copenhagen was the calmest stop of the trip. Even the bicycles seem calmer. The colours of Nyhavn at sunset will stay with us. The smørrebrød lunches. The hygge of the city — that untranslatable Danish word for cosy contentment.

We will come back, probably in winter, to walk Strøget when the Christmas markets are open and the streets are lined with lanterns. Tivoli at Christmas is meant to be one of the best things in Europe. We saw it in summer; the kids want to see it lit up.

Favourite moment
Nyhavn at golden hour. The painted houses, the boats, the jazz duo at the end of the dock.
Hardest moment
The Little Mermaid was disappointingly small. The kids laughed at her.
If we come back…
Christmas in Tivoli. Skagen in summer. Aarhus.
— Avinesh, Lana, Avisha, Aviann & Avin Munasinghe-Fernando
